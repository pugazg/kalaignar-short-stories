# Neruppu

“Those called friends are companions who share in happiness and wipe away sorrow in times of suffering! But some of the friends fate has given me grew very close to me—and now they put me over hot coals and torment me, they ‘roast’ me. They magnify ordinary problems—turning a mustard seed into a mountain... Ah! I cannot bear the torture they inflict on me. I cannot hate them and cast them aside. The reason is that I have grown so attached to them and so full of affection. Yet at times my friends themselves try to shatter that very foundation of attachment and affection! Sometimes they even stand in the way of my stream of thought and watch my pain with utter indifference. Forgetting the mistakes they themselves make, forgetting to turn back and look at themselves even once, they trample my heart. While I dream that they will remain forever as imperishable sculptures in the temple of my heart, if those very sculptures burst and turn into thorns... then where is peace for me?... I know for certain that I have done no wrong! They too know that I have yielded to them to a great extent! For their sake I

have faced how many kinds of ‘enmity’—that too is not unknown to them! Yet they make me weep; I do not know why... What a harsh punishment for me!”

Arul, having finished reading the storybook ‘Friend’, kept reading only the lines above again and again as his train journey continued, seated in third class. He was travelling on the train from Chennai toward Mayavaram... through deep darkness....

Even when he bought the book ‘Friend’ in Chennai... Bhoopathi had stood before his mind’s eye, smiling carelessly. Wiping his troubled eyes, Arul bought the book, boarded the train, sat down and began reading. Even after finishing it, he went back over many passages again and again, trying to commit them to memory.

“They make me weep; I do not know why; what a harsh punishment for me?”

Every time he read that sentence, his body thrilled. The hairs on his skin stood on end. Calming his shivering body, he let his thoughts run.

Ten or twelve years earlier, Arul and Bhoopathi had been fellow students at Chidambaram College. Vallam Padugai, near Thillai, was Arul’s native village. Kollidam, next to Vallam Padugai, was Bhoopathi’s native village.

Bhoopathi—true to his name, the pampered son of a wealthy household. Arul—the embodiment of affection, a model of friendship.

<!-- source scan 13; printed page 12 -->

Their college association made Arul and Bhoopathi friends dearer than life. If people saw Arul alone, it became customary to ask, “Where is Bhoopathi?” If they saw Bhoopathi, they would ask, “Arul is well, isn’t he?” Their friendship had grown to that extent. Arul was extremely careful that no one should say he clung to Bhoopathi merely to increase his own comforts and opportunities. Even when his father decided to sell the last few acres of land left to them because there was no money for Arul’s studies, Arul did not tell Bhoopathi. From the money obtained by selling that land, he even lent Bhoopathi one hundred rupees for an urgent need—and when Bhoopathi later returned it, Arul took it back. Bhoopathi did not like Arul’s habit of always thinking about something or other... or studying the moral lives of great men such as Buddha, Gandhi and Socrates. Yet somehow an affection bound them together as friends. If Arul’s favourite colour was the blue of the sky, Bhoopathi’s would be the yellow of a lemon. If Arul read a book called “The Great Souls Who Reformed the World,” Bhoopathi would be immersed in a volume called “Cleo, the Great Beauty of the World.” Old wine is served in a new pot, is it not, with mythological tales polished afresh and a golden-coloured cover illustration painted for them? Bhoopathi would sink into that intoxication. Chandra and Tara, Indra and Ahalya, Krishna and Radha—books about such pairs filled his library. In the embrace of Chandra, who in a moment cooled the bodily heat and inner throbbing of Tara burning with desire, he would lie lost to himself—

<!-- source scan 14; printed page 13 -->

—and Bhoopathi would delight in praising, “How on earth has this great genius described the way he lay like that!” At that very moment Arul might have his mind fixed on a research book asking whether human beings lived on the moon.... Only an expert in erotic literature, Bhoopathi would say, could possess the skill to note that Ahalya remained silent even after learning that it was Indra. When she was receiving a rare pleasure she had never known before, it was only natural that Ahalya would not suddenly rebuke him with, “Indra? You low fellow!... This is wrong!”... Such would be Bhoopathi’s criticism. He had grown tired of hearing his friend object to such criticism again and again. Yet differences like these did not make their friendship wane like the moon... rather, it grew....

Because nature’s summons was drawing near for his father, Arul left college with his studies half-finished and returned to the village. He was thrown into the necessity of somehow fulfilling his father’s last wish. His father longed to see his son in wedding finery before he died.

Some village elders told Arul that there was a girl in Kollidam, that she was very beautiful, but that her mother was “a certain sort.” Arul had read often enough of a red lotus in the mud, a pearl in an oyster, a rose among thorns; he did not develop any dislike for the girl. Instead, he felt sympathy.

Gomathiyammal of Kollidam, who worried that her daughter was being punished with unmarried life for the mistakes of her own life—would she refuse to give her daughter Prabha to him as a wife?... The marriage was fixed.

<!-- source scan 15; printed page 14 -->

Bhoopathi himself was astonished that such a queen of beauty had lived all this time in his own village without ever coming before his eyes. On the wedding day the two friends embraced each other and shed tears of joy. One must say that the marriage now raised a great storm that no difference between them had ever raised before. Bhoopathi was happy that his friend had married! But... he had not expected that Arul would receive a celestial beauty, like a statue perfectly cast from beauty itself... or that through her a cyclone would rise in his own heart. A friend’s wife was like a younger sister to him!... He tried again and again to seize and hold on to that thought. But it kept slipping, slipping away from that plundering heart.

“Ayyo, how pure Arul is! He is a friend who deals with me without stain.... And it is his wife we are thinking about like this...” Bhoopathi’s conscience gnawed at him!... Then mythology entered between them! Chandra made Tara, his guru’s wife, his own wife... and it was he who received the highest place of all—on Shiva’s head!

Sugriva joined with his elder brother’s wife and took pleasure in her.... Yet he was the dearest friend of Rama of Ayodhya!...

Bhoopathi did not dare. Nor did his desire subside. After blessing the newlyweds, he left for Kollidam.

A few months later, Arul’s father finally closed his eyes. Bhoopathi came to offer condolences and, even after the memorial rites for the dead were over, did not return to his own village. In order to console his friend,

<!-- source scan 16; printed page 15 -->

he stayed on there. Arul was pure-hearted. He did not sense the fire burning in Bhoopathi’s heart. Prabha alone had begun to sense it. The flame sprouting in his chest had come out through Bhoopathi’s eyes and burned her many times....

“Why does he keep staying here?.... He is the son of a rich family.... How many days can he put up with the simple food in our house? Why don’t you send him back to his village?” she tried to hint to her husband. Her reward was only Arul’s anger.

Bhoopathi’s inner struggle kept tormenting him. He knew very well that what he was doing was wrong, and yet he suffered because he could not stop himself from doing it. At times he came to the conclusion that nature itself had given him this peculiar itch of mind and that the path he chose to relieve it was therefore right. When he did not see Prabha, the madness did not ripen so much. When he went with his friend Arul to the riverbank and sat on the sand mound talking over old stories, he would tell himself, “No, no; one must not betray a friend!” But once he returned home and saw Prabha’s beauty as she came to serve food, all those resolutions made on the sand mound turned to dust. His schoolmate, a man full of affection and attachment, was sitting nearby; yet Bhoopathi would forget even the horror of letting base thoughts wander about that friend’s beloved wife. She too was that beautiful. As she served food, his eyes would circle around her without his knowing it. And to make her lift her face and look upward, he would often

<!-- source scan 17; printed page 16 -->

drink water. Since sipping would not make his gaze go upward, he would lift the tumbler high and drink like a man obsessed with cleanliness. Arul, unaware of that low motive, would say innocently, “Just put your lips to it and drink, Bhoopathi!” Bhoopathi would feast his eyes on her. Arul, meanwhile, would look to see what was missing from Bhoopathi’s leaf and repeatedly call Prabha to serve whatever was not there.

What he was doing was wrong! Bhoopathi knew it clearly! He even regretted it. Had not the Trimurti desired that Anasuya come and serve them completely naked?.... Perhaps he had decided that what he was doing was not as great a wrong as that!....

Bhoopathi also understood that Arul was suffering for want of money. If he offered financial help, Arul would not accept it. Bhoopathi knew that nature of his too. From every place to which Arul had applied for work came the unanimous reply: “No.” Afraid that Arul might begin to suspect anything, Bhoopathi left for his village, carrying the sorrow that what he had wished for had not happened.

Through Bhoopathi’s estate manager, Gomathiyammal learned that Arul and Prabha were struggling even for food. She came to her son-in-law’s house. Saying that her daughter could stay in Kollidam until her son-in-law found a job, she took Prabha away with her. Unable to bear separation from Arul, Prabha cried through an entire night. Arul explained his situation and promised, “I will go somewhere outside, try for work, succeed, and come back to take you with me. Do not worry!”

<!-- source scan 18; printed page 17 -->

Accordingly, Arul set out in search of work.... Not for one day or two; he wandered for many months seeking employment.

Hearing that Prabha had come to Kollidam, Bhoopathi often visited her house and inquired with great concern about how Arul was faring. Prabha’s mother grew increasingly respectful and affectionate toward Bhoopathi. She wondered that the son of an important household took such concern in her family. Having heard of the friendship between Arul and Bhoopathi, Gomathi came to trust him completely. Whenever she was short of money, Gomathiyammal borrowed small sums—ten, five—from him.

One day...... it was the Margazhi festival in Chidambaram. Though Gomathiyammal had slipped and fallen in life, she had deep involvement in devotional observances; she set out for Chidambaram to see the Margazhi festival. Since Prabha refused to come, she left her alone and departed.

Learning that Gomathi had gone to Chidambaram, Bhoopathi descended on Prabha. He began by asking, “Has any letter come from Arul?” Then suddenly he bolted the door, seized her hand and pulled her toward him.

Prabha was afraid to cry out. If she shouted and the village gathered, she trembled at the thought of making an enemy of a powerful man. So she looked at Bhoopathi humbly and pleaded, “Do not betray your friend!” Bhoopathi stood there like fire.

There came the sound of someone knocking at the door. Terror rose in Bhoopathi. Wanting somehow to escape,

<!-- source scan 19; printed page 18 -->

he opened the back-yard door and ran. It was Arul who had knocked at the street door!... Arul noticed someone running through the back. He ran after him and saw that it was Bhoopathi. The street door opened. On seeing Arul, Prabha rushed forward, crying “Aththan!” and embraced him. “Leave me! I am not Bhoopathi!” Arul flung her away and walked rapidly off somewhere.

Arul, who had been looking into the dark sky with his thoughts caught in a whirl of memory, realized that the train he was travelling on was nearing Killai station. Next came Chidambaram, then Vallam Padugai, and after that Kollidam!... He was going to see the wife from whom he had separated seven or eight years earlier. The “great man” who, deciding wrongly that she had become Bhoopathi’s mistress, had quietly run away to Malaya to earn a living without showing anger either toward his friend or toward her, was returning after seven or eight years to meet his wife! A strange sigh of pleasure rose in him.... With that, Arul opened his box. He took out a long letter and read it. On that journey, this was the seventeenth time he had read it.

“Beloved Aththan! Greetings from your Prabha. After thinking and thinking many times, I have finally made this decision to write this letter. Your friend has become a traitor to you. I too have become a traitor. That day you suspected me, though I was without blemish, and because circumstances played a trick you laid a stain upon me and left. About eight years have rolled by. Should you not know what schemes overtook Prabha’s life during this interval, and who was responsible for them?

<!-- source scan 20; printed page 19 -->

I was born in the womb of a mother stained by disgrace. When my mother’s wrongdoing fell upon my head and no one came forward to tie the wedding thread around my neck, you came—like a god! I believed that my life would smell of honey. I did not expect a forest of fire to surround me. You suspected me and, without clearly investigating anything, left. We suffered without even knowing where you had gone.

For two years my mother and I waited in longing!... Your dearest friend Bhoopathi too did not tell us where you were. You suspected me—but what actually happened that day? Let me tell you from the beginning.

When Bhoopathi came to the village at the time of Uncle’s death, he stayed in our house without even going to Chidambaram College, didn’t he? It was for me!.... Even then I hinted to you about him—you flared up at me. When Mother went to the Thillai festival, he came to ruin me. You arrived at the right moment; he ran out through the back. But you imagined that event in another way.

For two years I searched for you.... and he began to seek me out. You know about Mother. A ruined woman!.... In this condition consumption took hold of her. The great responsibility of saving Mother fell upon me. Mother began giving advice for the welfare of the family. Though there was no purity in that advice, I had to follow it if Mother was to live.

‘Yield to Bhoopathi, my dear!’ Mother begged, holding my chin, so that there might be money for her illness

<!-- source scan 21; printed page 20 -->

to be cured! What more is there for me to write, Aththan!

You once showed me a letter you had written to Bhoopathi. In it you had wished both of us well.... When that letter reached him, I still belonged to you. Only two years later did I become his vellatti—his kept woman! I became for him an object of lust with no time, season, rest or respite. As a result, money piled up. Mother’s consumption diminished. And now! That consumption has caught hold of me!.... I earned and gave so that Mother’s consumption might be cured—now that it is I who have it, there is no one in this house to save me. The very next day after Bhoopathi heard that I had consumption, he left me.

In Chennai he married the daughter of a judge and lives in peace. As for me, I live on—full life, perpetual peril!

Aththan, I read the letters of good wishes that you wrote week after week to the friend who betrayed you. From them I learned your address in Malaya. What a great and noble man you are. What a great sin I have committed against you. I do not ask you now to accept me again as your wife. I will soon disappear—from this earth! If only I can see your face once before that, that alone will be atonement for the wrongs I have done!

Only if I can bathe your feet with my tears and then close my eyes will I have peace!..... Will you come?

With love,
“Prabha.”

He folded the letter and put it into the box. His eyes became waterfalls. Looking up at the sky, he cried aloud to himself, “I have forgiven you, Prabha!” The sound mingled in the air with the sound of the train.

The train stopped at Chidambaram station. Arul leaned out through the window. Who was that?..... Bhoopathi! Yes, it was he—leaning out from a first-class carriage. Arul forgot himself. Crying “Bhoopathi!” he ran and climbed into first class. Bhoopathi understood nothing; by then the train had started again.

Looking at the silent Bhoopathi..... Arul pointed to a woman beside him and asked, “Who is this?”

“This is my wife!”

“Oh...is that so?!.. Hmm... Is my wife keeping well, Bhoopathi?”

Bhoopathi started. His face went pale! Bhoopathi’s wife too stared awake, understanding nothing!...

“Why are you staring, man? I hear my wife is unwell—I am going to see her. Isn’t she your kept woman now?... She must have become a juiceless husk, I suppose!”

<!-- source scan 23; printed page 22 -->

“Hey, Arul, enough—stop! What are you raving about?”

“You wretch! You separated her from me! And I let it happen. I gained nothing from you in the name of friendship. I myself gave even my honour as a gift to friendship! How precious was the thing I gave! Could you not at least preserve it, sinner!.... If a silver jug is crushed, people melt it down and make a new jug. But you—you threw the jug itself into the rubbish and bought another one..... Such is the arrogance your money gives you!.....”

Bhoopathi sensed the rage choking Arul’s speech and trembled.

“You ruined Prabha—neither letting her live with me nor keeping her alive with you..... Only until now were you my friend—from now on you are my enemy! Enemy!” Screaming, Arul pulled out a knife tucked into his waist and stabbed Bhoopathi again and again in the chest, stomach and face....

Bhoopathi crumpled down, breathless!.... By then Bhoopathi’s wife had pulled the emergency chain. The train stopped—after crossing the Kollidam bridge!.... The moment it stopped, Arul jumped down and ran, directionless in the darkness!....

There... a light.... He ran toward it.... In a cremation ground a corpse was burning. In the firelight he saw that his shirt was covered in bloodstains. Afraid that if he ran into the village like that they would identify the murderer, he stripped off the shirt and threw it onto the fire. The shirt too served to feed the fire consuming the corpse.

<!-- source scan 24; printed page 23 -->

From there he ran toward Prabha’s house. Gomathiyammal sat at the entrance, looking up at the sky!.... A lamp burned with a faint flicker.

“Auntie; where is Prabha?...” he asked. Gomathiyammal wailed aloud..,

“Thambi... Prabha went away this morning.... It was only at ten tonight that they set fire to my darling’s body,” she said, beating her belly and mouth in grief!....

Then—was the fire onto which Arul had thrown his bloodstained shirt the very fire that was consuming Prabha?...

Both the fires that had consumed her had gone out ....

Only Arul kept being destroyed.
