“Director sir! Don’t worry about money—you can put up whatever sets you like. Do you want America’s buildings to rise inside the studio? Must the very streets of Moscow be built? Shall we show a sea? Shall we summon the sky itself? Rain? Storm? Earthquake? Whatever you want, whatever it costs, the film must be a great film. And by a great film I don’t mean forty thousand feet long or something like CinemaScope, sir! It must run for a year in every town! Anyone who does not talk about this film should count as someone not yet born! Box-office collection is not the only aim! A magnificent story! Rare dialogue! Wonderful direction! Effortless acting! Enormous crowds! The whole country must be in an uproar like this. I am not making this film to earn money. I am going to spend the whole profit from it on building four or five colleges. I have already arranged the writer you like. In writing with a progressive outlook, he has no equal but himself. I have set aside half a crore of rupees for it. Selection of actors and actresses, shooting—the work must begin at once.”

Film proprietor Paramasivanandam was a man of progressive thought. He had a strong desire to release films with good ideas in a way that would reform the people—

<!-- source scan 195; printed page 186 -->

—nor was he someone forced to make films merely to earn money and live; nor was he miserly enough to heap up money beyond measure just to admire it. He possessed generosity, and at the same time a principle that ideas needed by the country should be propagated through the arts. The owner of many lakhs of rupees, he put up fifty lakhs—a large part of his wealth—as capital to make an incomparable great film capable of shaking the whole world. He built a studio of his own so the film could be made conveniently. A very famous director, a dialogue writer influential with the public, and an innovative poet were contracted. The film proprietor opened his good heart to them; they too prepared an intellectual story that would astonish the world, together with beautiful songs.

Shooting began. Every famous star without exception acted in the film. Throughout the country there was talk of nothing but the new picture. People waited longingly: “When will it be released?” The fifty lakhs melted away, and as the shooting expenses approached a crore the film proprietor borrowed money in some places and hastened the film’s growth. The film took shape at a level no one had released before, in a way that captured the heart! Revolutionary ideas that no one dared to state were explained by the picture in a civilized manner without obscenity. Ways for the advancement of labourers—a society without caste distinctions—the deceitful tricks carried out in the name of God—aashada-bhoothithanams performed in the name of art—an explanation of how religion ruins human society—all these were contained in that film like a jewel! The acting—ah, the heartbeat of the heart! The story—the light of life! The dialogue—a living epic! The songs—honey syrup—

—rock candy! The direction could not even be praised enough! Such excellence—such excellence!

From the time the film grew by thousands and thousands of feet until it reached ten thousand feet, the film proprietor would watch it every day and savour it! The joy of being about to give the country a great film it could praise possessed him. Friends would come; he would show them the film; they would rejoice and praise it.

Only a few asked doubtfully, “It is certainly an excellent film—but will it pass the censor?” The film proprietor would say there was nothing to worry about. “What outrageous thing have we said in the film now? Is there sex appeal? Or obscene dialogue? What is there that can be objected to? None of the obscenities found in puranic films are here! Have we made a film like Tara-Sasankam? No—why should the censor interfere?” Paramasivanandam asked angrily. Like his friends, the people of the country too worried about the censor. The people’s verdict was that the censor was an institution full of partiality. So they expected that some danger might come to Paramasivanandam’s revolutionary film. Even before censorship, advertisements for the picture appeared.

**A medicine for religionists! A feast for heralds of renaissance!**

**The world is deceived in the name of God! No master-and-slave order is wanted in the world!**

With bold-letter slogans like these, advertisements for the revolutionary picture flashed in the papers.

At last the film went to the censor. News came that the censor had cut only two places, and that no scene in the picture had been harmed—

<!-- source scan 197; printed page 188 -->

—and the news spread throughout the country. “Ah! The film has escaped alive,” the people sighed in relief.

An advertisement announced that the film would be released on the appointed date. Ah! I have come this far without even telling you the name of the film—**“Sevvaanam”** was the name of that revolutionary picture.

The film was released on the same day in four hundred theatres. The crowds were so dense that one wondered whether the theatres would collapse. No film had ever created such excitement!

Paramasivanandam had come to the theatre where it was being screened in Thilaganagaram. Seeing him, the people welcomed him eagerly and rejoiced.

The film began.

At the sight of the title “Sevvaanam” there was thunderous applause—enough noise to wound the hearts of a few jealous souls. The name under “Story, Dialogue” appeared; then came the actors’ and director’s names. Finally the words **“Production: Paramasivanandam”** received a great ovation. The people waited impatiently to see what the first scene would be. The screen remained completely white! It stayed that way for a few seconds. Then the following words rolled into view:

**“The film is over—greetings.”**

The people were stunned. The theatre bell was rung. The curtains were drawn. There was a revolution inside the theatre! The people surrounded Paramasivanandam.

“What is this outrage?” they shouted.

“It is all the censor’s work,” he said.

“But didn’t they say, sir, that the censor had cut only two places?”

“Yes, comrades! The censor cut in only two places!

“The first place: they cut immediately after the titles ended.

“The second place: they cut at the frame just before ‘greetings’! So it was only two places!” the film proprietor replied.

The flood of people cast an angry look in the direction of the “Censor Office”!
