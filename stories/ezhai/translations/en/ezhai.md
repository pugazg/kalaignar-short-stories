As usual, Banu’s push-cart set out toward the ‘park’. Parvathi pushed the cart along, answering the baby words that kept scattering from Banu’s tiny mouth. Banu was not even six. At home she never stayed quiet; she was always ‘thuru-thuru’, darting about. Most of all, she would not let father and mother talk—just imagine! From morning onward Nallakkannu Pillai had to attend to village accounts, payments from tenants, kisan troubles and business problems. Only after six in the evening did he get any rest. Pillai’s aim was that not even a second of that leisure should be wasted. Karunambal—yes, his wife—waited for six o’clock too, and that lady desired the same pleasure of leisure. Their sweet time would be cut in the middle again and again by that endlessly playful child, breaking its continuity. However strong her parental affection, Karunambal’s heart wanted at least a little while without anyone’s disturbance. And what of it—were that couple poor, to ask what colour pleasure was? It is the kings and queens of huts who, in their kingdom of poverty, cannot set aside even a few minutes for pleasure—

They cannot. An invasion by creditors; a siege by the curd-seller; after managing all that, in the few hours that night mercifully grants, once the veena of desire has been played, time goes in lamenting and crying to God, and perhaps whatever remains may sometimes be used. Besides, the poor empire has many subjects. There is no shortage of children arriving one after another. The seventh child may be only six years younger than the first. Have those pitiable creatures been granted Karunambal’s kind of comfort—treating money as dust and changing the kaappa bag!

Young, his youth unworn—handsome, his beauty undiminished—such was the husband Parvathi had got. He would return home by five after finishing his mill work. Yet she had no release from work in order to welcome and attend to him. For the rest required by master and mistress—the poor young wife Parvathi restrained the feelings of her own youth and took Banu to the ‘park’. Was it only for one day? No. Every day. She was no woman; she was like a cow in this world, doing housework all day until her bones seemed to break. Was not the master’s mansion itself her home? Even the sun’s work ends in the evening, but Parvathi’s...? She could not even bear to think of it. That untimely creator made a heart—why did he also make a stomach?

Who....asked whom?

She was showing Banu the swaying flower plants and the beauty of the clusters, enjoying them herself, when she noticed the sky growing dark. At the end of the month when Parvathi asked for her wages, she had often seen an even deeper darkness on the master’s face. Being used to it, she paid no attention to the change in the sky and walked toward home. All the waves of her mind that day were striking against...aththaan...nose-stud...a contest of kisses... A sweet—

—dream must have been there; otherwise a smile would not have placed its tilak on her dry lips. Only because the dream was delicious could her eyes have been shining like that. Perhaps it was a dream of aththaan giving her a kiss—or the memory of the first day—even her cheeks suddenly flushed red.

Nothing more: aththaan was bringing her a nose-stud. Money saved for many days was taking the form of a nose-stud. Throwing the broomstick far away, he himself placed the ornament on Parvathi’s nose. The white nose-stud flashing on her dark complexion called to mind the moon rising in a blue sky. “Parvathi...where?” aththaan said, shaping his lips into a bud. Parvathi’s lips too gathered. The buds struck and blossomed. ‘The drizzle on Sanjeevi Parvatham’—an important part was enacted.

The dream sang its mangalam. Parvathi regained ordinary awareness. In the cart Banu began singing, “Rain is coming, rain is coming.” Two heavy raindrops fell on Parvathi’s face and burst apart.

Thunder that made the world tremble—the dreadful flash of lightning, the monstrous laughter of the man in the sky—rain as though the sea had overturned—the shock of the Himalaya rolling down and colliding with the Vindhyas.

Parvathi feared that Banu would get drenched. Poor woman—that poor servant had such affection for the master’s house—no, no, for the child! Humanity is the crowd of the poor, is it not? Rich humans are a herd of animals. Even among them, humans are sometimes born by mistake. Among the group of poor humans too, there are those who, unable to bear suffering, grow into beasts. That growth had not yet seized Parvathi. Nallakkannu Pillai would call all this Russian talk. Russia tasted to him like eating poison.

Parvathi was thoroughly drenched. The child was terrified and began to cry. Parvathi stopped beneath a tree—

Even in all that hardship and terror, the coming arrival of that happiness continued to give her face a special brightness.

Suddenly a large branch of the tree broke and fell. The rain stopped; the storm subsided.

Nallakkannu Pillai’s motor car flew rapidly along the road.

At the foot of the fallen tree....Pillai got down calling, ‘Banu!’ Banu lay unconscious. He lifted the child and revived her. “Appa,” the child said as her eyes opened. He got into the car with the child. The car drove away—toward the doctor’s house!

Another figure lying beneath the tree opened its eyes. “Aththaan, ayyo...” A long sigh. The eyes closed. Who was it? **“The poor woman”!**
