The whole town was in an uproar—police lorries were flying from corner to corner! Ripping apart the stillness of night, the shouts of the red-capped policemen rushed through the town—through every street—through every house. Not a single house had its door shut. Nor could the sound of snoring be heard. Seeing the talk and agitation of their mothers and fathers, even tiny children stayed awake to watch the commotion. At the doorway of one house an old woman sat pounding betel...without realizing it she had fallen asleep...poor, aged eyelids! The “boom boom” of a car startled her awake, and she began pounding the betel pestle hard against the floor. Her grandson clapped “kadak-kadak” and enjoyed the sight.

The policemen who got out of the car entered the house and began searching without even asking the old woman anything. They looked at the loft. They inspected the adukkalai. They examined the cracks of the doors. At last, pursing their lips, they came out. The only thing left was for the old woman’s grandson to collapse crying, “Ayyo, poochandi!” Searches like this went on in every house. The whole town was turned upside down! The reason: Ramadurai, owner of Leela Mill, had at seven that evening—

died!......No.....no; he had been murdered! The murderer was a mill worker; his name was Vittal.

That very night the police were racing like a storm in lorries, cars and bicycles, trying to catch the murderer and lock him in a cage. Who was Ramadurai? Was he some poor man who rose in the morning, called once upon “God,” cursed his fate, and went out to earn his stomach’s food? When a poor man is killed, it is like an ant being crushed! Can death come to a rich man like Ramadurai? When God had granted him the means to live, wealth, and inexhaustible pleasure, should He not also have increased his lifespan to a thousand years? Such was the waxing concern of the people!

“Ayyo, what a pity! Ramadurai was not even forty! Such a good man! A philanthropist praised by the whole country! Wasn’t the Navaratri festival at Nageswaran temple sponsored by him? Wasn’t that holy man the one who built the thousand-pillared hall at the Akilandeswari temple? And some sinful fellow has stabbed such a charitable benefactor to death!”

“What did he ever do—did he torment his workers? Was there ever a strike in his mill? Could anyone even say he dismissed a single worker? For nothing at all—just because he refused to lend this fellow two hundred rupees for some loss of his—should he commit such a great sin? Sinner! Will he ever live well?”

“That Kubera’s wife—like Mahalakshmi herself; and two little children. What anguish must they be in? Even after penance through seven times seven births one could not get such a husband—how must she be writhing after losing him? You wretched sinner, you fivefold criminal—if someone stabbed you dead like this, would your own wife’s belly not burn? Hm...do you think they are going to let you live either?

“They should not merely hang that chandalan! They should strip the flesh from him piece by piece and kill him slowly!”

Thus people talked in many parts of the town. The accepted reason for the murder was this: Ramadurai had refused to give Vittal two hundred rupees. So Vittal killed him.

Leela Mill—a large factory situated in a pleasant part of the city. About three thousand workers laboured there. Its work was to turn cotton into thread and thread into new kinds of cloth. A short distance outside the city there was a place called Leelapuram, set aside for the workers to live in. There all three thousand pushed along the little boat of life. Vittal was one among those three thousand.

That evening at five, as usual, the factory conch sounded. The labouring crowd that had been shut inside streamed out rank after rank, breathing deeply as they went home. Vittal too came out with them, but stood beneath a tree opposite the mill, staring without blinking at the route taken by the women. It was the gate through which nearly a thousand women came out. Every woman had left. Only she was nowhere to be seen. Yes—his wife! Even his legs seemed to lose their strength.

Thinking she might have gone ahead, he walked quickly from there. He ran home—the house was locked. He turned back again. ‘This morning, when she left for the mill, she said her head was aching—has she perhaps developed a fever?’ he wondered. Vittal could not bear even an ant biting his wife. Such was his love! What other joy does a poor man have besides that? Vittal hurried back toward the mill, half running, half walking.

When the factory conch sounded, Thangam too had set out overflowing with eagerness to see her aththaan. All day long her task in the mill was to process cotton. Her dark complexion and the distinctive radiance that belonged to her face alone had driven away Vittal’s terrible worries

so many times. After labouring from morning until her hands and legs ached, the very ‘darshan’ of him was her medicine. In the same way she too would feel soothed when she saw him. When work ended, she would not even have time to brush off the cotton clinging to her hair. In haste she would set out to see her aththaan.

That day too Thangam was coming out in the same way. The peon Singaram ran up behind her and said, “The proprietor is calling you.” Not understanding why, Thangam was startled.

She entered the proprietor’s room and stood modestly to one side. Ramadurai took a cigarette, lit it and asked, “Thangam, have you finished all your work?” Thangam nodded, saying “Mm.” “They say you work very skilfully; the manager told me.” She gave no reply. Only a little smile. Ramadurai watched that smile closely. “I have ordered that your wages be increased from this month.”

Still Thangam did not speak; only dimples appeared in her cheeks. She longed to tell this happy news to her aththaan. Seeing Singaram standing there grinning, Ramadurai snapped, “Hey, get out......bring coffee.” He ran off.

Ramadurai tapped the ash from his cigarette. Even before any ash had formed he tapped it again and again. He took another cigarette. The match went out. Tch! What a wind. He shut the window shutters and struck another match. But the cigarette still did not catch. “Damn, what a wind! Saturn! Thangam, shut that door,” he said, and even as he spoke Ramadurai’s voice trembled like that of an old man shivering in the cold. Thangam understood her employer.

“I am leaving,” she said, taking one step. Ramadurai could bear it no longer. He sprang upon Thangam. The doors closed.

She screamed, “Ayyo!” Intoxicated words fell from Ramadurai’s mouth. “Green young beauty! Living golden picture! Yield to my desire—golden dove!” She trembled. “Golden beetle that walks—why do you tremble?” Ramadurai embraced Thangam. She struggled and cried out loudly. Falling at her employer’s feet, she pleaded, “Save my chastity.” “Lamenting on the battlefield—and wailing in the pleasure-field—are the acts of cowards, delicate beauty!” he preached.

Thangam could not free herself from that rhinoceros-like beast’s grip. Thangam’s soft lips—the lips Vittal alone had tasted—were pressed against the lips of the intoxicated Ramadurai. She fainted. The beast satisfied his hunger to the extent of his desire. Thangam collapsed in exhaustion.

The clock struck six. The room door opened. Ramadurai pressed a hundred-rupee note into Thangam’s hand. She tore it into shreds, flung them away and ran.

Vittal had come very close to the mill. The moment he saw Thangam he was filled with joy. He ran toward her. ......But Thangam was like a worm scorched by embers.

“Aththaan, do not touch me. I am echchir pandam”—she wept.

“What are you saying, Thangam?”

“The proprietor......my chastity....” She did not finish. Nor could she...sobbing, she fell at Vittal’s feet. He did not even look at her. He charged toward the mill.

The Gurkha standing at the entrance looked at Vittal in astonishment. Vittal looked at his waist. A gleaming dagger. In a single leap he snatched it and ran toward the proprietor’s room. That was all—before the taste Ramadurai had sampled from Thangam could change, Ramadurai was a corpse.

With the dagger tucked at his waist, Vittal ran outside, but the gatekeeper—the Gurkha—blocked him. The Gurkha’s knife plunged into Vittal’s side. Vittal drove the dagger into the Gurkha’s neck, lifted and flung him aside, then ran toward Thangam clutching his side, trying to stem the blood gushing out ‘gubu gubu’ through the hole in his ribs.

Thangam lay curled on the ground. Lifting her with one arm, he carried her to a dry canal beside the mill and hid there.

People scattered from the mill. The “telephones” screamed.

Thangam tore her sari and wrapped his wound. Vittal could not stand. He staggered. All his blood was flowing out through the hole in his side. “Aththaan, kill me too,” Thangam sobbed; “Uss...” he said, gently patting her. His eyelids grew wet. They embraced each other. The great and terrible event that had come upon the two of them drove them about like mad people.

“If we walk along this canal, we can reach the next town’s railway station. Somehow let us try to escape,” Vittal said in a trembling voice. Thangam shed tears.

They walked...walked...kept on walking....While the police spread their net everywhere searching for the murderer, they too kept walking.

Here—the police were throwing the town into turmoil. They boasted that they had searched everywhere except the sky. There was no place inside the town they had not looked. When nothing came of it, they decided the murderer might escape on the train departing at nine, and lorries sped to every railway station within ten miles. One lorry raced toward Kamalapuram station.

Yes! It was toward that very station that Thangam and Vittal were moving. Only ten minutes remained for the train to arrive! They too had almost reached it. But Vittal could not take even one more step. The pain in his side had grown severe. He began crying, “Ayyo, ayyo.” Thangam’s heart beat ‘padak padak,’ as though its pounding struck her own ears. Where was the Thangam who, after the mill work ended, sank into bliss and slept beside her aththaan?...And where was this misfortune-filled Thangam now, breathing the breath of death?...

“Aththaan!” she said tenderly, lifting his arms and placing them over her shoulders, gripping them as she held her breath and began to walk.

A reckless courage arose in her that somehow she could get him to the station. If only they caught that train?......Then, wherever they went, somehow they could escape—that single desire alone drove them forward. Vittal breathed with difficulty. Thangam walked on with fierce determination..........

Suddenly her face brightened. The red light of the railway station had come into view. Only a few more minutes; they had to climb out of the canal; that was all......In a little while the train would arrive; they could escape.

Thangam embraced Vittal and said, “Aththaan!” Tears and laughter mingled, deepening the sorrow. At the bank—

she climbed up and, exerting all the strength she had, lifted her beloved aththaan and brought him onto the bank. “There is the station!” she said, holding him tightly. In that embrace even the pain in his side seemed to vanish. “Thangam,” he said—in a low register overflowing with tenderness. “Ayyo!” screamed Thangam!

Policemen were rapidly getting down from the lorry. Torchlights swept in many directions. Thangam’s thoughts spun. Her head reeled: shackles on Vittal’s hands—then Vittal on the gallows—Thangam in a widow’s garb. That horror shook her violently. “Aththaan! We have been caught!” she cried. Vittal rolled his eyes and looked. Her cheeks were wet. Thangam gathered all her desire into her lips at once and kissed Vittal. The kiss ended.

They saw the police force running toward them shouting, “Don’t let them go! Catch them!” Thangam took the dagger from Vittal’s waist. Now she knew a way to save themselves from the police. One powerful thrust into the chest of her beloved aththaan—he became a corpse! Then that blood-dripping dagger plunged into the hollow of her own throat! She saved Vittal from the police. She saved herself from suffering.

Both of them had escaped from the cruelty of society.
