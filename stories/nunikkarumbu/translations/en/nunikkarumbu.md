# Nunikkarumbu

<!-- anthology scan: 253; printed page: 244 -->

‘Anklets caress the feet that tread in rhythm, seeming to float in the sky;
above her lotus hand, an unwithered tender champak bud;
the finger that makes its gesture is, to those who behold it, a stem of red coral.’

When she gave beauty to this poem of Bharathidasan through her art of dance, the entire auditorium itself danced with delight. Who in that hall could keep from wondering whether a lotus vine, bearing a blossoming face and two buds, had gained life and pictorial form and come there to perform a joyous dance?

“Her body is refined gold—her beauty a casket made of gems—filled to the brim with radiant pearls—and the art of dance is her wealth,” declared Arulnambi, the secretary of the hall, in his speech of praise. The building shook with applause. Amudha was a queen of dance, filled with beauty worthy of her name and with youth overflowing through every limb. There was no stage on which she had not danced—no heart that had not run after her! That day was her *naaruvathu* dance performance. Arulnambi had made a great effort to have it held in his own auditorium, and had succeeded. He was the secretary of the institution. He had learned to compose poetry. He proclaimed as his creed that service to Mother Art was the goal of life. He had crossed roughly fifty years and possessed the experience and appearance that made one think of sixty.

Under his administration, that auditorium had hosted countless artistic programmes. Yet what eagerness he had shown to stage Amudha’s performance there! What agitation! What he wished had happened. He entered the world of literature, pleaded with poets, turned over epics, and at last prepared a speech of praise rich in Tamil. He memorized it for two days, day and night, muttered it even in his dreams, and after all that, described Amudha from the stage that day and won the appreciation of the assembly.

Was the assembly’s appreciation all he desired?

It would be enough if Amudha’s sidelong eyes moved toward him just once; for that alone he had performed such prolonged penance. Arulnambi was an influential figure in the world of art. There was hardly a Tamil stage on which he did not appear. At public celebrations his mark would be found somewhere. At feasts and entertainments he was always the forerunner. In this way he was skilled at attracting attention somehow and fastening himself in people’s memory. It was customary for many to forgive even some of his disagreeable actions, saying, “Leave it—poor old man.” But as far as he was concerned, there was no greater abuse than calling him an old man. He would bear anything except that word. If anyone denied that he still possessed the power to enjoy the pleasures of the world with the feelings of youth, his heart would be wounded. With that feeling he continued his ways.

For such a man, surely there was nothing wrong—at least in his own view—in waiting for Amudha’s loving glance?

After delivering his speech of praise, he went near her to place a jasmine garland around her neck. Before he could reach her, Amudha herself came toward where he stood.

Would she take the garland directly around her neck? Arulnambi was a little confused. If she accepted it around her neck, it might mean she had done so only on the ground that “he is an old man, after all; it doesn’t matter.” He could not bear that. Before he could think through all this and reach a conclusion, Amudha took hold of the garland in his hand with her beautiful hands and received it. Ah! At that moment he felt the softness of those hands. And the thought that, while taking the garland, her fingers had separated from his only slowly made him giddy.

The curtain of the auditorium fell.

Amudha went into the dressing room. He went along with her. They brought her almond milk to relieve the exhaustion of dancing. She gave it instead to Arulnambi. As she handed him the bowl, the gentle touch of that flower-like hand drew him for a second time toward the city of pleasure. “Amudha! I’ll phone you tomorrow!” said Arulnambi as he took leave.

“Do make sure you phone,” Amudha said, bowing as she sent him off.

Thinking again and again of what that cuckoo-like voice might mean by “Do make sure you phone,” Arulnambi went home in delight.

Cursing the night and trying to drive it away as he waited for dawn, Arulnambi telephoned Amudha’s house at eight the next morning. The maid answered. “Madam is sleeping; she asked you to phone at ten,” she said. He hurriedly finished his morning obligations and sat as though whipping the hands of the clock to make them run.

Ten o’clock arrived! The telephone at Amudha’s house rang for a long time. No one answered. After quite a while, a rough voice said, “Hello.”

“Who is this—is it Amudha’s house? Is Amudha there?”

“No! No! Wrong number!”

“Sorry.”

Without even listening to the apology, the man slammed down the phone. Arulnambi felt somehow embarrassed. He studied the number carefully and phoned again.

“Hello?”

Ah! That was Amudha’s voice. Arulnambi swallowed. He cleared his throat.

“It’s me—Arulnambi speaking!”

“It’s Amudha. Greetings...”

“What, are you well?”

“When you are there, what shortage could there be in my well-being?”

Amudha’s laughter reached his ear. At once his whole face broke into a sudden sweat.

“Hmm? What else?”

“Nothing!...What is this—am I never to have a feast at your house?”

“Oh...freely! When are you coming? Whenever you come, there will be a feast.”

“What feast?”

“Every kind of feast. What do you like—meat or vegetarian?”

“Vegetarian? Tsk, tsk! Man is a slave to flesh, after all!”

“All right, I’ll have meat cooked separately for you. We are all vegetarian!”

“No...no...one meal for you and another for me? Let mine be vegetarian too. When shall I come?”

“Why don’t you come this very afternoon?”

“Would a daytime feast have any relish? Shall I come at eight or nine tonight?”

“Oh! Come! You must definitely come. Don’t fail to come! You must come for certain!”

Arulnambi remained seated for a while in a daze, pressing the receiver against his cheek. When he came back to himself, he wiped the sweat running over his body and, crying aloud, “Father Muruga! It is all your grace!” took up a *Thiruppugazh* book and began turning the pages.

Half past eight at night. Amudha welcomed him with a young smile and repeated, “Come, come.” Arulnambi sat in the hall of her house with his whole body burning like embers.

“What, I don’t see anyone at home?”

“They’ve all gone to the cinema for the six-thirty show! They’ll be back soon.”

“It’s already half past eight. They’ll be back now?”

“It’ll take another hour. Will you eat now itself?”

“Only me? What about you?”

“Just the two of us! Wait—here I come!”

As Amudha walked toward the kitchen, Arulnambi watched her from behind, entranced by the way she moved. As though that were not enough, the cinema too had shown scenes of that sort and spoiled people’s hearts

—hadn’t it? Half an hour passed before Amudha returned. Until then, how many different dances of Amudha took place inside Arulnambi’s heart!

“Come; let us eat,” she said. At those richly coloured words, he sprang up and followed her. An ornate table held an extravagant feast. The two sat opposite each other.

The maid served them both.

Two or three times in between, Arulnambi’s foot happened to touch Amudha’s foot opposite him. “Siva! Siva!” he would say. “It’s all right,” she would reply with a smile and continue eating. He could hardly eat. Electricity seemed to be running through his whole body. Somehow they finished the meal and returned to the hall. She brought all the albums of her dance performances and gave them to him. Their hands brushed as she handed them over and he received them. Arulnambi could no longer sit still. It was already half past nine. Pointing toward a room, he began trying to say something to her. Just then came the sound of a car stopping at the entrance.

“Oh! The people who went to the cinema have come back,” she said, rising and walking away. Her upper garment caught on the chair and fell. She picked it up, adjusted it, showered him with one more smile, and went toward the street entrance. The people who had gone to the cinema returned. Amudha came toward Arulnambi holding a child by the hand.

“Do you know this boy? My son!” she introduced him.

Arulnambi already knew that Amudha had a child, so he asked eagerly, “Oh! Our little fellow?” Amudha did not pay much attention to that. Looking at the child, she said:

“Hey, hey! Kanna! Come on—say vanakkam to Grandpa!”

She brought his hands together in the gesture of greeting.

The boy stubbornly went off in another direction. She followed, pulled him back and returned to the hall.

The hall was empty!

Amudha kissed the child with a victorious smile.
