He disliked employers who tormented workers, gave no value to their labour, and roamed about in intoxicated arrogance. Nor did he like workers who, without any sense of duty, were driven only by agitations for rights and caused trouble.

Aadhavan was just such an upright worker. At the famous “Avittam Studio,” Aadhavan was only a light-boy! He was one among the many workers who operated the strange lights of the film world, which could create daylight in darkness and darkness in daylight.

Sitting on the loft above the studio shooting floor, he had watched events in countless forts and battlements, in the inner-

<!-- source scan 51; printed page 46 -->

quarters of beautiful palaces. He had heard the anguish of huts. From a termite mound to a work of art, things had gained beauty under the light he cast, entered the camera, and captivated the eyes of viewers.

One day, in a palace corridor, a king's daughter and the prince who had married her would sing a song of pleasure with their bodies brushing against each other. “Nectar moon! Dancing painting!” the lover would say, caressing his beloved's face with his hand. For the task of making the heroine's face resemble the moon, the light operated by that light-boy was absolutely essential.

The next day there would be no palace corridor there. In its place would stand the ashram of sages who had renounced everything. A rishi's wife would appear in half-open clothing. In place of little words of sensual pleasure—“Beloved! Delight!”—would overflow grand words of bliss: “Swami! Lord of my life!”

The next day, where the ashram had stood, a thief's cave would appear. The day after that, the same place would become a police station.

Thus, even if forts became huts and palace corridors turned into cremation grounds, as far as the studio loft where Aadhavan sat and worked was concerned, nothing ever changed. The same loft! The same light!

The same work! Hour after hour, day after day—the same work commands, heard until they had turned sour!

“Hey, move that koda!”

“Soft-en Baby a little!”

“Shift fifteen to the left!”

“Cut the big light!”

“Don't put Junior!”

“One nedway in that Senior!”

“Make it strong on the actor's face, pa!”

“Ready! All lights!”

He was one of those responsible for carrying out these routine orders from the cameraman. He did not fail to notice workers who had grown weary of doing the same job and complained of boredom. Nor did he fail to feel sorry when he saw friends sitting on the loft and behaving lazily, without paying attention to their work. And he did feel happy when he saw many fellow workers like himself performing their duties conscientiously. From that high loft he had cast light upon and clearly seen all kinds of faces. With the lamp in his hand he had beautified all kinds of people. The power to turn so many captivating faces

<!-- source scan 53; printed page 48 -->

into terrifying images belonged to the light under his control. There were no film stars he had not seen, no directors or story writers he had not watched. He had encountered many kinds of cameramen.

One would switch on all the lights at once and finish the matter in a second with, “Ready, sir!” Another would fuss over the character of every single lamp, coaxing them, strangling the life out of some, and tormenting both the eyes of the lights and the eyes of the actors until they hurt. Once in six hours he would say, “All right! Ready!”—and even then mutter that some hairline flaw remained. Once the actors were ready for the relevant part of the story, a lamp would be switched on according to the cameraman's opinion. Until the next order came, Aadhavan would sit silently on the loft with countless thoughts.

“This Avittam Studio owner is making a film like this in this age! At a time when the light of rationalism has spread across the world, would anyone make a film out of an old, moss-covered rag of a story called *Bhajanamirtha Gaanam*?” he would ask himself. He would think of workers like himself, earning fifty or a hundred in wages, arriving at work at the appointed time and leaving at the appointed time, while

<!-- source scan 54; printed page 49 -->

some actors earning fifty or sixty thousand, if they were supposed to arrive for a seven-o'clock shoot, would arrive two hundred or three hundred minutes late like the trains of the Indian government. He would sigh. Considering their talent and art—and the fact that the producer earned money by using that talent and art—even their wages did not seem excessive to him. But when he thought of how they earned more than enough money and yet apparently lived without even buying a clock or calendar for their houses, he was astonished. He even felt sympathy for film producers.

If a major actor had still not reached the set, the director might decide to film a scene involving a smaller actor. When the cameraman began telling them what lights to put on that actor, Aadhavan would have no heart to cast the light upon him. He would feel instead like directing that beam at the face of the film producer sitting broken-hearted, lips twisted in disappointment, and making that face beautiful.

Every day before shooting began, he watched the ritual of breaking a coconut, placing flowers and applying kumkum to cameras and other equipment, and performing worship. Seeing that

<!-- source scan 55; printed page 50 -->

he would laugh inwardly. He would mutter that some foreign “fool” had invented these scientific devices, and our country's “wise man” was now decorating them with flowers and worshipping them.

More than five years had passed since that young man with such modern ideas had come to Avittam Studio. When he first came there, he had not come seeking work as a light-boy. He had come with the desire to act. When he and his elderly mother could no longer survive in their village, many people had told them: “The boy is fair and handsome. If he goes to the city, he'll get work acting in films!”

Believing them, the old woman set out for the city with her son. After arriving they wandered endlessly, went hungry, and curled up on roadsides. The eyes of no film producer fell upon that young would-be actor. Somehow or other, after suffering and doing penance at studio gates, Aadhavan finally got a job as a light-boy. After that, his desire to become an actor disappeared. The reason was that he saw, again and again with his own eyes, the hardships endured by crowds of supporting actors inside the studio. Swearing upon his mother, he firmly decided that he wanted no acting job at all.

He had come to the city at eighteen. He made hard work his chief aim, caring not merely about washing away his own poverty but struggling every day in the world for his beloved mother's comfortable life. He was now over twenty-three. Aadhavan's concern that, even if he himself had to go hungry, he must attend to the cry of his mother's stomach was not something that could be said to belong to every son.

Even if he received a banana free of charge, he would bring it home and tell her, “Amma! Today I got a whole hand of bananas. I ate all of them and brought you just one. Here, eat it!” and give it to his mother.

“Why did you bring even that, kanna? You eat that too!” his mother would pour out her love, and tears would flow between them. This was an everyday event in that house. Without her son knowing, the old woman had tightened her stomach and restrained her mouth, saved nearly two hundred rupees, tied the money into a bundle, and hidden it inside a stacked pot.

One day the mother called her son and said in a trembling voice, revealing her unquenched desire: “Thambi! You're grown now. I've reached the stage when I too must soon go. While my eyes can still see, my heart longs to watch you tie a thaali around a woman's neck with your own hands.”

Aadhavan lowered his head shyly and turned it aside.

What child would not feel embarrassed at the mention of marriage? Does everyone immediately sing a war-song—“Aha! Marriage? Here I am, ready!”? First they put on airs and say, “I don't want any of that!” Then they pretend to be angry: “Mind your own business.” After that, if the family does not bring up marriage at all for a month, they fret inwardly: “What is this? They gave me a taste of it and now, without saying another word, they're minding their own business......”

There was no one around Aadhavan before whom he could keep up his shyness for long, put on airs, twist himself away, and run off. No maternal uncle, brother-in-law, elder brother, younger brother—no one. There was only a single patch of giving shade: the loving shade of his mother. And even that was the shade of a tree that might wither away at any moment. One day of shyness, the next of sadness, the following one of thought—the marriage talk his mother had started grew in this way.

These days, whether Aadhavan sat on the studio loft or lay flat in his hut counting the holes in the roof, no other thought burrowed into him. The strange shape taken by the marriage talk his mother had created touched and stroked his youthful heart and laid him upon a soft bed of pleasure, admiring him there.

So many dreams...... His memory slid rapidly toward the past....

As he recalled the time he had left his village for the city, Anandhi, the girl from the neighbouring house, appeared before his mind's eye. She had been eleven then. When they studied at the village school, Aadhavan and Anandhi were an inseparable pair. Whether studying or playing, they stayed together. Yet they were not children of related households. The two families might have tried to twist together some distant kinship and tie a knot around it, but otherwise there was no bond between them. The people of the street disliked Aadhavan and Anandhi studying, playing, and moving about together. Anandhi was not the daughter of a well-to-do family; she too was a little girl from a poor household. Jealous hearts could not tolerate those young minds running and playing, bound by affection without any stain.

Trouble began. One day Anandhi and Aadhavan were flying a kite. Anandhi's father entered the scene. He struck her four times on the back and dragged her hurriedly into the house. The glare she gave Aadhavan as she was taken away left him completely shaken.

After that he did not try to see her. At school

<!-- source scan 59; printed page 54 -->

the two would meet, but they would not speak. Before their eyes could attempt to speak, tears would come and put up a barrier.

A year or two passed like this. Suddenly Anandhi stopped coming to school altogether. Aadhavan burned to know why. He overheard people in the street saying, “She has come of age. So her parents have stopped her schooling!” On hearing the news, a fullness he could not understand arose in his heart. He did not know why. The vine that had been only a bud had now flowered, he heard. How had a new kind of pleasurable thought arisen in him now, one that had never arisen when she played with him, studied with him, or flew kites with him? He himself could not understand. His schoolmate—his play companion—had now entered the age when she could become his life companion. Yes; she had begun to bloom.

New light appeared in the eyes of Aadhavan, who had been sunk in old memories inside his hut. That light could surpass the thousand lights he operated at the studio. A desire arose to bring the vine blooming in the village to the city and let it spread inside his hut. He told his mother what was in his heart. She too agreed happily. He ran toward the village. That little town appeared to him like a desert. It was as though, in a sudden cyclone, all the houses had been destroyed and someone was telling him

<!-- source scan 60; printed page 55 -->

so. But the village was exactly as before. Nothing had been destroyed. Every house stood where it had stood earlier. Yet everything appeared ruined to him. The reason: Anandhi, whom he had gone searching for, was not there. The news reached him that her family had left the village a year earlier. What had happened to them? Where had they gone? He knew nothing.

He returned to the city as though every pulse in him had gone still. His mother devoted herself to calming her son's mind. Putting marriage arrangements aside, that poor mother took great pains first to console him.

That day Avittam Studio glittered with a new splendour. The reason was that the studio owner was conducting a puja for a new film.

They had planned to film a story called *Malarnthathu*, written by a famous film-world writer, and had arranged an inaugural function for it. Newspapers advertised that a new actor and a new actress were being introduced in the film.

They had also planned to shoot one of the important scenes of *Malarnthathu* on the first day. As scheduled, the pujas ended, and everyone moved toward the place where the film was to be

shot and gathered there. Aadhavan too sat in his usual place, attending to his work while letting his thoughts graze in the village.

The new actor entered the floor. He looked impressive and appeared to be a man with a good future ahead of him.

Another light-boy sitting near Aadhavan said excitedly, “Anne! Today it isn't only a new actor; a new actress is coming too!”

Before he had even closed his mouth, the actress entered the floor. Her name was Asha!

The moment he saw her, darkness fell across Aadhavan's eyes. His body trembled violently. The woman who had come was a new actress to that studio—but to him she was the old Anandhi. He felt as though his breath would stop. For an instant a doubt cried out inside him: “Could she merely be someone with the same appearance as my Anandhi?”

By then the hero had begun to speak. Yes—a love dialogue!

“My life! My precious beloved! Our love, which many doubted could ever be fulfilled, has been fulfilled! Today a new life has blossomed for us!”

When he finished the line and completed the rehearsal, the director prepared to take the shot.

Above, Aadhavan's fellow worker told him: “Anne! This actress Asha—they say she was somewhere in a village. This new actor married her and brought her here to act as well. Asha, it seems! Asha! Her beautiful name used to be Anandhi, they say; our producer changed it!” No sooner had he said this than Aadhavan's scream made everyone there shudder.

The next instant...........

Following a cry of “Aiyo!”, Aadhavan fell from the forty-foot-high loft.

The actor and actress, who had been preparing themselves for the love scene, screamed and ran. Those who had been concentrating on their duties did not ask what had happened; the flood of blood spreading in competition with the red-earth-painted floor of the set announced by itself what had occurred.

As that broken body twisted for the last time, his crushed lips murmured, “Anan....dhi.” The voice, unheard by anyone else, dissolved into the air.

Asha too went near and looked at him. Poor thing, she could not recognise him. Only if his head had remained whole could she have known who he was.

Saying that an ill omen had occurred on the very opening day, the Avittam Studio owner stopped the film *Malarnthathu*.

The next day.........

The lover of Asha, the new actress of the film world, was reading the newspaper. “Asha! There's a report in the paper about the light-boy who died yesterday. Did you read it?” he asked.

“No! I only looked at the advertisement for the new film we're going to act in!” said Asha.

“His name was Aadhavan, it seems!”

“Whoever he was, what does it matter? One of our films has already met its sunset! That wretch..... couldn't he find some other place to die? He's gone and ruined our very first contract!”

Before Asha's condemning voice had died away, some sound was heard at the entrance. The two rose and went to see. Aadhavan was making his final journey. Behind him rose the sorrowful lament of the woman who had given birth to him.

Yes. That poor old woman was spending the money she had saved for his marriage to perform Aadhavan's final rites.
