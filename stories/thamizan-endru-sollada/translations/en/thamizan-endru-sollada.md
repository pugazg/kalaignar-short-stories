Sugi Subramaniyam was one who gave his heart to the Tamil movement. He was one who forever refused to give up the self-respect of the Tamil. Such a tireless worker! At the Tiruchirappalli radio station and later at the Chennai radio station, he lived and worked as an actor, a playwright, a gifted writer, and as one capable of developing many actors and writers.

His entire family is a family of writers. His wife, his daughter, his sons, and his daughter-in-law—all of them are writers.

In that family’s collected volume, the very title given to the first article is ‘Say You Are a Tamil.’ (We keep saying, “Say you are a Tamil; stand with your head held high!” Whether he actually stands that way or not.) Without adding “stand with your head held high,” the title given to that article

seems to have ended simply with ‘Say You Are a Tamil.’

There is a short story in it. The time of the white man. In that period there was a judge named Stone, and there was a clerk. His name was Kumaragurupar. Because the white judge had to carry out his duties in Tamil Nadu, he wished to learn Tamil. At that time it became necessary for him. So he learned Tamil. He accepted Kumaragurupar, who was a clerk, as his Tamil teacher. Whenever the clerk went to the judge’s house to teach Tamil, the judge would stand up, welcome him, attend to him, place a chair for him as an equal, and behave toward that clerk as a student learning with all the humility that a student ought to show.

Kumaragurupar’s heart rejoiced: if a judge behaved with such humility toward a clerk, was that not an honour received by Tamil? He was delighted. One day, a petti collector saw this scene of the lesson being taught. He was a Tamil. His name was Sambasivam.

He was thoroughly irritated. The judge was listening to the lesson with such humility, while this clerk too sat as an equal in a chair and taught him. That Tamil man became jealous. The white man had bowed to Tamil. For the Tamil man,

jealousy. Alas, he is sitting there submitting to a Tamil—that was his jealousy. So that petti collector, Sambasivam, met the judge privately and said, “This is not good. He is merely an ordinary, humble clerk. You are an honourable and distinguished judge. It is not fitting for you to learn with such humility before him. It is improper.”

The judge listened to this. The next day Kumaragurupar comes to the house to teach. The judge sits in a chair and tells Kumaragurupar to stand and teach the lesson. Kumaragurupar asks, “Where is the chair?” “There is no chair. You are an ordinary clerk. I am a judge. I will sit and listen to the lesson. You stand and teach,” he said. At once Kumaragurupar says, “I may be a clerk. But I am a teacher who has learned Tamil, a Tamil man. I cannot teach without sitting down,” and walks out.

After he leaves, the judge’s wife asks what happened. He tells her. “This is what the petti collector said. Listening to him, I refused to give him a chair. So the Tamil teacher—the clerk—became angry and left,” he said. At once his wife says, “This is not an honour received by the clerk. With the courage to let whatever may happen to him happen, because he gives honour to his mother tongue, Tamil,
