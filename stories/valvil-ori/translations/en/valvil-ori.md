# Valvil Ori

> **English translation of the verified Tamil archival reading.** Controlling source: 1987 collection `கலைஞர் சொன்ன குட்டிக் கதைகள்`, lower scan **39 / printed page 38 → scan 40 / printed page 39 → upper scan 41 / printed page 40**.

<!-- anthology scan: 39; printed page: 38; source span: lower -->

The generous ruler Ori of Kollimalai was called “Valvil Ori.” Such was his mastery of the bow.

<!-- anthology scan: 40; printed page: 39 -->

What kind of mastery did he possess? If he fitted an arrow to the string and shot it from his bow, that arrow would pierce an elephant; after piercing the elephant, it would emerge from the elephant's body and strike a tiger; after bringing down the tiger, it would emerge from its body and strike a deer. Then it would emerge from the deer's body and kill the boar below. Emerging from the boar's body, it would kill a monitor lizard running along the ground.

<!-- source illustration: the single arrow passes successively through elephant, tiger, deer, boar and monitor lizard -->

<!-- anthology scan: 41; printed page: 40; source span: upper -->

A doubt may arise. When the arrow is shot—when it is travelling—what is the height of the elephant? After striking the elephant, how can the arrow go and strike a tiger that is lower than the elephant? Then how can that arrow strike a deer lower than the tiger? And then a boar shorter than the deer, and a monitor lizard lying close to the ground?

The king shoots the arrow from the top of a hill. Along the slope of the hill, the arrow passes step by step through the elephant, tiger, deer, boar and monitor lizard.

I told this story by way of explanation during the period when the Emergency was in force in India.

If a rutting elephant is killed by the Emergency, one may say never mind. If a leaping tiger is killed, one may say never mind. But must the spotted deer that bounds along, and the innocent monitor lizard, also die? I asked. I think there is no need to explain what I was comparing them with.
