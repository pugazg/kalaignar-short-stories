# Ezhuthalar Ekalivan

That day there was a shop shutdown! Everywhere across Tamil Nadu, in every place without exception! The shutdown had not been ordered by the government, nor requested by the opposition parties. The traders themselves had closed their shops in protest against a government that had not come forward to fulfil their demands. Those who knew about the shutdown in advance had already bought and stored the essential goods needed at home. But poor people who lived from one day's earnings to the next were in distress, unable to buy anything with the wages they had earned only that day. Not only the shops—even lorries and autos, in support of the traders' agitation,

<!-- source scan 39; printed page 34 -->

had stopped here and there. More than all this, what greatly troubled the writer Ekalivan was that the cable-TV channels too had shut down that day! Usually he would sample a little of every programme broadcast in rivalry by domestic and foreign television channels, unable to decide what to watch and what to skip, and then begin writing enthusiastically at four in the afternoon. But that day, after the domestic television channels had given him a headache, he had switched them off altogether, rubbed on “Pulithailam,” and gone to sleep. He woke only at five in the evening, utterly weary.

His face was sour because he had been unable to watch any cable TV. On the ground floor, the elderly domestic worker was speaking loudly with his wife.

“Since morning today it's been a terrible nuisance for poor folk, amma. Only two or three big hotels were open. How can we go all the way there and pay who knows how much just to quiet our hunger! What a fine government this is—in these three years, how many times have they made the shops close!”

“Karuppi, your worry is your own! Upstairs, the master of our house has a great worry because there is no cable TV today.”

“If there had been, even you wouldn't have come downstairs as early as two o'clock, amma.”

<!-- source scan 40; printed page 35 -->

Ekalivan burst out laughing. Delighted that his wife had been neatly caught by Karuppi, he went into the bathroom. After bathing, he put on a khaki-coloured vest dyed with ochre, tied a flower-patterned lungi around himself, and glanced over the letters piled on his desk. One envelope bore the office seal of a famous weekly magazine. He tore that envelope open first and read the letter.

“We request you to help us by sending a short story within one week for publication in our next issue.”

That was the essence of the letter. He read it once more and placed it separately on the desk. Then he opened the other envelopes. Some contained readers' praise and criticism of his stories, articles, poems, short stories! Others contained cheques sent by the magazine offices that had published them. He carefully read and examined everything once, then again, locked the cheques inside the desk drawer, and called affectionately, “Leela!”

“Leela came a quarter of an hour ago,” she said with a teasing laugh, holding out coffee to him.

“Has it been a quarter of an hour? I didn't notice at all. If you'd come with a bell tied to your ankle, I'd have heard it and

<!-- source scan 41; printed page 36 -->

noticed you at once!”

“Buy me an anklet! It'll keep making a sound.”

“One anklet? For my Kannagi I'll buy a pair of anklets.”

“Oh! So the plan is to turn me into Kannagi and then go looking for some Madhavi?”

Unable to hold back his laughter, he struggled to swallow his coffee. He drew her into a tight embrace.

“I'm going home, amma.”

Hearing the domestic worker taking her leave, Leela slipped out of his arms.

“All right, Leela! I'll go toward the beach and come back.”

“Why? Alone? Uh-huh—to sing the *Kaanal Vari*?”

“Don't talk about tax—that's why the shops are shut today!”

“Come back soon. If you say you're going alone to the beach to think and then come home late, the town may have a shop shutdown—but this house will have a door shutdown!”

“First I'll shut your mouth now.”

—“Ich!”

Taking only the letter that had asked him for a short story within a week, Ekalivan descended the stairs. His feet began walking along the road toward the beach.

Across the road at the beach stood a cut-out made of electric lights, hiding the statue of Kannagi. Because it was not yet fully dark, the lamps had no flood of brilliance. “Have they hidden you too?” he asked inwardly, moved to one side, and looked to see whether the Kannagi statue was still there or had been removed. Fortunately, she stood there, anger overflowing, a single anklet in her hand.

A seed for the imaginary short story he had to write formed—and shattered at the very speed with which it had formed. The thought began, “How many Kannagis in Tamil Nadu, inside police stations.....” but at once another thought flashed like lightning: “Che! Haven't all the magazines already exhausted this as short stories—and as true stories at that?” The story-seed vanished.

“Is a short story written merely to be read as entertainment? Shouldn't it contain some idea that society needs?”

With that thought, writer Ekalivan had reached the place where the statue of revolutionary poet Bharathidasan stood. For a minute he remained staring at the statue.

“Is there no coolness in the fragrant breeze that comes?
In the streets of Tamil Nadu, it is Tamil that is absent.”

<!-- source scan 43; printed page 38 -->

How deeply the poet must have grieved, turning his longing into verse with tear-filled eyes! Why not make that very anguish the theme of a short story?

A surge arose in Ekalivan's heart. He decided he could weave a short story around a young man who thundered the slogan, “Tamil everywhere, Tamil in everything!” But at that very instant another obstacle fell across the idea. Was that not the effort begun by many Tamil scholars such as Maraimalai Adigal, Thiru. Vi. Ka., and Devaneya Pavanar? Was Bharathidasan not expressing the anguish that this effort had not been fully fulfilled? When a separate struggle itself was needed to win a place of distinction for Tamil in the streets of Tamil Nadu, could the short story he was going to write attain that goal so easily? With a long sigh, he left the beach road and walked toward the spread of sea sand, looking at Gandhi's statue. He could not see the statue fully. A gigantic cut-out rose almost to the sky, hiding that statue too.

From sand that would not easily release a foot once it sank in, he pulled each planted foot out with effort and reached the place where the sea waves struck the shore. Though the arms of darkness had embraced the world, electric lights burning brightly across the sandy expanse continued to give light.

<!-- source scan 44; printed page 39 -->

As he moved slowly over the damp sand by the sea, five or six little crabs appeared before his eyes. Watching their bustling play from a distance, he felt an urge to go a little closer. But hardly had he moved toward them when every one of the little crabs darted into the tiny holes in the wet sand with a speed surpassing lightning.

Aha! A theme for his short story was ready. In villages, girls who had reached adolescence would be out at dawn sprinkling water before the house, sweeping, and drawing kolam. If some man happened to come along the street, wouldn't they bustle just like these little crabs and hide inside the house?

Wouldn't it be good, he thought, to begin with the flight of these little crabs and write around the earthy fragrance of Tamil Nadu's villages, so that this old culture might not disappear from the villages? The moment that question arose in his heart, a whip-lash came from his brain:

“Those who thought it evil for women even to touch a book
have vanished away;
The strange men who said, ‘We will lock women inside the house,’
have bowed their heads.”

<!-- source scan 45; printed page 40 -->

Yes—it was Mahakavi Bharathi's song that had come back to him. The tip of that whip of memory, flung by his brain, struck his heart with a sting. He picked up that mouldy, hidebound imagination that village women ought to behave like crabs and threw it straight into the sea.

What else could become the central point of the short story he had to write?

Walking with his feet in the waves, he became absorbed in a fish that ran upon the waves, leapt and sprang, fell on the shore, then joined hands with the returning waves and went back to sea, only to come again and leap upon the shore—dancing without rest. When a wave came toward the shore, the fish came with it. When the wave returned, it too went back. Once the fish rode in on the back of a wave but remained on the shore instead of returning with it. Perhaps, Ekalivan imagined, it thought: now I am no longer under the wave's control; I can jump and dance on the shore exactly as I please. He imagined that this was why it was leaping—for the sake of his short story!

Poor thing. The fish laid on the ground became motionless within seconds. What dancing it had done only moments before! What speed it had leapt with! Everything subsided. He had even found a title for the story he was going to write—

<!-- source scan 46; printed page 41 -->

*The Story of a Fish*. He rejoiced inwardly that the title had come together well. Just then, a dog that came running that way seized the dead fish in its mouth and bounded away in a single leap. If only that fish had remained where it was supposed to remain... he drifted into imagination.

He had only to go home and begin a short story centred on this incident. Then a doubt arose. What if it turned into a political story and they hesitated to publish it? No! No! He could think of something else. Leaving the wave-beaten shore, he went and sat on the sandy stretch directly opposite the lighthouse. In the sky of imagination were dark clouds of thought—but not even a drop of rain fell. Then how could he plant the seed of a short story? He slowly closed his eyelids and listened to the waves' lullaby when his ears were interrupted by a boy calling:

“Kandal murukku, peanuts!”

Ekalivan opened his eyes. The boy did not even turn toward him.

“Hey, Kandal Murukku! Can't you see someone sitting here? Come here, thambi.”

“Sorry, sir! When I saw your lungi and vest I thought you were some fisherman and walked on. How much do you want, sir?”

“Give me everything for two rupees.”

He held out the money. The boy wrapped the goods into a parcel and handed it over.

“Tell me, thambi—what village are you from? At this age, why aren't you studying? Why are you doing this?”

“My story is a long story, sir!” The boy said it and rose. Ekalivan thought that if he could keep the boy talking, he might get fine material for a short story. “Stay, thambi! Sit for a while! I'll buy all your stock. What village are you from?”

“Sivakasi, sir!”

“You came all the way from Sivakasi to Chennai to do this work?”

“Yes, sir. Back in Sivakasi, my father, my mother, and I—the three of us—had good work.”

“Good work? What work was that, thambi?”

“Work in a match factory!”

“Then why did you come here?”

“One day, suddenly, the factory caught fire. My father was burnt to death right there.....”

The boy fell silent for a moment and wiped his eyes. Writer Ekalivan's eyes too filled with tears.

“What happened to your mother, thambi?”

“My mother..... survived.... but one whole arm was burned.... now

she is the one support I have, and I am the support she has!”

“Thambi, tell me your whole story! I'm going to write it and give it to a magazine. Whatever honorarium they give me for it, I'll give all of it to you and your mother.”

On the boy's face appeared expectation—hope—that unclouded light became visible in the lighthouse beam. The young child was ready to tell his story. Ekalivan was ready to hear it.

“My father, mother, and I—the three of us—worked at a factory in Sivakasi.”

“There's a law saying small children must not be made to work, isn't there, thambi?”

“If we look at the law, what way is there to get food? Will the law feed us, sir? If my mother and father had earned enough, they wouldn't have sent me to work; they'd have made me study......”

Just then another boy came across the sandy stretch so fast that one could hardly tell whether he was walking or flying. Panting for breath, he shouted as he arrived: “Hey, Kuppi! You're here? Run, da, come running!”

“Hey, why? Why?” Kuppan asked, but before he could finish,

“Your mother was hit by a bus and died, da!”

“Ah! Amma—aiyo, Amma!” Leaving the vessels that held the goods he had been selling, and forgetting

<!-- source scan 49; printed page 44 -->

Ekalivan who had been listening to his story—why, forgetting everything—the boy ran. The boy who had brought the news ran with him.

“Thambi! Thambi! Stop!” How could writer Ekalivan's call fall upon their ears?

“What a tragedy! We let him go without even asking where he lives. If only we knew, we could go there and do whatever help is within our power. Why, he's a good boy—we could even keep him in our own house and raise him. Now I don't know what to do!”

Tears from the bewildered writer Ekalivan's eyes wet his cheeks.

He picked up the vessels the boy had left behind and walked with his feet sinking into the beach sand.

From a somewhat darkened area came a voice:

“Hey, Kandal Murukku fellow, bring it here!”

The owner of the voice lay with his head resting in the lap of a fashionably dressed woman.

The same voice continued: “Hey, I'm calling you! Why are you standing like a statue?”

Yes. The man who had come searching for a story stood on the beach just like a stone statue.
