# Kannadakkam

<!-- source scan 166; printed page 157 -->

A devotee ran in panic. His feet stumbled. Sweat had turned his body into a pool. His hair had come loose in disorder and flew in the wind. The sacred ash smeared on his forehead, and even the broad vermilion mark above it, had dissolved in the sweat and were running down his cheeks like blood and pus. His face looked like a foul-smelling stream issuing from a festering wound. And over all this came his cries: “Devi! Devi!! Kalika Devi!!!”

It was pitch-dark. The river was in flood, overflowing its banks. Across it stood a bamboo bridge. The devotee ran over it without steadiness. A corpse burning at the cremation ground on the bank was being dragged away by the river water. He saw it. Crying “Lokamatha!” he turned. Four or five people were approaching with torches. One held a fire-pot. They were carrying a corpse. This was not the wet corpse floating in the river; it was a fresh corpse. The devotee stood on the bamboo bridge. Utter silence. The fresh corpse began to burn. Its relatives had gone away. From now on it belonged either to the fire or to the sky-fish reflected in the river.

If the relatives were still alive in the morning, they would come there to gather the bones and cast them away. Before that, the river might do the job itself. Sighing deeply, the devotee resumed his running journey. Ahead were more torches. Not a bier! On a long pole was a thuni-en; upon it, a child. A sleep that would not awaken. There was no fire-pot. Some men carried spades. A corpse for burial, perhaps! The devotee began to run many times faster than before. Karuvel thorns pierced his feet. He did not notice. His face scraped against the thorny screw-pine leaves along the bank. He did not notice that either. Thorns stood out all over his body like the hooks—like the silagaigal—seen on the body of one carrying a milk-kavadi to Palani. How he crossed the ruined well ahead, no one knew. How he passed the Pavadairayan and Kathavarayan shrines, terrifying even to look at, he himself did not know! He reached the edge of the village. There—a temple. Forest all around. The shrine within! “Amma! Mother!!” he shouted. He ran as though possessed. The door was locked. He leapt onto the compound wall and jumped down on the other side. Before him stood the fierce form of Kalika Devi. A roaring lion—a blood-dripping head—all only in sculpted form! So the devotee went near the Mother without fear. “Bhadrakali? Mahadevi!! Mahishasura Mardhini!!!” he cried.

“Who is that?” Kali asked.

“Do you not know me?” shouted the devotee.

“I do not.... Tell me!”

“I am your devotee—Kalidasan!”

“Which Kalidasan? The one on whose tongue I wrote and made into a master of words—is it that Kalidasan?”

“No, Mother...I am not that man! I am naturally a little intelligent myself! Still, I am your devotee!”

“All right then—devotee! What do you want?”

“What is this, Mother? Do you not know the trouble happening in the village?”

“Trouble?”

“Yes, Devi! People are dying in heaps. Terrible vomiting and diarrhoea! Deadly poisonous fever! They say plague; some new English disease! Jaundice!.... Ayyo...it cannot even be told, Mother; baskets and baskets of children’s corpses; bier after bier of adult corpses!”

“Ayyo...is that so? I did not know!”

“It is true, Amma, it is true! In another day or two all your people will be gone!”

“Devotee! I will tell you one thing. Will you listen?”

“Tell me a thousand things, Mother!”

“Convey my deepest condolences to those left in the families destroyed by the cruel disease!”

“I did not come to ask for condolences, Sakthi! I came seeking refuge. I came to appeal to Mother to rescue the maanudindra people. I have made it here. By this time how many bodies have lost life—there is no count! The cremation ground has no rest. Proper food for the fire! Arise and come, my Mother!”

“I cannot, son! I cannot! Forgive me. Devotee, go!”

“Forgive you? Me? This wretch forgive Akilandeswari? I fall at your feet; whatever anger you have, forget it and come forth, Parasakthi!”

“Whether you lament or cry, there is no punyam!”

“Why, Kali—why? You who possess gracious eyes that destroy the wicked and protect the pure! Anantha Nayagi! Open those eyes that rain compassion and save the helpless women, the orphans, your holy children who are dying without refuge—why this hesitation, Mother?”

“Son, I do not have eyes that rain compassion now!”

The devotee’s body trembled. His voice thickened. In fear he cleared his throat. He looked up at Kali and roared.

“No compassionate eyes? Are you Kali—or a kallI? What difference is there between you and a demon that rejoices at pinakkolu? You slew the buffalo—you subdued the lion—you cut off the head of the mamathaiyaalan—then there was fire in your eyes! At other times, is there not supposed to be a flood of love? That is what I have heard about you! Are you trying to deceive me? While the lives of your children fall away pol-pola, is your work to rest in this forest-side ‘bungalow’? What wonderful motherly affection, Amma!”

“Son! However much you abuse me, however much you sing a puranam of curses with sparks flying, you cannot expect compassion from me! I am not the Kali who made Kalidasan and Kalamegam sing poetry—not now. I am without compassion! There is no light of love in my eyes!”

As she said this, Kali’s throat rasped. A crying voice was heard. The sobbing sound bored into the devotee’s ears. He was startled.

“Why are you crying, Devi? What happened?”

“It is all the work of a devotee like you!”

“What did he do? Which devotee committed the sin? Who created a situation in which Parvathi Devi had to weep? Padmasani! Tell me, Mother!”

“Listen carefully! I possess the power and ability to save all the people of the world together. And I used to save them. Those dreadful deaths and the diseases causing them that you described as happening in the village—all of them, the moment my gaze fell upon them—

would fly away like cotton. Even Paramasivan’s forehead-eye does not possess the power my eyes have, he himself would say when ruling over me! My sacred eyes have the fire that destroys injustice and the gracious gaze that protects orphans! One supreme devotee ruined that power, son!”

“How, Mother, how?” the devotee cried again.

“Listen without interrupting, son! A devotee—a rich man. He had a child. There was some small trouble in the child’s eye. He prayed to me. The child’s eye gradually began to worsen. Doctors began treatment with medicines and herbs. The child’s eyes began to recover. Then the devotee prayed, making a vow to me. According to that vow he promised to offer a **‘kannadakkam’**. You know what kannadakkam means, do you not?”

“Yes, Devi! They say an eye is made in gold or silver and fixed over the eyes of the idol; I have never seen one!”

“Never seen it? Look at what has been fixed over my eyes! This is kannadakkam! That devotee’s vow.”

“What connection does that have with the purpose for which I came, Mother?”

“Fool! Do you still not understand? He closed the very eyes that possessed the power to show compassion and save people, covering them with this silver kannadakkam. How can I come out now?—What way is there for me, blindfolded? How can grace come forth from closed eyes?”

“He is no devotee; he is a sinner! Mother, if he committed this injustice, could you not at once have removed this silver covering?”

“Remove it? Son, this is an offering made by a devotee! Can it be removed? It must not—it must not!”

“It may be improper for you to remove what a devotee gave! Here, I will remove it. Let your eyes open. Let compassion pour forth. Let the people of the village live! Let your procession take place!!” In a frenzy the devotee sprang forward and tore away the silver kannadakkam fixed over Kali’s eyes and threw it aside.

The devotee stared closely at Kali’s eyes.

“Can you see me now, Mother?”

“Yes—I can see that you are a fool who is fond of me!”

“Why, Mother, do you blame me again? Show me some gratitude, Mother! I have opened the eyes that rain love so as to save the people of our world who are passing away!”

“You opened the eyes; but you cannot give them light! Closed for ten years, my eyes have become like a well gone dry from disuse, son! Was it one year? Two years? Has not this kannadakkam hidden my eyes for ten years? No one can now restore light to my eyes and make compassion overflow in that light! Please go away!” Kali spoke angrily through tears.

The devotee stood for a while and laughed mockingly at Kali. He thoroughly wiped away the remaining vermilion and sacred ash sticking to his forehead and made the forehead clean. Freed from the temple, he ran toward the village.

Ahead, doctors and their assistants arrived in two or three cars. Preventive injections were urgently given to all the people of the village. Through the night, the doctors began carrying out the immediate sanitary measures the village required. The devotee too took a leading part in that work.

Suddenly I woke. I realized that I was lying in an eye hospital with both eyes bandaged. Even in that painful moment, I laughed to myself, thinking that however many operations were performed on that Kali, her eyes would never open.
