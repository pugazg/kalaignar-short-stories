# The Prisoner's Story

<!-- source scan 44; printed page 43 -->

Santhanam says:

Kumudham studied with me. She was a maiden from Kubera’s house. I was the son of a peasant. Determined somehow to fulfil my dead father’s wish, my mother educated me. I studied. Kumudham and I—why stretch the story?—fell in love. Apart from the fact that a poor man had loved a powerful man’s daughter, no one could point to any other fault in our love. As usual, our plan to marry was broken up. My aged mother too brought her life to an end. Then, one night, Kumudham came running to my hut. “I have become your bonded slave; come, let us make a family,” she called. “The opposition will rise like a mountain, Kumudham,” I warned her. “Our love is the little chisel that can split the mountain,” she said. How could I refuse? We set out that very night.

Our family life began in another town. It was blissful. Why should I cry now, thinking of that life of happiness! A little treasure—a son—was born to us.

.... We walked the road of life joyfully with the child. Kumudham’s elder brother—he had gone to Malaya to earn money—returned. The family had told him what had happened. He had come back with a Burmese woman as his wife. He became fiercely angry with Kumudham and set out to confront us. He was a rough man. It was not easy to handle him in a fight. Someone had told him that it was my beauty that had enslaved Kumudham—indeed, Kumudham herself often used to say so. Declaring that he would ruin that very beauty, her brother set out carrying a caustic liquid. I was asleep at home. He arrived. I woke up. He flung the liquid onto my handsome face. Ayyo—the thought of that burning still frightens me.

It burned worse than the belly-fire of the jealous! They say it is better not to touch with the hand a place on which such liquid has been poured—but in an emergency does such advice come to mind? I wiped my face with my hand. That was when all this flesh tore away. Kumudham’s brother ran. I chased him and tried to catch him. I could not. I collapsed in the street.

Kumudham and the child were not at home then. She had gone to take the boy to school. Someone from the street immediately carried me to a medical

hospital. Kumudham and our son Selvan came running to the hospital and cried out. Kumudham saw the horror of my face and screamed. At first I thought she was screaming at the very height of grief. Only later did I guess that she had cried because the handsome face she wanted was gone. The next day, while I was still in the hospital, I heard that Kumudham had gone away somewhere. Only the child Selvan was cared for by people of the street.

A case was brought against Kumudham’s brother. I too received treatment and came out with this disfigured face. The case went on for nearly five years. In the end Kumudham’s brother was acquitted. Judgment was given that he was not guilty.

I could not bear it. I writhed in intolerable anger. I thought that if I did something in haste, the child Selvan would be left without anyone. At last, through the recommendations of a few people, I placed Selvan in a boarding hostel attached to a school in another town and heaved a great sigh.

Following that sigh came the breath of vengeance against Kumudham’s brother. Kumudham had gone somewhere because she could not bear the grotesque form of my face. Kumudham had not loved my heart; she had loved only my beauty. The fiery liquid thrown by her brother taught me that great truth. Yet if a thorn pricks us as though warning us that the path ahead is bad, can we pull out that thorn and kiss it merely for giving the warning? We break it, snap it

and throw it far away! In the same way, I had no mind to spare the brute who ruined my face and thereby taught me that the path of love was bad.

I gave my beloved Selvan pieces of advice. A thousand times I told him, “Commit whatever wrong you want, but never love a woman.” Then I set out with a knife in my hand. Kumudham’s brother, that Burmese woman, and their daughter were sitting near the courtyard. Without asking anyone, I entered. There was no one else in the house. Kumudham’s father had died two years earlier. I had heard that the Burmese woman too had contracted leprosy and was suffering greatly.

Though all these sorrowful events had struck Kumudham’s brother and caused him pain, I still longed to settle my vengeance directly. I moved quickly toward them. The Burmese woman was coughing hard. I drove the knife in my hand into Kumudham’s brother’s chest—yes, straight toward the heart of that sinner who had disfigured me. I stabbed again and again. He died there. That is the result you see: all these years of imprisonment. He killed me without killing me and went free. I killed him in one stroke and received punishment.
