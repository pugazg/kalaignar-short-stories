# Thidukkidum Kathai

[Note: This imaginative piece is drawn from a small humorous anecdote.]

<!-- source scan 199; printed page 190 -->

We have heard a great deal about New York City, even if some of us have never seen it in person! Travellers describe it not merely as America’s beautiful city but as a city of splendid towers. There are multi-storeyed mansions there that touch the sky! A newcomer may naturally wonder whether the light burning at the top is an electric lamp or whether that upper storey rises all the way to the realm of the stars. In one hotel with many storeys of such enormous height, three friends rented a room near the very top and stayed there. They had come to see the city. Usually they would come down by the “lift” provided for climbing those tall buildings, tour all the attractive parts of the city, and return to their room at ten at night. In this way the three spent several days enjoying the elegance of New York.

One day, after seeing every amusement, novelty and curious entertainment, they returned to their hotel. The hotel manager on the ground floor looked at them and gave them a regrettable piece of news. Namely:

<!-- source scan 200; printed page 191 -->

“Dear friends! Today you will have to walk to your room, because our ‘lift’ has broken down. I am sorry.” That really was regrettable news. If they entered the lift and sat down, they could reach the appointed place within a few minutes without any trouble. But because the lift had broken down, there were now many storeys they had to cross. The steps they had to climb numbered more than two thousand. After roaming the whole city until their legs were exhausted, was it an ordinary matter to climb so many steps, circle floor after floor, and reach their room? What could they do—they had to go. The three decided to climb the stairs. To keep themselves from feeling the fatigue, they also made a new and cheerful arrangement. Before reaching their room, each of the three would tell one story on the way. The first would be a love story, to be told by the man named Romeo. The next would be a heroic story, to be told by Antony. The third would be a startling story, to be told by Charles. Accordingly, Romeo began with the love story.

## Love Story

Babylon was a city of wealth. The characters of the story I am going to tell lived there. A man named Ovid wrote it beautifully.

Two houses stood joined together like twin children. A single stone wall divided the houses. In them lived two lovers whose hearts had become one. Thisbe was a figure of beauty; Pyramus a handsome young man. Their parents decided to shut away and conceal their love, though their minds were united. With burning hearts, they withered inside their homes. That clay wall served as a fence set against their feelings.

<!-- source scan 201; printed page 192 -->

Whose eyes could be sharper than the eyes of lovers? A small opening in that wall attracted their attention. That was enough!

Without caring what hour or time it was, Thisbe and Pyramus began whispering their words of love. On one side of the wall Pyramus would pour out words, describing her as he stood there. On the other side, Thisbe would stand lost in herself, agreeing with all of it. Without caring whether it was daytime or the dead of night, they bathed in a sea of delight. The clay wall that for so many days had stood as a barrier separating the two love-birds now became a great bank holding back their stored flood of joy, and thus rendered them great assistance. Through the hole, the music of loving words sounded at all hours. Their lips never touched. Yet the soft sound “ich” seemed always to tremble there.

How long could they live as lovers deceiving their parents? Those young hearts decided to go elsewhere, marry, and begin a household life.

Near the city of Babylon stood Nisin’s tomb. They decided that at night they would meet beside the malberi tree near it, and from there flee the parents who were cutting down their youth with a fortress called restriction.

Women are always hasty, are they not? At the appointed time Thisbe reached the foot of the malbari tree, without letting her heart fail before the midnight at which even thieves tremble. But she could not find Pyramus there. In that darkness, where could the innocent girl look for him? Even the sound of dust moving in the wind seemed to that poor woman like the sound of Pyramus’s footsteps. She heard a sound like “O!” and turned.

A lioness that had killed a spotted deer was approaching, licking its blood-dripping tongue. Poor Thisbe trembled violently. To hide herself from the gaze of the approaching lioness and save her life, the young woman threw off the upper cloth that covered her breast and fled in a single instant. The old lioness clawed the cloth that floated in the wind and covered its face, tore it with blood-stained teeth, and then walked back toward its cave.

Pyramus, who had kept his beloved waiting, arrived only after the lioness had moved away. He searched for her and searched. But all he could see was Thisbe’s blood-stained upper cloth and the lion’s footprints.

His heart bursting and his body trembling, he raised a great cry of grief. “By arriving late I have lost my beloved! You did not die because of the lion. My dear one, you died because of me alone. At midnight—in the middle of the forest—I told you, a young woman, to come alone; I myself killed you!” Lamenting like this, he drove his sword into his chest and fell to the earth embracing Thisbe’s upper cloth, that handsome young man.

Meanwhile Thisbe returned to the malbari tree, building castles in her mind that her lover would have arrived and that they could go to a far-off land and live a life of love. Alas! She could not believe the sight before her. A sharp sword was lodged in the heart of the king of her heart. Thisbe leapt at once, embraced her lover’s body and cried, “Beloved, it was not the cruel sword that drank your life; I killed you. Your unfailing love for me killed you!” She pulled the sword lodged in Pyramus’s chest and plunged it into her own breast. Subjected to the restraints of their relatives and the condemnation of the town, heartsick and setting out to begin a new life, the lovers lay as corpses beside Nisin’s tomb—

—together. The pair whom society would not unite were joined by death with its dark hands! But there is one thing: Pyramus, who had come late to the malbari tree and caused Thisbe pain, had gone early to the flower-garden called death and was already searching there for his precious life.

Romeo, who told this story, and the other two became absorbed in it and had crossed more than a thousand steps. Only a little distance remained to climb. As agreed, Antony began to tell the heroic story.

## Heroic Story

“Romeo, under the name of a love story, really did tell a love story. Under the title of a heroic story, I am going to tell a strange story—very briefly. Listen, both of you!

“Was there not a great uprising against the slave trade? In the same way another uprising took place to protect a different right. Do not ask in what town it happened. I do not know the name of the man who led the uprising either. Its aim was this: a certain caste-group in that town had to be driven out of the town altogether. The leader of that caste-group was the very man who governed the town. The leader of the uprising spoke furiously, demanding that the leader’s caste be driven out. He roared that if those people did not leave, they should be killed wherever they were found. The caste leader was very cunning. Using his strategy, he found a man born in the caste of the rebels themselves and made him the new leader of the town. The new leader summoned the leader of the uprising and said, ‘From now on you must not speak of driving anyone out of this town, or speak in a way that frightens them. If you do, I will take severe action against you.’ Faced with the new leader’s threat—

<!-- source scan 204; printed page 195 -->

—the leader of the uprising immediately cried, ‘Jai to the new leader!’ He promised that he would no longer speak of driving out the caste leader or his people and began turning the attention of the warriors in the rebel force elsewhere. That, my friends, is my heroic story!” Antony finished.

The three had now climbed almost all the two thousand steps and were near their room. The third story, the startling story Charles had to tell, still remained. Antony looked at him and said, “Charles, tell your story.” Charles began: “Friends! I really am going to tell a startling story now. We who have climbed two thousand steps must go down two thousand steps again—and then climb two thousand steps once more,” he said.

“Why—is your ‘startling story’ that long?” Romeo asked.

“No, Romeo! I left the key to our room downstairs in the manager’s room,” Charles said sorrowfully.

(Surely that must have been a startling story for the other two.)
