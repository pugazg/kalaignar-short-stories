# The Yasodhara Epic

Former I.G. R.N. Manickam gave a statement before the Ramamurthi Commission appointed in connection with the industrial-spirit corruption scandal. In it, his

<!-- anthology scan: 42; printed page: 41 -->

two relatives, he stated, had paid nearly 11 lakh rupees as a bribe to M.G.R., to M.G.R.'s elder brother, to the sons of M.G.R.'s elder brother and, generally, to M.G.R.'s family, in order to set up an arrack-blending factory. But they did not receive the licence for that factory.

Opposition leaders asked in the Legislative Assembly what the Chief Minister's answer to this was. Instead of answering himself, the Chief Minister wrote an explanation and had it read out through Navalar. “We want one day's debate on this statement and this explanation,” we asked.

Navalar agreed. Speaker Rajaram and Electricity Minister Ramachandran also agreed.

After all this, two days later, it was said that the ministers and the Speaker had answered without understanding the law; the assurance they gave did not bind him, and what they had said was wrong.

Navalar, Rajaram and Panruti Ramachandran—these three had been ministers of the Dravida Munnetra Kazhagam.

How well and respectfully we had treated them. I sincerely felt sorry that such a fate should befall them.

<!-- anthology scan: 43; printed page: 42 -->

Had they been anyone else, they would immediately have resigned their posts and come away. At that moment I remembered a story I used to tell often in the past.

There is a northern epic called the “Yasodhara Kaviyam.” A man named Yasodharan was a king ruling a country called Avanti. His wife's name was Amirthavalli. She enjoyed palace life with that king. Though she was with the king, though she had the pleasures of palace residence—a durbar hall, a retinue of attendants to carry out the commands she issued—even with all this, that queen loved an elephant mahout in the palace: a scabby, hunchbacked, half-blind, feeble fellow.

She would wait all night for this elephant mahout. He would arrive late. As soon as he came, he would strike the queen ten times with the whip in his hand. She would endure it. After that the two would embrace and enjoy themselves in the grove. The queen found a pleasure in that.

No matter how lovingly and supportively the Maharaja kept her in the palace and gave her rosewater drops and fruit-juice drinks, the queen did not like any of these things. The elephant mahout would strike her ten times, ask, “Why did you come so late, woman?” and then embrace her; she found delight in the pleasure he gave her.

It is just the same with all these people here. The pleasure they had while they were with the Maharaja in the palace was not enough for them. Just as the queen thought that only the pleasure given by the elephant mahout was truly pleasure, they have reached the point where they believe only what M.G.R. gives is pleasure and that there is no greater pleasure, making themselves his Amirthavalli

—when I see that, I feel deeply pained.
