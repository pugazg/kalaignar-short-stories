# The Truth Buddha Made Clear

> **English translation of the verified Tamil archival reading.** Controlling source: 1987 collection `கலைஞர் சொன்ன குட்டிக் கதைகள்`, lower scan **15 → 16 → 17 → upper 18**. The verified Tamil remains authoritative.

<!-- anthology scan: 15; printed page: 14; lower shared span -->

There are people who even today imagine that mahatmas—great men—capable of saving the dead once existed in this world—

<!-- anthology scan: 16; printed page: 15 -->

—that they even exist now, and who, by saying so, create an illusion in people's minds.

But there is a note about an incident in the life of Buddha, who wished to make the world understand reality as it is.

One day, as Buddha was walking along a street in a town, a woman came running to him, weeping and wailing, carrying the corpse of a child in her hands. She came to Buddha and cried.

“What happened to you, mother?” Buddha asked.

Placing the body of her dead infant at Buddha's lotus feet, the woman said, “This is my child; I have lost this one and only child. You must give it life.”

The whole world celebrates Buddha! He is praised as a great human being! She believed that he could do things beyond human power.

That was why she placed the body of her dead child at Buddha's feet and pleaded, “Somehow give this child life and make it live again!” She knelt and cried out.

Buddha realized that the woman could not easily be consoled. “All right, mother! I will do so,” he said, and then added, “You do one thing.”

Believing that her child would live again, the woman asked, “Whatever the task is, I will do it. Tell me.”

<!-- anthology scan: 17; printed page: 16 -->

Buddha immediately said, “Go into this town and bring me a handful of rice.”

“Is that all? I will bring it right now,” the woman said and began to run.

Buddha clapped his hands and called her back. Startled, she returned and asked, “What is it?”

“Bring a handful of rice. But do you know from which house that rice must be obtained? Bring it from a house in which no one has ever died,” Buddha said.

She was desperate for her child to live. So she went all through that town asking; she ran to the neighbouring town; she ran to nearby villages. At every house where she asked for rice, people were ready to give it. But at some time or another a death had occurred in that house.

A death last year, or a death four years earlier! An infant had died! An only daughter had died! A bridegroom had died! A father had died! There was not a single house that did not have such a story of death to tell.

She returned and stood before Buddha with her head bowed. Then she looked at him and said, “I have understood the greatness of your instruction. I am going.” She took the dead child and left.

Thus there is no house where death has not occurred! Death is something that can touch every house and every place.

<!-- anthology scan: 18; printed page: 17; upper shared span -->

If Buddha had wanted to present himself as “a great one, a person endowed with glory,” stories could even have been invented saying, “In that town he restored that child to life! In this town he restored this child to life.” But because they regarded such stories as things that could corrode society, it is for that reason that we find incidents of this kind in accounts of Buddha's life.

- `மகாத்மியம்` is rendered “greatness” in the woman's realization; no doctrinal explanation has been added.
