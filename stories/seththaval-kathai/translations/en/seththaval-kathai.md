“The fire first set—in the three cities!
The fire set later—in southern Lanka!
The fire set that day—in the gaping hearth!
The fire I too set—in my Yaanumark cigarette!
Blaze, blaze away!”

It would make anyone ask, “Who is this ‘modern Pattinathar’?”

Neither Pattinathar......nor Kittinathar......It was a comic feast prepared by Ellappan.

Before smoke could rise from the cigarette, Sellayi burst into laughter. Only the previous night she had returned after seeing the film Pattinathar.

“What is it, dear! You are saying something or other. He threw the appam saying, ‘The ottappam will burn the house’......and immediately the house began to burn.”

“It will burn......it will burn.........It burns better in cinema than in drama.”

“Would they make a film showing Pattinathar burning it like that if he hadn’t really done it?”

“There is a substance called phosphorus. They must have put it in the appam and thrown it. The house would have been reduced to baspam.”

“All right! So that is how he put it in the appam and burned the house. But at the cremation ground he stacked banana sheaths over his mother...sang a song and burned her...how was that done?”

“Everything is like that! Lie quiet. You going to see films and me getting beaten by your questions...this itself has become a great nuisance!”

“I’ve taken a vow that I won’t go to see films anymore.”

“What kind of performance is that? Then the sky itself will overturn and pour rain!”

“As long as these men exist...is it fit for women to go outside?”

“All right, all right!......So you want all men to die.”

“If women are to go outside, they must be locked in a box and carried. Otherwise...all the men must tie their eyes tight before they go out.”

“Sellayi!...Sellayi!...You are laying down all sorts of laws that won’t pass!......What happened? Tell me in detail.”

“What is there to happen! I can’t walk in the street! I can’t even stand at the doorway! If I go to the cinema, there he is, all dressed up, parading about.”

“Who?......Who?”

“That Govindan from the house at the end of the street. Like a thick kottaappuli cudgel...a fellow with a sprouting moustache, forgetting that I am another man’s wife, yawning at me.”

“Hm!...Then?”

“You see how you are putting your hand on your moustache now—just like that—he twists his moustache at me. He gives me a smile. He lights a cigarette too. Wherever I go, he comes.”

“Sellayi! That is natural. An unmarried fellow! And though you are dark, you are in a form that steals the eye! Even for me, who sees you all the time....when I look at you, it feels as though a new life comes into me!”

“All right...all right...stop it!...This is your only work.”

Sellayi rose abruptly and went inside the adukkin. Ellappan relit the ‘cigarette’ that had gone out in the pleasure of conversation and went outside.

“What a body, man? What a body!....”

“Did you see her when she laughs...as if milk is flowing!”

“Does she speak to anyone with respect?...Does she have any fear, modesty, shyness?”

“She walks like a soldier.”

“Now I understand why our...Veeraiya Chettiar has moved into this side of town.”

“What is that, man?”

“To somehow enchant her...”

“It won’t happen, brother, it won’t.”

“Chettiar is rich, see; if he shows her money....”

“Money or whatever—only if a woman dislikes her husband....will all that happen. Even if a man is Kubera himself, if she does not like him...a woman’s mind can change.”

“Sellayi’s husband looks exactly like Arjuna Raja. Would she dislike him and go after the money of that crooked Chettiar...?”

“Only Ellappan is a lucky fellow, man! Men spend a thousand, ten thousand on marriage and end up with a wife whose eyes are diseased and ears are pierced through. Look at him—he has got a woman shaped like a top freshly turned on a lathe.”

“A perfectly matched pair. She is like a blue dove...and he suits her.”

The above were the “young men’s conference reviews” about the couple Ellappan and Sellayi.

The night meal was over. Sellayi could not sleep. Ellappan, who had gone out of town saying he would certainly return that evening, had for some reason not come. Four or five times she opened the door, came outside and looked down the road. Even the shapes of old men walking along the street kept deceiving Sellayi by appearing to be Ellappan....until clarity came. Like a statue on a temple tower, she held the front of the thatched house and stretched her eyes into the moon’s silver smile....then drew them back and placed them again on the road. She stood....stood....stood....stood until her legs were spent! A shadow moved in the moonlight. Perhaps she rejoiced thinking it was Ellappan....With her diamond smile she killed the moon and bounded into the house.

But it was Govindan walking along the street without taking his eyes off her house.

“Govindan from the house at the end of the street.” That sound scattered through Sellayi’s heart and died away. As the reflection of disappointment, the door of Sellayi’s house slammed shut with a bang.

Like a fruit knocked down by a stone, she lay down on the floor. In the speed with which she dropped down, the kitten that had been scratching at the still-unspread sleeping mat fled in fright.

Before long Sellayi’s breathing grew heavy; she fell into deep sleep.

As the moon that had been peeping through the gaps in the thatch sank lower and lower, a beam of light poured through a hole in the roof and made a circle around the bolt of the street door. Beneath the bolt, as though thrust forward with arrogance, a small iron rod moved—

about. After moving at many angles, the rod lifted the bolt upward, turned it to the other side and pushed it away, then withdrew through the same hole from which it had come and disappeared.

The door opened slowly without a sound. He came inside. Yes...Govindan from the house at the end of the street.

In his hand were orange peels, half-eaten halwa, and a few pieces of kara-boondi in a paper! He looked at them once.

If Sellayi saw him and screamed, the people of the street would run in. They would take his life. The things he had brought were proper shields against that danger! He could look at those who came and say, “Varunaiyaa vaarum! She is pretending to be a chaste woman. The two of us ate these fruits. I myself fed her the halwa. She poured half this kara-boondi into my mouth. Who is at fault? You have come running and shouting merely because she screamed over some quarrel that arose for some other reason! Go back quietly and sleep,” and chase them away. Or, if he told Sellayi that he had come with such elaborate preparation and showed her the fruit peels and half the badam halwa, she too would be frightened. His purpose could then be completed without trouble.

All these cruel thoughts danced a terrible dance in the trembling of Govindan’s lips.

Sellayi was asleep. Looking at her face, Govindan sat beside her. He slowly raised his hands and pinched her cheeks. She was in deep sleep. Rubbing her cheeks, she turned and lay on the other side. Govindan trembled. He bit his lips.

“Sellayi! Sellayi!” Words of muttering rose from his dry tongue! Every letter of the words—

trembled. Her long hair—he stroked it! Again he pinched her cheek. Sellayi woke with a start.

“Aah!” How much fury was in that cry was told by the slap that landed on Govindan’s cheek. Sellayi’s mouth was closed.

A mute battlefield inside Sellayi’s hut! In the end Sellayi did not win. She was defeated. Blood was seeping from Govindan’s cheeks—from his eyelids—from the side of his neck—from his hands.

Sellayi’s mouth was no longer being held shut. Yet she remained mute.

“Look here!...What happened has happened; when your husband comes, don’t advertise it and ruin your own name! That is all I will tell you.”

Without even noticing the tears budding in Sellayi’s eyes, Govindan left.

“The fire first set—in the three cities!
The fire set later—in southern Lanka!!
The fire my wife set—in honour that will not fade!!!”

The thatch of the house wailed and sang as though Ellappan were singing these lines.

Ellappan came in the morning. Before that she had picked up and thrown away the fruit peels scattered in the house.

“Why do you look strange?”

“Nothing; my head hurts.”

“Here...apply this oil!”

Sellayi completely hid the matter from her husband. A doubt held her back: if he learned about “that night,” would he not live with her thereafter with troubled eyes? Days passed.

Govindan walked past Sellayi’s house a thousand times a day, in many decorations and many clothes! One day Sellayi was standing near the doorway. Govindan was walking along the street. A ‘paper bundle’ fell at Sellayi’s feet. Before Ellappan came, she ate the quoted ‘jeevey’ that was inside and threw the leaf and paper into the backyard. On another day Govindan, passing along the street, smiled. Sellayi smiled too. Another day a packet fell before her. She picked it up. Inside were ten silver rupees. She kept them safely in a box.

That day Ellappan had gone out of town. There was no moon that night; it was new moon.

Midnight had passed. The world was turning toward the rising sun. Ellappan was coming home. The eagerness to see Sellayi danced in every movement of his body.

The door was knocked. First softly—then quickly—then with a loud noise! The door did not open.

“Poor thing, she must be sleeping heavily—she works all day—what can she do? Apart from this time, what rest is there in her life?” Thinking this, he looked around the roof of the house and finally took up a rod. The rod went through the little hole in the door; the inner bolt turned slightly and opened—just as Govindan had opened it before!

Pushing the door, Ellappan went inside. The lamp was burning dimly. Calling “Sellayi” with affection overflowing, he came to the bed and touched her.

“Who is that?”

Sellayi sprang up, gathering herself. Ellappan did not speak at all; Govindan walked as far as the door. Then he flew away.

“Forgive me,” Sellayi cried.

“Don’t cry! Why are you crying? In this world no one seems to know what crying means. The prostitute cries. The chaste woman cries. The good man cries. The bad man cries. The rich man cries. The labourer cries.”

“I am crying because”......Sellayi did not finish.

“I know; you are not crying because you committed a wrong! You are crying because the wrong you committed has been discovered, Sellayi! People give all sorts of reasons for prostitution in this world! They sound the conch saying it is a defect in society! What are they going to say about your case now! Widows—women cut off from married life while still young—young women married to old men—along with all these, they must also add greedy demons like you. Tell me why you entered this prostitution work; why did you commit this betrayal? Give me some reason.”

“It is my fate!”

“Fate! A perfect trick for escaping blame! Go tell that to some devotee.”

“My mind went bad!”

“Hm! They say that if a woman does not get the husband she desires, her life will shrivel. They say that without a young man life will wither. Everything was according to your heart’s wish, yet I do not know what reason they will give for your going bad! Will they say a monkey’s mind jumps from tree to tree?”

“Sellayi!...How highly I thought of you. When I went out of town, I thought only of home. I would not raise my eyes to look at other women. In my heart I had made you into a ‘photo’ picture—

and kept you there! You have smashed and thrown away everything in a single blow!”

“Sellayi! You are lower than a worm floating in a sewer. Drunk on show and luxury, you are a miserable household insect that murdered its own sense! I learned one thing. Women too can go bad because of a monkey mind. Society cannot be responsible for everyone.”

“Don’t stab and gouge me with words; if you want, kill me.”

“I am going to kill you. Tomorrow I will not show myself before Govindan’s face as some ‘gosaa’. I will appear before him as a murderer. Sellayi, I have told you so many times on this very bed that if you died, I too would die. Now I am going to beat you to death and continue living.”

Before the words were finished, Ellappan sprang upon her.

“Ah, ayyo......hh......hh”

Sellayi crumpled to the floor. Ellappan removed the great thumb that had been pressed into the hollow of her throat.

Sellayi’s corpse was at the cremation ground! Ellappan lit the fire with his own hand.

“A woman of gold. She is going away as embers.”
The street’s lament.

“Not gold. Merely base brass with polish on it.”
The thunder in Ellappan’s heart!

Sellayi burned.

“The fire first set—in the three cities!
The fire set later—in southern Lanka!
The fire my wife set—in honour that will not fade!
The fire I too set—in the fortress of prostitution—blaze, blaze away!”

Ellappan did not sing.

This was the ‘mangalam’ that surged like waves in his mind for the ‘Story of the Dead Woman’.
