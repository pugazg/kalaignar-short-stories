Paavalareru Perunchithiranar sang here all those beautiful poems—the poems composed by the Revolutionary Poet. Through them, all the feelings they instil were poured out here in abundance. We must not simply say that we enjoyed all of them and went away. We must make them mingle as one with our blood and our feelings, and labour so that the Tamil people become a people who

So many great poets have played with this Tamil language. I have said this before and illustrated it at many events. The verbal contests among poets, the debates arising through language, have brought Tamil many benefits.

There is an imagined quarrel between Pugazhendi and Ottakoothan. In that imagination, their contest takes place in the royal assembly hall.

“O jasmine! As a white conch, the bee blows you...”

said Pugazhendi. Ottakoothan asked, “How can that be? You see a bee sitting on a jasmine flower, turn the jasmine into a white conch, and say the bee blows it. Do people blow a conch from the back? Or from the front?” Pugazhendi then said, “Because the bee is already intoxicated from drinking honey, it does not know which is the front and which is the back, and so it blows it.”

In more recent times there was a great playwright, Sankaradas Swamigal. I have read some

<!-- source scan 75; printed page 73 -->

historical notes about him. Sankaradas Swamigal was a great dramatic poet who fashioned many puranic stories into a theatrical style suited to his time. Among them, *Abhimannan Sundari Maalai* was one of the plays he wrote. It was one of the plays his troupe performed across the country.

In it, while Sundari believes that she is going to become Abhimanyu's wife, news arrives: “You are not going to be married to Abhimanyu; they are going to marry you to a man named Ilakkanan.” Hearing this, Sundari cries out in anguish. Then another message follows: “No, no, Abhimanyu himself is going to marry you; Ilakkanan has been driven away.”

She then compares her feelings to the sounds of the musical instruments resounding in the wedding hall. “Until now many kinds of music were sounding here. That music was a source of sorrow to me. The moment I heard that Abhimanyu was going to become my husband, all that sorrowful music changed,” she says.

“How?” asks her friend.

She says: “The thundhubi resounds ‘thum thum’. The conch resounds ‘pam pam’. The cymbals resound ‘theem theem’. Together they sound thum pam theem thom—‘thunbam theerndhom’, our sorrow is over.” This is dialogue Sankaradas Swamigal wrote in his play *Abhimannan Sundari Maalai*. (Using the musical instruments and their very sounds: sorrow is over—(thum, ‘thum’, ‘pam’, ‘pam’, ‘theem..... theem’) all joining as thom, thom, thunbam theerndhom—our sorrow is over. This is a land where great poets lived who spread its glory even through the music of the Tamil people.
