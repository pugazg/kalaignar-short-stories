“**We** are not merely students—we are the sculptors of the world to come!” Whenever he saw those ten students, **Kandaiya** would swear and say this. He would say it smiling. He kept repeating it as though reminding others of this alone were the purpose of his birth!

Our college was a general-goods market: whatever anyone came expecting, they could find without fail. Want love? A battalion of young men would march toward Kothai Mayilal’s jaakkai! The others would move aside with the great counsel, “Let them live,” without blocking the way. Want knowledge? There stood the library. One could embrace the books and weep over them.... Want gossip? A group would come asking whether you wanted an hour or the whole day! And there were medanic-show enthusiasts, virtuosos of card concerts, and countless others of these kinds—countless!

At the last ‘desk’ of that class, a few stars shone like a Vasishta constellation. Kandaiya was the moon moving among them.

“One must have an ideal in life,” he would preach like Bernard Shaw. He would bring Ingersoll forward too.

“Are we fortunate? We are the ones who must make the new Dravidam blossom. The darkness of oppression has swallowed the blue sky. Look! The bees that are our future generations stand singing praise, waiting to drink honey. O silver moon! People of the great Dravidian race! You are the ones who must rise!... Tomorrow itself Dravidam will bloom....” Kandaiya would scatter handful after handful of such poetically striding gems. When he mounted a platform, gatherings of pundits would fear that puranic doctrines themselves were about to be carried to the bier!

He was not alone; he had an army with him. At the last ‘bench’ sat Kannaiyan, the last boy in the class. Kandaiya had conferred on him the title, “He is the eldest in Bharathidasan’s lineage.”

Beside him sat Ramu. In that college he was the one who knew ‘Einstein’s theory’ well. A scholarship awaited him if he secured a first-class result. His dream was to go to England, study, and become a ‘professor’!

Another was a singer, forever murmuring that South Indian music must be “modernized”!

Kandaiya had been giving ideological shape to all these future geniuses and nurturing them—as though each would be assigned a department in a future Dravidian country! The college principal thought that this ‘Kandaiya’ group would bring the college a different kind of fame. Even if the teachers did not show them affection, they paid respect to their ‘future’ plans.

A statesman praised by the whole world, a scientific wizard, an expert painter, a master musician—their future appeared in many such forms.

Poor fellows! Their future had begun without stain, unlike a gamble; their student days gave them no warning of what age would turn that future into!

Circumstance is a sorceress dancing a sadir! Youth may surge, and there may be strength enough to destroy an enemy at a mere “Hmm”—yet she abandons all that and, with the smile of wealth, garlands a corpse sunk in decay. How could poor Kandayyas escape the cold grip of that deceptive sorceress called circumstance?

Years passed like this. I was moving along in a running train, saying “Ticket please!” That man sat silent, letting his thoughts graze somewhere far away. Thinking, “Right—here is one fellow caught,” I tapped him. He turned. “Don’t you recognize me, sir? I am Kandaiya, sir—the one who studied with you in college!” he said.

I could hardly believe it. I had imagined Kandaiya would have gone abroad for higher studies and would shine as a scholar admired by the world. I could not reconcile that image with this Kandaiya dressed in ordinary rags.

“What, Kandaiya! What are all those companions who were with you doing now? Did Ramu, who got a first class, receive the scholarship to go to America? And poet Kannaiyan—wasn’t he saying he was going to do some Tamil research?” In my eagerness I kept piling question upon question.

“Yes! You expected Kandaiya, a first-rate speaker, to become a good politician in the future.

“Ramu—a mathematical genius! Kannaiyan—a poet! Sundaram—a gem of music! Gopalan—a researcher who built a flying aeroplane when he was still a boy. They—

—all are clerks now,” he said, and burst into a loud sob.

“Look at this,” he said, showing me a photograph that had appeared in a newspaper. “Hey! Isn’t this Pichumani? Wouldn’t he stand struggling on the platform, unable to answer the questions you asked? What has happened to him now?” I asked.

“He is no ordinary Pichumani now. His father is a former Collector. His athimber is in the Delhi Secretariat. His son-in-law is related to a minister. Now he is going to the U.N. Assembly as our country’s representative!” He went on speaking heatedly. The train stopped at a station. “I’ll be going, Kandaiya,” I said and got down.

“Sir! Read that,” Kandaiya said, pointing to a poster pasted on the wall. I read it.

“We, the people of the free Indian Republic, along with freedom of speech, freedom of writing, freedom of worship and freedom to express opinion, provide and guarantee equal opportunity and the like to all citizens.”

I finished reading. The Constitution of India, which promised equal opportunity to everyone, seemed to mock me.

When we were in college, Kandaiya was a cannon of oratory! Ramu a mathematical genius! Kannaiyan a crest-jewel of poetry! Sundaram a gem of music! Gopalan an engineer who had learned his craft without even attending the classroom! Now they were all clerks!

Pichumani, who used to shrink on the platform like a touch-me-not, unable to answer Kandaiya’s speeches, now roams America like a mountain—

—while Kandaiya, who once defeated him and wore the garland of victory, is reduced to travelling without a ticket! The fragrance of a rose carried by the breeze! Its pollen that turns the black bee golden! That garden rose, which ought to spread fragrance through a native land rotting in stench, is withering on a white stretch of sand, in a desert beyond the reach of sight! That steel rod, which ought to have been forged into a sword to destroy enemies, is rusting in the damp of poverty and the waters of hunger! There—Pichumani, with scarcely any ability, climbs higher and higher through the favour of those around him. They squeeze these roses dry and use them to paint a red colour onto that white erukku flower.

When I was a student, I never liked Kandaiya’s ideology. Yet when I thought of this sight, my heart shed tears.

“Cry! Cry, stain-covered Dravidam! For the blackened cloud and the heart made heavy with sorrow, there is only one remedy—tear-water! It is on that stream of tears that the boat of liberation of the new society must float,” Kandaiya said, laughing aloud. Even as he said it, he wept. With that cry, the train moved away!
