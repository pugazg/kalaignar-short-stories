## Queen of the Art of Dance

She had been made to stand beside the fire pit. The red tongues of the blazing flames stretched, rose, bent, twisted and danced as though yearning to make prey of her. The punishment imposed on her was terrifying. She had been brought there so that it could be carried out.

She was to walk around the fire pit three times. On the third round she would be thrown into the flames. Even if she fell in of her own accord, it did not matter—there was an order not to stop her. She would be allowed to remain in the fire for only a few seconds; once she was half-roasted, barely alive and half-dead, she would be dragged out. They would look over her golden body to see where wounds had appeared. No one should imagine that a physician would then be called to heal those wounds and restore her life. Chilli powder capable of producing fierce burning would be sprinkled into them. Unable to bear the agony, she would leap up—writhe—and then the palace dance-<!-- source scan 26; printed page 26 -->master and the drummer would be summoned. They would beat the rhythm and sound the drum in time with her movements. Only after she had exhausted herself, fainted, fallen down and her breath had wholly ceased would they leave her alone. Arrangements had been made so that the king could watch this spectacle of death from the palace itself.

The corpse of that beauty, covered in burns, would be tied up and hung in the middle of the capital for four days! Crows and vultures would be allowed to peck and tear at her body to their delight. On the fifth day the body would be thrown into the sea.

Having accepted the punishment announced in this fashion, that woman with a body like gold stood near the field of embers.

After all, what crime had that graceful woman committed? What harm had she done to anyone? What dangerous deed had that lovely woman performed—enough to make the state rage and fire embrace her? Had she become a spy for a foreign country? Had she roamed as a sorceress helping to enslave a free land? Had she committed murder? Had she lost her chastity and destroyed a foetus? Had she waylaid passers-by and forcibly called, ‘Come, I will show you the way to the City of Pleasure’? No; no. Her crime was none of these!

<!-- source scan 27; printed page 27 -->

Who was she? Why was she standing there?

She was a dancer! Her name was Gomedhagam!

The Bhagavata luminaries who mixed devotion into their talk of Bharatanatyam would say that sin vanished at the touch of her feet. Young bulls of men would pour out floods of rapturous words, describing her as a jasmine creeper swaying in the wind—or a peacock of the woods!

Were those eyes—or spears in battle array?

Was that speech—or the three sweet fruits?

Was that waist—or the shape of two crescents?

So sang the gathering of poets!

The anklets at her feet would coo—and the eyes of the assembled spectators would plead! She would cast a sidelong glance—and young men would cry that they were going to battle the army of ‘Manmatha’!

Her fingers would curve—and the shoulders of warriors would bend! She would whirl round and round like a top—and youths in their prime would suffer as though cotton were dancing in a whirlwind!

Gomedhagam—the tender beauty who bestowed loveliness upon Aazhi Nadu! She was herself one of that country's wonders. In dance halls across the land she had received titles such as Apsaras, Queen of Dance and Queen of Beauty, and shone as a lamp of light for the art of dance. Poets—<!-- source scan 28; printed page 28 -->poets and sages, gathered in great assemblies, had showered countless honours upon that spear-eyed woman. Praised as the nation's treasure of beauty and casket of art, that golden creeper was the very woman now standing beside the fire pit. Why?

She had corrupted an ascetic. She had aroused desire in a young ascetic who preached good counsel to the people of the world and made him a slave to her bed. The king declared this a grave crime. In the king's court it was a most extraordinary case. Until then the court had never encountered one like it. Not even another case bearing a resemblance to it had come before them! Even verdicts delivered in cases more serious than this had never been so harsh and so dreadful.

The people of Aazhi Nadu and the officers of the royal court were astonished. They asked one another: the king had never given such a cruel punishment to anyone; why had he given it only to Gomedhagam? Hearing of the pitiful fate of that queen of art, many masters of the arts, their hearts jolted, submitted petitions for mercy and begged that the ruler's order be relaxed. Nothing reached the monarch's ears.

<!-- source scan 29; printed page 29 -->

‘Carry out our order,’ he said sternly; and he had even come to the top of the palace to witness the spectacle.

That day, Gomedhagam's dance was being performed in the royal hall for the very first time.

There is a woman called Gomedhagam, everyone in the palace had said; nowhere on earth is there anyone who can defeat her in dance. The king heard this. ‘Bring her to the royal hall,’ he ordered. She came. She danced. The emerald necklace that lay upon the king's chest—an extraordinary prize no one had ever received before—he gave to her. He also conferred upon her the title ‘Nattiya Kala Rani’, Queen of the Art of Dance. “What fortune I have received’ she said in thanks, ringing out with laughter. The victorious monarch rejoiced. Gomedhagam—Gomedhagam: he uttered the name like a mantra! She took her leave. To the king it came as a shock, as though beauty itself were departing from the palace!

He lay awake all night. Gomedhagam had plundered his heart! He did not even care to attend to affairs of state. He summoned his son. “Prince! From today, look after the affairs of the kingdom for a few days. I need rest!” he said.

Prince Inbasagaran gave his consent. He took up the affairs of the empire.

<!-- source scan 30; printed page 30 -->

He had to meet Gomedhagam somehow. He had to rain kisses upon those eyes that killed him without killing him—but how? The king thought—and thought—and no way revealed itself.

It was a night when the lovely moon came to caress the earth. Gomedhagam had closed her small blue eyes and was travelling through the land of sleep. A knock sounded at the outer door. She rose. She opened it. The king stood before her.

“Your Majesty!”

“Gomedhagam!”

“Here—at this hour?”

“I came to inspect the city. I turned this way so that I could ask whether you were well before I left.’

“I am very happy—what great fortune is mine!”

“Gomedhagam!” A tremor entered the king's voice.

“What is it, my king?”

“Nothing!” Even as he said it, he put his hand upon her shoulder. Gomedhagam freed herself and, in a somewhat firmer voice, warned him:

“Your Majesty.”

He had no intention of returning defeated.

<!-- source scan 31; printed page 31 -->

“Goddess of Art! Show me mercy!” he said, embracing her tightly.

“Go away from here!” Gomedhagam cried, pushing him off.

“I cannot! You are a subject who must obey me!” he said, pulling her toward him. “Move those lips, my delight! Today you are the Queen of the Art of Dance—tomorrow, the Empress of this very country!” he pleaded.

She refused—and gave him words of counsel.

“O king! This is a disastrous act! No—go away! I may be a dancer, but do not imagine that everyone with wealth may make me dance as they please! Gomedhagam is not such a woman! I know the art of family life too! I will never agree to take a crooked path merely to climb a tower!” she said aloud.

“Shh... do not make a noise!” he said, moving close again.

She became fire! Its result was a slap across his cheek!—after that, the king did not remain there. He left.

That incident brought a new turn in Gomedhagam's life. From that day onward, she refused even to see the wealthy men who came adorned in jewels to visit her.

<!-- source scan 32; printed page 32 -->

She would not even admit the poets who sang her praises! Performances in the dance halls stopped. She arrived at a firm conclusion: until the philosophy that a woman engaged in the arts must necessarily be a commodity for sale disappeared, women should not enter the arts. Welcoming neighbours—orphans—guests, conversing with them about the affairs of the world, giving them whatever help they needed—Gomedhagam's life turned in this direction! She came to dislike even living in the capital and set out to live in a tiny village tucked away somewhere in a corner. There stood a hut. In it lived she and the orphans who came seeking her support! She found a special joy in giving all the wealth she had earned by dancing to those without support.

It was at that time that a young ascetic came seeking her home! She welcomed with affection that very ascetic who would become the reason for the terrible punishment imposed upon her. The ascetic stood enjoying the dew-laden gaze of the young peahen who came before him with a platter full of fruit. It was this ascetic whom she was accused of corrupting—and for it she received a dreadful sentence! Yet the fact that she had refused to submit to the victorious king was not the only reason hidden in that verdict!

<!-- source scan 33; printed page 33 -->

Nor was that punishment given merely because she had corrupted an ascetic!

Her refusal to submit to him—her corruption of the man of penance—besides these two, another secret reason lay concealed! That reason was known only to him and to his confidential ministers. Alas, what pity; even Gomedhagam, queen of dance, did not know it!

And what if she had known? Could she have escaped the fire pit? Could she have escaped that terrifying death which stood before her with its mouth agape?

Lotus-red woman! Red fire was about to spread over that crimson rose! A grotesque platform had been erected in the middle of the capital! Her corpse was going to swing upon it!

They had described her as a beauty of the sidelong glance—vultures were going to peck out those eyes as though plucking pearls from shells!

Oh! It was impossible even to think of it. What a dreadful death! What an unjust punishment!

Yet she stood motionless, bearing even that—is that not the wonder?

Her memories circled around her. She saw the king standing opposite, high on the palace. ‘One day<!-- source scan 34; printed page 34 --> at night, because I refused to submit to his desire and join him in a revel of pleasure, has he not today ordered me to dance in a revel of wounds?’ she said to herself. She did not worry about being pushed into the fire and burned there to ash. So many beauties—Cleopatra, who came to rewrite world history; sensual royals who bathed their bodies in streams of rosewater, wore splendour fragrant with sandalwood and feasted among beauties of dalliance—they too had all been caressed by tongues of fire and turned to ash! There was only one difference: the bodies of many among them were fed to the flames only after they had grown cold. For her, the ‘punishment of falling into embers’ had been imposed while the warmth in her blood had not yet cooled. Even entering fire alive was no wonder; nor was it anything new! The history of Rajput princesses sets out a list of women who bathed in fire.

So that living statue did not grieve at the thought of leaping into the fire and giving up her life. But they would not let her die even after she rolled in the flames, would they? They would drag her out at once, set her on her feet, sprinkle chilli powder upon the wounds, call her leaps of agony Gomedhagam's dance of delight, beat drum and rhythm, and the king and the others<!-- source scan 35; printed page 35 --> would burst into peals of laughter; alas—she could not even think of these things! When they threw her into the fire, more than half the clothes she wore—perhaps all of them—might burn away. When they pulled her out half-alive and ordered her to dance while throwing burning powder upon her, perhaps she might even have to stand naked—alas; they said that naked corpse would then be hung on a platform in the middle of the street for all to stare at; passers-by coming and going along the road—oh! How could she bear this torture! O flame of fire! The instant they throw me into your mouth, swallow me and digest me at once!—grind me up and consume me without leaving even one of my bones!—Gomedhagam lamented all this within herself.

“Even if I corrupted an ascetic—an ascetic! Let it be so; that is a crime. But who made the law that such a crime deserves so great a punishment?”—arrows of questions flew through her heart!

More fiercely than those arrows of questions, spears smeared with the poison called memories of pleasures past flew in rapid succession into the soft walls of her heart! Memories of pleasure indeed—yes; memories of the happy turn her life had taken after the arrival of that young ascetic!

<!-- source scan 36; printed page 36 -->

That first meeting—yes; the meeting between that young ascetic and her—she had not known that it would assume such a ‘cosmic form’. So many guests used to come to her house; she had entertained them. They would stay a day or two. They would enthusiastically preach about how to cut oneself free from worldly bonds. She would listen eagerly—and send them on their way with respect. “How much happiness there is in a simple life,” she would think to herself, swelling with contentment. What if she too were to set out with an inexhaustible bowl like Manimekalai? she would wonder. Manimekalai, the lamp of Madhavi's lineage, had also been circled in this way by Prince Udayakumaran. Fearing that, she fled. The woman who fled became Manimekalai, praised by the world. Why then should she too not become another Manimekalai? The thought grew day by day. Manimekalai, daughter of a queen of dance, became an epic heroine—was she herself not also a queen of dance? Could she too not become a jewel among the women of the land? It was in a way that blunted the sharpness of this question that her meeting with the young ascetic occurred!

As she welcomed every refugee, Gomedhagam welcomed and entertained him too. But she came to hold him in greater esteem than all the other ascetics. The reason: he<!-- source scan 37; printed page 37 --> was unlike the other ascetics she had seen, who were all roughly forty or fifty years old. He alone was a youth in the fullness of his season—the age when the heart melts at the sight of the moon—the age when even the soft touch of the breeze is too much to bear and one swoons—the young season that pours out a million thoughts at the sight of a pair of doves!

The stage of being overcome by pictures of the Ajanta paintings in books—arts in which statues shaped for embrace would melt him like wax set to flame!

If he merely heard someone sing, “Golden bee that tastes the fresh flower!” intoxication would rush to his head! The thought that he had renounced such surging youth and become an ascetic gave Gomedhagam even greater respect for him. She offered him varieties of fruit and asked him to refresh himself. He ate, slept for a little while, and the day passed.

The next morning, Gomedhagam spoke with the ascetic.

He delivered an erudite lecture on the question, “What is the state of renunciation?” Gomedhagam leapt with delight as though every word were sugar candy—such tender youth!—such sugarcane-sweet words!—Ah! Is this not a sage who understands the world!—she thought with admiration.

<!-- source scan 38; printed page 38 -->

The next day the ascetic preached on, “How is the world impermanent?”

“What you see today will not be here tomorrow! Life in this world is water, a bubble upon water! One foot of earth to stand on—at the end, six feet of earth on which to lie as a corpse—that alone belongs to man! No. Not even that. Where one man stands, another stands later—and then another. In the same way, even in the place where he spreads his final bed, after he has decayed into the soil, another man spreads his mat. And within all this: possession—bondage—grabbing—rolling about—threatening—oh, oh, foolish man—foolish man!”

Hearing the ascetic's conclusion, Gomedhagam became motionless. So moving was the teaching!

“Even preaching is an art! Not everyone can preach! So many people state the very same philosophy you have given, but in a dry way; it does not capture the heart! You preach in a manner that melts the heart,” Gomedhagam said, garlanding him with praise.

The next day the ascetic delivered a lecture on “What is art?” That day did not end with a mere lecture.

<!-- source scan 39; printed page 39 -->

Gomedhagam's dance, which had been stopped for many days, took place again. The reason was the ascetic's sermon about ‘art’!

“Art is a gift bestowed by God. That power does not arise in everyone born into this world! Gomedhagams cannot be born in every household! Only a few can be Gomedhagams. It is wrong to destroy, without benefit to anyone, the artistic talent placed in those few at the time of creation! So do not waste your gift. Dance; I shall watch! The satisfaction of my soul will bring satisfaction to the Lord who gave you your artistic gift.”

Gomedhagam danced. On the first day the ascetic remained silent and enjoyed it.

The next day, when she danced, he sang. The sermons continued as well.

Following the subject ‘the impermanence of the world’, one day the ascetic preached on ‘How should one live in that impermanent world?’

“The world will perish—that is true! It is true that tomorrow we will not be here!” But because of that, must we lose what is to be experienced today and suffer?”

The ascetic asked such a question. Gomedhagam turned the question back on him: “If so, why did you become an ascetic?”

<!-- source scan 40; printed page 40 -->

“I am not speaking for myself! I am arguing for the world”! the ascetic replied.

Sermons, questions, answers, dances, songs—these created a close relationship between Gomedhagam and the ascetic. The days too continued to move. Gomedhagam's affection, respect and humility toward the ascetic grew more and more. He decided to use that to accomplish the purpose for which he had come. All the preparations for it began to take place.

◆     ◆     ◆

The sermon would end—and Gomedhagam would offer a feast of art! By now, even the way the ascetic savoured it had become an art! With intoxicated eyes he watched every one of her movements closely. In order that she might gradually understand what lay in his heart, he displayed his emotions more and more openly.

For many days Gomedhagam did not understand him. Only an unstained affection for him kept growing within her! That too kept becoming known! The ascetic too came to know it.

He resolved that he must begin his work by making capital of the devotion and affection she placed in him.

<!-- source scan 41; printed page 41 -->

People who possess devotion and affection, when wronged by those they respect, may turn into frenzied enemies! Or else, thinking, “He is someone I respect; if I refuse, he will be hurt,” they may yield—such is the way of the world!

Gomedhagam became a victim of the second of these ways of the world.

One night—the sermon, dance and song had all ended. Because she had danced for a long time, Gomedhagam said, ‘My legs are aching.’ The ascetic treated it as a wonderful opportunity.

“I will massage your legs,” he said, rushing toward her.

Gomedhagam refused. “Oh no, that would be a great sin,” she said.

“It is an act pleasing to the Lord—among souls, what difference is there between man and woman? It is all the same clay—from it one may mould Pillaiyar; one may mould a monkey! What difference is there in the clay?” he said, and began pressing her legs. For a little while Gomedhagam remained in reverential fear—the ascetic had won.

<!-- source scan 42; printed page 42 -->

What could she do? He was a man she respected—she had known him for many days—the consideration that he must not be hurt—Gomedhagam had fallen victim to this second kind of worldly nature!

◆     ◆

After that, the sermons began to diminish.

The eyes of the villagers turned toward Gomedhagam's hut.

◆     ◆

Complaints piled up before the king. Some men possessed of ‘patience’, thinking, “This fish-eyed beauty did not come to us, but some ascetic got her!” took command of that army of complaints. “An ascetic—a very good man—has been corrupted by a courtesan named Gomedhagam; the two of them are ruining the whole village with their dance and song”—this was the manner in which they complained! The king heard it. He became eager to know who the fortunate ascetic was who had obtained her. He longed to go there in person, catch the ascetic and her red-handed, and ask, “What do you say now, prostitute! Will you submit to me?” Sending the complainants away, that very night he mounted his horse and set out for the village where Gomedhagam lived!

<!-- source scan 43; printed page 43 -->

Inside the hut—Gomedhagam and the ascetic! Gomedhagam was massaging the ascetic's legs. The ascetic reclined while running his fingers through her loosened black hair. Delight danced in both their eyes.

“Gomedhagam,” the ascetic called, sweetness flowing in his voice.

“Why?” she asked, love overflowing.

“You are a Goddess of Art! Noblemen are waiting to heap gold to the very skies if you merely glance at them! You gave all that up and took possession of me—how did that happen, Devi?”

“You are an ascetic! You had performed penance seeking to reach the lotus feet of the Lord. Why then, Swami, did such a man leave the Lord's lotus feet and one day take hold of my lotus feet?”

“Do you not know? The Lord's lotus feet have been grasped and grasped by thousands of devotees until they have become calloused! I am the first devotee to touch my Goddess of Art's flower-soft lotus feet!”

“Not merely the first; you are the last devotee too, Swami!”

“Goma...!”

<!-- source scan 44; printed page 44 -->

“Swami...!”

“I have broken your resolve—have I not?”

“No—it is my beauty that has broken your resolve!”

“You asked me for the path beyond worldly bonds—I myself......have plunged you into worldly bonds.”

“No, Swami! If this bond could remain ours forever—then!”

The king, who had been listening to this delightful conversation from behind the hut, looked inside through a small window. Gomedhagam and the ascetic were exploring what a ‘worldly bond’ meant. The king asked himself—“Who is that ascetic? Who is this ascetic?” He could not remain there. He mounted his horse and flew away—he seemed likely to faint and fall—somehow he reached the palace.

The next day came the inquiry—Gomedhagam was dragged in. She was accused of having corrupted an ascetic. The verdict was delivered at once. The fire pit waited for her!

<!-- source scan 45; printed page 45 -->

Do you have anything to say at last?” a brutal officer asked.

“No!” she said, shaking her head.

Two soldiers tied her to an iron bar. Lifting her with the bar, they threw her into the embers. While she was still being scorched they pulled her out. As specified in the verdict, chilli powder was sprinkled—Gomedhagam writhed—the drum and rhythms resounded -without opening her mouth she rolled on the ground and writhed like a worm—a little while in a deathly state—the naked dance the king had expected did not take place - she quickly became a corpse. The spirit of that wondrous beauty whose loveliness had flashed across every stage in Aazhi Nadu departed in agony! The body of that graceful woman who had moved hearts with the movements of her body now presented a terrible sight! According to the third plan of the verdict, her corpse was taken to the public platform for display!

People from across the land gathered to see Gomedhagam's funeral procession. The procession moved. Was her corpse not to hang for four days on the platform in the middle of the capital? The procession set out toward that platform. It drew near. The sea of people stared at the platform. There, before Gomedhagam, some other figure was already hanging.

<!-- source scan 46; printed page 46 -->

“Some ascetic has hanged himself on this platform,” said the people. Officials ran over and looked. “He is not an ascetic,” they said—in the garb of an ascetic, the prince's corpse was hanging there.

“When did the prince, who had set out on a journey abroad, become an ascetic? Alas! What is this strange thing?” the people cried.

There was a palm-leaf letter in the ascetic's lap. They took it to the king.

“Father! I am not Salim, the son of Akbar, who went on living even after Anarkali was killed—long live—your unjust status and your power!”

Vanakkam,

Inbasagaran.

The king stood like a tree. He had thought of separating Gomedhagam from the prince—both had indeed been separated—and he grieved!

The people now understood the reason for the cruelty of the punishment imposed upon Gomedhagam.
