# Will It Happen or Not?

In earlier days, when I spoke at weddings, I would sometimes tell a foreign short story at one wedding function or another. I would elaborate and polish that story and tell it in a way suited to our Tamil Nadu.

A husband and wife lived together in harmony. Poverty struck them.

<!-- source scan 40; printed page 38 -->

They struggled with no means of livelihood. One day the wife suggested to her husband that if he took the bull they had at home, sold it in the market, and used the money to open a small shop, they could keep the family going. He agreed and set off along the road to the market, driving the bull. On the way the bull became frightened and ran this way and that. A man driving a goat came along and said to him, “Why struggle with that rough bull? Give it to me—in return I'll give you my goat.” The husband, lacking in sense, gave him the bull and continued toward the market driving the goat.

A man came toward him carrying a hen. He deceived the husband by saying, “A hen can simply be carried in your hand.” The foolish husband agreed to that too, gave him the goat, took the hen, and set off again for the market.

On the way there was a biryani shop! The shopkeeper wanted the hen to cook that day. With a plan in mind, he spoke sweetly to the foolish husband and took the hen from him in exchange for a cup of tea.

While the foolish husband was drinking the tea, his next-door neighbour, who had been following him, looked at him

<!-- source scan 41; printed page 39 -->

and said, “You fool! I've been watching you all along. You gave away the bull and took a goat; gave away the goat and took a hen; and now you've given away the hen and are having tea in return. If your wife learns all this, she'll either run away from you or beat you and drive you out.”

The foolish husband said, “Nothing like that will happen.” “It will,” said the neighbour. The two made a wager over whether it would happen or not. If it happened, the foolish husband had to sign over his house to the neighbour. If it did not happen, the neighbour had to give his small shop to the foolish husband. Having made this wager, the two returned home.

The neighbour told the fool's wife all the foolish things her husband had done and mocked him: “Your husband went to the market with a bull and has come back with a cup of tea.” But the fool's wife looked at her husband and affectionately asked, “At least did you have enough of that tea to fill your stomach?”

The neighbour was utterly shocked. He had lost the wager and signed over the small shop.

The next day he saw the foolish husband and asked, “What, man? Is your wife even more foolish than you?” The foolish husband replied, “Nothing of the sort. However much disappointment or anger she may have toward me, she will not show it in front of others. I am the same way. It was with that confidence that I made the wager with you.”

This story is an example of why family problems should not be magnified in front of others.
