## “The Old Man's Dream”

*(An imagined narrative)*

... Hey; will you give it? Must I kick you?

— Kick me, then—let's see!

... You—may you go to ruin; give it and be done with it, boy.

— If this curse were going to come true, Lord Krishna would have died long ago!

... Is all this some game?

— Yes: Duplicate Krishna Leela.

In the Puranas! Krishna Leela is one episode. Krishna, moving amid the revelry of the Gopika women; the **Vastrabharana scene** from that supremely holy tale in which he stole the clothes of maidens who were bathing and climbed a tree—pictures of it are engraved in homes! Lenin, having seen this scene in the bhajan hall, staged a Kali-Yuga Krishna Leela of his own. One day, by the pond! With the resolve that many women who regarded Krishna himself as god, and his obscene antics themselves as divine plays, would gather there, Lenin began this revolution.

**Not in Russia!—in Tamil Nadu!!**

Lenin is the dear son of Vipulanandar. Whenever Vipulanandar and Mallika became absorbed in talk of reform, Lenin's ears too would incline in that direction.

<!-- source scan 8; printed page 4 -->

“Contradiction from start to finish.”

“I cannot even tell what comes first and what comes after.”

“One history in Valmiki's Ramayana.”

“One concoction in Kamba Ramayanam.”

“The Periya Puranam in one fashion.”

“The Bhagavatam its opposite.”

“Even in an age when the truth that the world is round is known, people still live believing the tale that the earth was rolled up like a mat!”

“Even the **Puranarthika Iyer** who teaches geography lessons at school keeps that very tale as a convenience for life.”

“Tamils will not understand any of this so long as they have lent away their self-respect!”

“Even for men and women to come together, the Aryan's favour is needed, isn't it?”

“Why on earth must these falsehoods lie about in Puranic form?”

“All of it was done to make life overflow with prosperity!”

“How many avenues, just so one section may eat and make merry? God, religion, shastra, custom, fate, stories of every kind; good heavens....!”

“When the physician, after setting up all these devices perversely in life, sings, ‘Do not trust life’—that is when laughter makes the belly burst.”

<!-- source scan 9; printed page 5 -->

“‘False! An air-filled bag! A bubble! Of use to no one!’—the homeland of these words is advancing in self-defence. Is that all, Mallika! Those of a fallen disposition do not understand the meaning of the life that stretches open before them. Those who wander about lamenting, ‘False, false’—if the bee swarming over a flower thought the flower false, would it hover through the flower garden? Youth that scatters and strings together sweet pearls surely takes the mother to be real. Does a woman live without trusting the husband who gives her caresses?”

“Will the ochre-robed folk who wander about saying, ‘This body itself is false!’ answer these questions?”

“Whatever you ask, fate is the mantra they seize upon.”

“Fate, fate! This band, boasting that simplicity and honesty mean poverty, has reached the highest pitch! The handrail of the staircase called religion! A great fortress that protects one's livelihood!”

“Open the door of your eyelids—the lightning eyes flash forth! A dawn of reason that makes one blush! Will you not see it, Mallika!”

“Ah, Vaidika orthodoxy, keep your distance! Even my empty talk is only a way of passing the time, my love!”

While Vipulanandar and Mallika were entwined in this delightful conversation—

<!-- source scan 10; printed page 6 -->

Lenin would be thinking about the future. Truly, the name Lenin suited him. A volcano of revolution! A surging sea! A leaping bull! A throbbing warrior! Lenin was the very mould of these descriptions!

Many years earlier in Russia, Lenin had confronted on the stage of death the politics of the wicked Tsar—politics that relished Rasputin's cruelty, ruled through injustice, kissed capitalist arrogance, seated religious folly upon the bridal couch, and lived treating the worker's thought as contemptible—and had triumphed. There was therefore nothing surprising in lovers of reform giving their own child Lenin's name! This revolutionary son, born to people of new ideas, too became known as Lenin.

One day Lenin went to the bank of the pond, stole the clothes of Brahmin women who were bathing, and climbed a tree; when they asked for their saris, he replied, “Krishna Leela.” It gave Mallika no small delight. Lenin's cheeks swelled with kisses! For Vipulanandar and Mallika, the love-talk of that day was all about his thought-provoking prank! Every act dripping with relish was Lenin's property! He would chase a pig running along the street into the house of a Vaishnavite devotee. As the devotee and his wife began to scream, he would start with, “O Dweller of Vaikuntha!” and expound the greatness of the Varaha avatar; and when Garuda was seen—

<!-- source scan 11; printed page 7 -->

you touch your eyes in reverence, don't you! “Why do you hate an incarnation of Vishnu?” he would ask, and laugh merrily. If any devotee of Siva happened to walk along the street, that was enough: “Are you not Iyarpagai Nayanar?” he would ask with great humility. Lenin's little revolution began to spread through his pranks like medicine mixed into sweetness; not only that, it also caught a few theist devotees in a fit of anger. The speed of youth went on without check or obstruction. Neither his brisk deeds nor his energetic efforts ever wearied Vipulanandar and Mallika.

They grew proud, thinking again and again of the fruit of their love and of how that fruit had come forth into the world. How many barriers society had heaped up, one upon another, against their living as lovers! Alas, even the thought of the sufferings they endured makes the heart burn. Once, when Mallika called her lover, “Vipula,” and was showering words of affection upon him, Mirasudar Markandeya Sastri saw them. As a consequence, Vipulanandar was accused of theft and sent to prison; even today the sight of it makes tears spill suddenly from Mallika's eyes. Fifteen years earlier, Mallika was the daughter of a poor old cart-driver. Mallika's father was a cart-driver on Markandeya Sastri's estate. When Mallika was a child, she had battled hunger. Mallika—

<!-- source scan 12; printed page 8 -->

—for the first three months after her birth, remained under her mother's nourishment. Mallika's mother did not know that death would flash out and pounce. In this country, where poverty and patience had been made the ideal of life; where a deceitful heart and poisonous policy alone had been made life's discipline; where the deception that the purpose of taking bodily form was merely to labour and, even without food, to sleep had been officially installed, through the mouth of God, as the sermon on life's illusion—what, then, befell Mallika's father? Are these explanations of fate?

“Rajam! Where is Mallika?” Veerasami would ask as he came in. How eagerness and anger managed to enter that question together, no one could say. In a weak voice Rajam would answer, “The child is sleeping.” There would be one **padi** of rice tied in a cloth. He would untie it. That rice had to last until the following night. Rajam would put the rice on to cook and go to the stove. She did not have the strength even to speak a single word to the husband who had just returned. She had grown that thin. Do you know what thoughts would appear and disappear in Veerasami's heart as he returned home after driving the cart all day and doing every kind of work at the Mirasudar's house?

He would be afraid even to think of his wife Rajam's body having become like a splinter under the cruelty of hunger, and of the baby growing thin from crying and crying because there was no milk. Mallika's little smile would scatter all that anguish in an instant.

<!-- source scan 13; printed page 9 -->

“Kaliamma, save my family”—Veerasami and Rajam would keep muttering that prayer.

Everywhere in the village, vomiting and diarrhoea began to spread. Though **karagam** and **kapparai** rites were celebrated for Kaliamman amid drumbeats and clamour, Rajam too fell victim to the sickness. Veerasami prayed to Kali.

Rajam looked and looked at Mallika and wept. The child cried because there was no milk. What else did the child know? It may have been midnight. “Ich! Ich!”—two kisses! This alone was what Rajam had kept in reserve for Mallika. With that, Rajam closed her eyes.

Somehow, Veerasami raised Mallika. Beauty and affection embraced Mallika, and she grew along with them. She had grown enough for Markandeyar to feel a desire to meet Mallika upon the bed. Markandeyar possessed not merely the title of Mirasudar; the embrace of the government had also crowned him with the title **Rao Bahadur**. And was that all? He was the editor of a weekly journal called **Ozhukkam**. Mallika should become a novelty of the bedchamber, an ornament of sensual pleasure, a beauty of dalliance, a maiden who adorned the bed, a jasmine blossom for Markandeyar. This was the foothill of his secret desire! The kiss bestowed by the moon—

<!-- source scan 14; printed page 10 -->

—at the hour when it shakes the world, Markandeyar's mind would break down and then settle back into order. What, then, was the conclusion of his thought?

“My dear! Mallika! You loved Vipulanandar—was that right? These arms that now embrace me, a man superior in wealth and position, once had a time when they desired a miserable, luckless fellow and lived for him, did they not? Ah! The pleasure you give is a graveyard to the thought that you are a cart-driver's daughter! When the feast of your cooing words makes me leap toward you crying, ‘Refuge! Refuge!’, I see the barrier called low caste being broken. Is not yours the smile of a garden of delight shivering in the breeze? Even the burst spathe and the jasmine whose knot has shown would blush! O beauty of gait! My forehead? Sit down! Here—half an anna, isn't it! Do you want an apple? Why, I shall eat these grapes. Enough? All right! Here, drink the milk. How tasty—did you taste it? Specially for you! Come... with... your hand... feed me! Ah, ah! How many days have I done penance for this bliss! — —

God's grace! Everything is Siva's doing! Is there any limit to all his divine play? Mallika! My love! I am your slave!”

Such ravings kept colliding in Markandeyar's mind as he lay upstairs, rolling about amid a dull cough. This was old Markandeyar's dream! In truth, at that very moment, Mallika—

<!-- source scan 15; printed page 11 -->

if she had actually been there.......... would that have happened with her? Mallika would often tell Vipulan about Markandeyar's base intention. While Vipulanandar and Mallika were in the flower garden, **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 15 / printed page 11: one word is worn and cannot be read with source-faithful confidence]**, Markandeyar entered.

The sight of Vipulanandar having secured Mallika permanently as the one dear to his heart tormented Markandeyar's mind. Its consequence ended in suffering.

Preparations for the **Ekabhishekam** at the Vallabha Ganapati temple were proceeding rapidly. A committee bearing this name was formed, and Markandeya Sastri became its chairman. Of the committee members, three were Brahmins! One was a Mudaliar! The other was a Reddiar! The Vallabha Ganapati shrine was very ancient. It had been built by some king of the Chola dynasty. The temple had a **sthala-varalaru**: the Chola king, it was said, had been childless; some **mahan** **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 15 / printed page 11: a circular library stamp physically covers several printed words]** had advised that if he built a temple for Pillaiyar a child would be born; the king believed it; after this was heard in the royal household and the festival took place, a temple was established for Pillaiyar and worship performed; afterward, a child was born to him.

Even while the income and expenditure for the kumbabhishekam were under discussion, the three Iyers prepared a plan. Aryam knows very well, does it not, that in temples built by kings of old, beneath the stone deity—

<!-- source scan 16; printed page 12 -->

—they would pour heaps of precious stones? So Markandeyar and the other two joined together and, one night, toppled Vallabha Ganapati. After dividing among themselves the profit that came of it, the news spread that Vallabha Ganapati was lying flat on his back. The goddess of bribery began her play. Is it not a truth known to the country that so many murders and robberies bare their teeth before money! The court's verdict was that Vipulanandar himself had toppled Vinayaka! Money scattered and ran here and there, and prison was what Vipulan got! Vipulan lay in prison for a year! His youthful force had already been a hurricane; it would have shattered Markandeyar. Mallika stood in the way.

The Mirasudar imagined that the moat called Vipulan, which stood around the mansion called Mallika, had been filled in. He thought the guards of that mansion-fort had fallen dead. He exulted that the flower, having lost the layers of its petals, was letting its honey run. The conclusion that the bees had flown away from the honeycomb set his brain aflame. And so, one night, the Mirasudar sent his men with orders to carry Mallika away. Seven men set out as the consequence of that command. Mallika woke when she was attacked—but what use was waking? “A deer trapped in the middle with hunters surrounding it on four sides—a fish cast upon the shore: that was Mallika's state! Could she cry out, ‘Kali! Kooli!’? And would crying out be of any use?”

<!-- source scan 17; printed page 13 -->

Would she be the one to scream? If seven bulls oppose you, even a lion would be troubled! Even a pouncing tiger, seeing thousands of goats, would hesitate a little. How many ranks in succession can even a mighty warrior—one who, raising his sword, could wage battle until the fish of the sky fall—destroy? Is this an age of wonders in which, the instant one cries, “O Parameswara!”, a radiance as high as the sky rises and, Parvati **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 17 / printed page 13: the short phrase immediately following `பார்வதியை` is worn/indistinct in the supplied copy]**, the Lord comes proclaiming protection! It is not. And so Mallika was dragged outside. Her hands and feet were tied, and with her mouth stopped as well, Mallika writhed and wilted.

At that moment, with the shout, “Hey, you scoundrel fellows!”, Mallika's father entered, and the grip of the ruffians who had been dragging Mallika loosened. Once upon a time, Veerasami had truly been Veerasami. That was the age when he lived a family life with Rajam! Today Veerasami was an old man! The question, “Is Mallika in danger?”, produced in him a feeling like a thousand spears entering his chest. That was enough! His sunken eyes filled with the colour of blood; his bent back was seen to straighten. His slackened sinews tightened—the thick beard that had hung downward rose proudly erect. The old man's attack began. Mallika was freed. Cries of “Ayyo! Ayyo!” brought the whole village together. When the villagers entered and looked, the old man's body was—

<!-- source scan 18; printed page 14 -->

floating in a stream of blood. The flow of tears from her eyes ran so heavily that it seemed enough to alter the redness of the blood, as Mallika rolled upon her father. Apart from crying, “Appa! Appa!”, Mallika remained unable to grasp anything; the people stood there in anguish. Veerasami lay like a tiger that had fought a warrior and fallen, its whole body pierced by spears. Like a lion struck by a bullet opening its mouth in the state of death, the old man began to say something.

“Mallika!”

“Appa, are you fainting?”

“No fainting—this is the state of death! Do not rely on me any longer. Take Vipulan as your life-companion, as you wished.”

“Ayyo, Appa! Who is there for me? They have made me an orphan!”

“Mallika! Money is drinking my life. Would a valiant Tamil lose his honour? That is why I embrace death and rejoice.”

As the old man's life parted from his body, this conversation was all it bestowed upon Mallika. Mallika lived with her lover in prison and her father in the grave. Could that have been a life for her! If, when a dry leaf falls into a river, even the creeper that served as its hold were to snap—if, when a ship has capsized and one struggles to swim through the sea and reach the shore, the legs grow weary—

<!-- source scan 19; printed page 15 -->

Mallika had come to such a state! Her suffering-filled life had been fashioned with turmoil and confusion as its mound, disappointment and yearning as its boundary line! His daughter's welfare was his own welfare—the desire that arose in her should be fulfilled through his own effort. Her life free from torment alone would be joy reaching the sky for him. Her tear-traces, and the turning pages of a sweet life—this was the joy old age would behold! This was the dream the old man had been dreaming. But his thought mingled with the earth. Markandeyar did not venture outside for many days. Mallika, however, had gone out into the world, and six months passed.

“With the gentle breeze beating the drum, the music of the sweet wind melting and pouring delight, the moon granting repose......” As words like these were being piled one upon another—

“Mallika! In those days, if we had sat upon the marble platform in the garden, would the world have failed to roar at the sight of you?”

As he asked this, a man touched Mallika. Who was that astonishing man who had recognized her? The sight of Mallika's face—once offering a smile joined with new thought—now so changed, was unbearable. And that was not all. How he recognized that worn figure, reclining on the raised platform of a choultry, as Mallika, no one knows.

<!-- source scan 20; printed page 16 -->

“Vipula! Is that you? You've gone and become a holy man!”

“No, Mallika. The marks of prison life.”

Yes. Vipulanandar's young beard had drawn that question from Mallika. She had sacrificed her father to Markandeyar's lust, begged from house to house to fill her stomach, waited for her lover's return, and lain in a choultry treating moonlight as poison; for her, the unexpected meeting with Vipulan bestowed a life of love. Was theirs merely a sham love? Had it not dawned at the summit of steadfastness? Vipulan, after completing his prison term, and Mallika had Lenin as the sign of the book of life. Lenin had crossed twelve years and was approaching his thirteenth. On the fortieth rung of life's ladder, Vipulanandar, a fire of revolution, sat with Mallika on the bank of the river of love, speaking words of reason and tasting the red sugarcane of reform that spurted delight. It was then that Lenin's youthful pranks were occurring with enough force to strike the heart. It was then, too, that one of his rationalist plays—the Vastrabharana drama at the pond—took place.

Before Mallika could say that Lenin had done this prank at the pond, her lips parted and her pearl-like teeth blossomed. Copper plates lay scattered—

—across the red sky, which at that moment offered as a gift a little hand of vermilion hue... Yes! The moment when Vipulanandar gathered Mallika into an embrace... the breeze that sang a Sindhu moved softly along.

Love gave—a life of beauty! Life gave—Lenin! Markandeya's attempt! The echo of thought! This memory **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 21 / printed page 17: a short phrase is worn/indistinct and cannot be read with source-faithful confidence]** melted and poured out the heart. In the music-pouring breeze, Vipulanandar's beard moved; quietly!

“Everywhere one looks, the verse of liberation! Everywhere, the conch-blast of equal justice! A history that joined the Tamil language with politics and lived to impel the world—with Dravidian rights—with property—with truth—with inner uprising! A Tamil, for calling an Iyer ‘Swami’ without self-respect, sentenced to six months' rigorous imprisonment! One who called himself a sadhu and practised deceit, imprisoned until death! A newspaper editor, for writing that titles and offices were our programme, called a fool in the people's assembly! Because the desire to see the poor grew excessive, **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 21 / printed page 17: a short reading is worn/indistinct]** for the Tamil! A historian named **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 21 / printed page 17: the name/short reading is worn/indistinct]**, in a book called *The Recovery of Dravidam*, had recorded that the imaginary bogeys called caste, religion and gods had once not existed even in Tamil Nadu. **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 21 / printed page 17: a short reading is worn/indistinct]**! Prison! The battlefield sacrifice of Thalamuthu Natarasan! The Bharata-land of the women who went to prison to protect Tamil! To guard honour—

—the uprising carried out by men! Sir Selvam, hidden by the Oman Sea! Thyagar! Nair! Panagal! Histories such as these became illuminating lights. “We lived—to be kissed by the sword. We bowed to the Aryans—we did not fall utterly—there! Our flag of liberation!” a lover told his beloved, and received a thousand kisses as the reward for those words. “Today is the day Dravidam was recovered! The Dravidian land is a day for Dravidians! Dravidian Festival Day!”—young men go along the street sounding their trumpets!

Vipulanandar and Mallika saw this dream in their minds! In the Dravidian realm, Lenin is a valiant citizen! This is the summit of their dream! As a memorial to the dream, more young comrades like Lenin are still needed!

Ramasami Periyar too sees this very dream. Of the Dravidam that is going to live, **[SOURCE BLOCKED — scan 22 / printed page 18: a large circular library stamp physically obscures the final story phrase]**; that is the old man's dream.
