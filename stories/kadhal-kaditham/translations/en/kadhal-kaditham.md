It is impossible even to say where we are. Nor can we say when our camp will be shifted elsewhere. We cannot say when we will die, or when we will escape death. Nor can the nature of the enemy’s attack be known with certainty. Minute by minute, news keeps falling into our ears that the rain of victory is pouring on the side of the frenzied Hitler and Mussolini. If we go by sea, they catch us with mines. If we fly, they shoot us down. If we walk, destruction advances all around us. In circumstances like these—if we leave the tent, we must tie plants and creepers over our heads and walk—no, no; move. Only then can we deceive the enemy aircraft. The enemy will go away thinking that some tree, plant or creeper is moving. If they realize it is a man—and moreover a man in a military cap belonging to the Allied forces—that is enough; bombs will rain from the aircraft. We must be scattered into dust! Even at such a terrifying time, when every second was a struggle with death, I did not fail to write to her. After the time spent training in the military camp, all the remain—

<!-- source scan 162; printed page 153 -->

—ing time passed joyfully in writing letters to my beloved. That day I was writing a letter. I did not even notice my friend Balan watching it from behind me. Balan had joined the army from Tanjore district in Tamil Nadu. He was a close friend of mine. In fact, he was the teacher who taught me Tamil. He learned Hindi from me. We were not merely teachers to one another; we were comrades bound by uzhaval-anbu too. I finished the letter, posted it, and returned. Balan looked at me mockingly. “What, Bala?” I asked. Balan began.

“To my beloved jewel Sudesha, Sundar Babu’s kisses of desire. Night and day, without cease, I pine thinking again and again of your beautiful face. When enemy aircraft circle overhead, when the danger siren sounds that peril is coming toward our camp, people hearing thunder cry ‘Arjuna—Arjuna’, do they not? In the same way, when I hear the sound of the enemy, I murmur ‘Sudesh—Sudesh’. That love-mantra alone, darling, is the nectar that gives me comfort. When will this wicked war end; golden beauty; when will I be able to run to you and embrace you? I do not know, Sudesh! Every day I write you a letter; why can you not write me one every day in reply? Do you think it will become tiresome? My honey! Earlier you wrote one letter every day; you ordered only me, ‘Write every day.’ Taking your command upon my head, I keep writing. Why have you alone kept silent for a whole month without even one letter a day? I do not know—Sudesh—I am withering away! I see you in dreams and become distraught. What anger do you have, my dear, when you are forever seated on the throne of my heart as the golden idol worthy of my worship? My life! At least answer this and grant me alms of life!

Apart from the one fault that I did not return quickly and marry you, what other wrong have I done? I shall return soon as a victorious soldier! Reply—reply.

Slave to love,

**Sundar Babu”**

Balan recited from memory, word for word, the letter I had written to her. Only then did I understand that he had hidden behind me and read the whole letter!

Whether a person is involved in affairs of love, writing a love letter, or casting his eyes toward his beloved, he never seems to develop the fear of wondering who might be standing nearby watching him! Was it said for nothing that love has no eyes? In my case it ended by becoming literally true. I do not mean only that Balan read the letter without my knowing; the very foundation of my mansion of love turned out to be a false foundation laid by blind masons! Balan had told me even then: do not write too often; it will turn stale! I wrote until my hand ached. I believed she would read it with sweetness of thought. But hope was ruined. The war that shook the world had ended—but the war that shook my heart had not ended.

A thousand letters. No reply to them. None of Balan’s consolations entered my ears. “Who gets war news correctly, anyway? Who knows how the frenzy of war may have affected your beloved’s family, living in some corner of India? Do not become needlessly angry with Sudesha! You must go in person and learn the true situation,” Balan said often. I stayed alive through his consolation. As though what he said were true, all the last letters I wrote to her began to return—

<!-- source scan 164; printed page 155 -->

—to me. I believed that my queen of love and her family had been affected by the war. I regretted within myself that I had weighed Sudesha’s affection and love too lightly. The war ended; all of us were happily sent home. Taking my friend Balan with me, I set out to learn what had happened to Sudesha. Sudesha’s house was locked. I asked the neighbours. They told me—she had got married!

My mind became a void! I felt faint! That was all; I collapsed! When I regained consciousness and rose, Balan was beside me. I asked him:

“Did you ask anyone who Sudesha’s husband is?”

“I asked and learned the details, Sundar! You are the one who found Sudesha a husband!” said Balan.

“Me?”

“Not you—the letters you wrote!”

“The letters.........?”

“Yes—you remember Sudesha wrote asking you to send one letter every day, don’t you..........?”

“Yes.”

“Do you know why?”

“Why?”

“To admire the beauty of the postman who brought the letters!”

“What?”

“Yes, Sundar! This is the truth the neighbours told me! Meeting every day that postman in whom beauty and youth were joined, Sudesha forgot her state; you so often—

<!-- source scan 165; printed page 156 -->

—speak about a woman’s path of dharma, don’t you? She fell from that path too. That was all; the postman and Sudesha were married!”

After Balan told me that news, it seemed to me that the world war had still not ended—it still seems so. Balan stayed with me for two days. Then he too left, saying his ‘aunt’s daughter’ had sent an urgent summons. What am I to do? I, who wrote the letter, could not receive even the good fortune gained by the man who carried my letter! I never knew there could be such a danger in writing too many letters! Would it not have been better if those German bombs had fallen on my head then itself?
