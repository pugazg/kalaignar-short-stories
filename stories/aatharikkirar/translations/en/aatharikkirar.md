His name was Punyakodi. There was hardly any public activity in which he did not involve himself. Even if he failed to win worldwide fame, even if he did not acquire an all-India name, even if no state arose in which everyone throughout Tamilagam knew him, in his own town there would always be many people gathered around him. News would arise that the municipal sanitation workers were going on strike. Before the news itself could get going, he would be off: “Sanitation workers! Get ready. We shall shed blood. We shall drive out the cheats!” he would thunder like a drum.

Even the leaders of those workers could not roar like that; he would sound the conch with such splendour. Until the agitation was completely dead and sent to the cremation ground, he would not sleep. Then he himself would produce several explanations for the defeat and, placing a finger against his nose, begin thinking about what struggle to start next. Some of the concessions the sanitation workers had enjoyed until then would even be reduced because of Punyakodi’s intervention.

Only then, he would offer a new explanation, would their anger swell more fiercely. And while garlands fell on him at public meetings, he would calculate whether they contained roses and, if so, for how many days they might be used to make coffee.

One day, suddenly, the municipality would announce an annadanam for a thousand Brahmins.

“Yes, of course it must be done. Only when the hearts of the Brahmins are cooled will the heart of God be cooled,” he would say, supplying a vyaakhyaanam for that too.

In the Brahmin feast, the service rendered by Punyakodi would stand foremost. Even the crowd who ate until their bellies bulged and belched would praise Punyakodi more than they praised the municipal chairman.

The municipal chairman, Vellaiyappa Pillai, though not quite as white-hearted as his name might suggest, was not excessively bad. He did have some concern for the town’s progress. He had taken on hardships, melted away a portion of his heap of wealth, bound himself by a vow to become municipal chairman somehow, and succeeded. He had affection and attachment toward Punyakodi. The public support that Punyakodi had acquired was also one reason Chairman Vellaiyappa Pillai felt affection for him. At the same time, Vellaiyappa could not recognize how cleverly Punyakodi diverted toward himself all the fame that ought to have gone to Vellaiyappa. Even if he had recognized it, he was not one to worry. All he wanted was high office. And the only office he considered high was that of municipal chairman. So he did not care on whose side the public stood or where fame accumulated. At election time, he thought, it was enough to get the public’s agent into his pocket. Was that so difficult? Not while the public-welfare man Punyakodi, who would curl up and fall when struck with money, stood beside him!

In private, Punyakodi moved as the servant of men of status; in public, as a worker for the common good.

“Ah, what a good man! When the poor suffer, he too suffers! Isn’t he truly the poor man’s partner?” praised the residents of the huts.

A flood would rise in the river—a battalion of people would gather to build the embankment. The labourers would be all sorts of people! The municipality would spend the money. There our Punyakodi would stand like a commander and raise the voice of authority. Without his body withering—without shedding sweat—he would easily carry away the title, ‘Punyakodi, the leader who stopped the river flood.’

A beautiful house—an excellent life—public service whose polish never faded—a life so cunning that its showiness never became obvious—thus did Punyakodi’s wheel keep rolling.

A good resolution was passed in the municipality. Because the election was approaching, Chairman Vellaiyappa, who was in office, had reached a stage where he needed to attract the public with some useful resolutions.

Every street should be tarred. The streets should be laid out in proper order. The work must begin immediately. A resolution to this effect was passed.

Next, an urgent resolution was introduced that the municipal building should be renovated.

Until the renovation was complete, a problem arose: where should the municipal office function? At once public servant Punyakodi stepped forward. Beating the drum about his generosity, he declared that for the people’s welfare he would give one of his houses temporarily to the municipality free of rent.

The whole town spoke in praise of his generous nature!

Work on the municipal building began. The office functioned from Punyakodi’s house.

The new building’s opening ceremony was held. At the ceremony, the municipal authorities, the presiding dignitary and the speakers—all of them—praised the magnanimous Punyakodi who had so freely given his house as the temporary office—

Thus they praised ‘the generous Punyakodi who gave’ it. In the public arena Punyakodi had acquired a fine name. Now what he needed was money, was it not? That was Punyakodi’s ideal, after all. He began the effort toward it. He sent a secret petition to the municipality. In it he wrote:

“Without even attending to my own work, without even looking to my own comforts, and without caring that many opposed me, I have until now given my house for use as the municipal office. To restore the damage that has thereby occurred to my house, ten thousand rupees is required. I request that this ten thousand rupees be paid to me as compensation.”

Before that petition could reach the municipal chairman’s attention, Chairman Vellaiyappa had to immerse himself in election work. The contest was fierce. Money flowed like water. The result turned pitiable. Vellaiyappa could not even become a councillor!

Punyakodi’s only sorrow was that the ten thousand rupees he was supposed to receive had now slipped away.

He sighed deeply that only the name ‘public servant’ was left to him.

A notable change took place in the municipality. Vellaiyappa’s dominance was completely wiped out, and a new party then famous in the town—the Yellow Party—came to office. The leader of that party was **Raja Nilaiyaththar**, who became municipal chairman. He and our Punyakodi had no unity between them. Had Punyakodi known beforehand that elections would come so soon and Raja Nilaiyaththar would win, he would have joined his side and performed public service there. Even now, he thought, nothing had completely sunk—

<!-- source scan 106; printed page 97 -->

—let us try one move. Hitching up his dhoti, Punyakodi set out.

He sent his old petition to the new municipal chairman and asked for the ten thousand. The new chairman replied, “All that is old business—ask Vellaiyappa himself.”

Like the tale of the ammammi whose mutton was stolen, Punyakodi lamented only within his heart. If he said it aloud, the public would laugh: “He gave the place free and now asks this many rupees for it!”

“Agitation! Agitation!” arose the cry. It was a storm created by Punyakodi. But all those storms became pusvaanam. He kept gathering public influence by speaking abusively and contemptuously of the new chairman. As a result Raja Nilaiyaththar filed a case against Punyakodi. The court ruled that Punyakodi had caused loss of reputation to the municipality and must pay ten thousand rupees as damages.

Punyakodi’s stomach burned. He thundered from public platforms. The public collected donations. The public themselves gave the ten thousand rupees. Punyakodi paid the damages.

The lion of public service, Punyakodi, moved about swelling with pride—treading upon the hearts of the public!

Another municipal election came. In this election Punyakodi fiercely opposed Raja Nilaiyaththar. Raja Nilaiyaththar alone was defeated. Many Yellow Party members won. As a result only the chairmanship changed; the municipality itself remained with the opposing side. The man who became chairman this time was regarded by everyone as extremely cruel, a man who would bring ruin upon the town. People came to Punyakodi and lamented. “After struggling this much, in the end that villain—

<!-- source scan 107; printed page 98 -->

—has become municipal thalaivanuga!” they cried. Punyakodi too shed tears.

Several months rolled by. The new municipal chairman’s excesses became unbearable. The public turned into rumbling volcanoes. Then Punyakodi advertised that he would speak on a platform about the new chairman. People gathered. First Punyakodi’s secretary spoke. He attacked the new chairman fiercely. The crowd rejoiced. Then Punyakodi rose. The public, brimming with eagerness, waited for his speech.

Punyakodi spoke. “Friends! I support the new chairman,” he said, and stepped down from the platform.

The public were struck speechless.

“The secretary roars; Punyakodi speaks like this. What a wonder!” the town marvelled.

“Perhaps they have made a plan: ‘You attack, I will support,’” said some.

“Something must have been discussed between the new chairman and Punyakodi,” said others.

“It is all for the good,” said Punyakodi’s hereditary devotees.

Poor people; none of them knew that the new chairman had considered Punyakodi’s resubmitted old petition and given him ten thousand rupees to repair the house—or that he had returned to Punyakodi the ten thousand rupees previously collected from him as municipal damages. Somehow everything had been hidden. **Punyakodi continues to move about as though he were a servant of the public;** this alone his secretary says.
