No comrade of the movement can so easily forget the cruelties of the Emergency period. If another movement had faced the cruelties unleashed against the DMK and the outrages committed at that time, that movement itself might have disappeared.

Five hundred of the movement's commanders and front-line members languished in prison as MISA detainees—there was a ban on writing, a ban on speaking, and even a ban on publishing truthful news, while the restrictions of censorship reigned supreme.

Standing all alone—with nothing but the point of his pen—Kalaignar has the distinction of having rescued this movement from destruction and lifted it back to its feet.

<!-- source scan 48; printed page 46 -->

To explain the events of the country and the wrongful acts of the rulers to the movement's comrades, while at the same time getting the writing published without falling into the grip of the censorship officers, every letter Kalaignar wrote in those days became a treasury of ideas that time cannot erase. No one can deny that truth.

Even after the Emergency ended, while the effects it had created still remained, on 24 June 1977 Kalaignar wrote a letter in *Murasoli* under the title “Butter Melts in the Sun.” We are proud to reproduce that letter here.

They say a single grain of cooked rice is enough to test a whole pot. Here is that letter, standing as unshakable evidence of the power of the writing with which Kalaignar protected the movement by writing letters:

“Udanpirappe,

It has been so many days since I wrote a literary letter, hasn't it? Realizing that you feel that absence, I write today.

During the Emergency, what a great support ‘literature’ was to us! After making the censorship officers suffer, even the words that remained after all they had cut away gave you pleasure and stirred you to rise—you would tell me so whenever you saw me! In your letters, you would encourage me!

Yes! If sugarcane is cut into many pieces—even if you eat just one piece, its taste

<!-- source scan 49; printed page 47 -->

does not change. Without understanding that, the censorship officers cut the literary letters I wrote into piece after piece. But you declared, ‘Cutting sugarcane does not turn it into something bitter,’ and enjoyed its taste!

I took the story-summary of a Sanskrit work called ‘Yasodhara Kaviyam,’ shaped it in writing and made it into a letter—for you! Do you know why I wrote it? When fine Tamil words lie heaped like bunches of fruit, some abandon them, search out obscene words and perform a ritual of slander against us. The purpose of that letter was to explain their nature! To speak about the conduct of such degraded people, I compared them with a queen who appears in ‘Yasodhara Kaviyam.’

She was beautiful! Supremely beautiful! ‘Amirthamathi’ was not merely her name; the king who married her drank her in as though she herself were nectar and rejoiced. He too was no less handsome than she. When she buried her face in that virile man's broad chest, it looked like the moon appearing in the blue sky. On his broad shoulders she could cling like a little parrot. He worshipped her as the goddess of his love—praised her—at the topmost floor of the cloud-touching palace terrace, on the Amsa Thalika couch,

<!-- source scan 50; printed page 48 -->

yet when he lay there with her, the feeling that should have been present was not there. “My delight,” he would coo. She expected delight somewhere else. He would touch her! She would not yield. Her eyes circled the sky, looking far away! “Breath of my life,” he would say as he embraced her. With a deep sigh she would roll over and lie facing the other way! “You who are full of life!” he would say, caressing her. Her heart would pound—but not thinking of him! Thinking of another man!

The queen of the land—mistress of the mansions—the king's crowned queen—companion of the bearer of the victorious parasol—the great queen who sat with the king in the royal hall and heard the grievances of the people!

At every moment there were countless servants ready to run and ask what command she had given! Great warriors who could play ball with the heads of enemies would bow and stand before her. She walked along the palace corridor. The great warriors too stood bowed before her.

Status, splendour, halls and towers, a place in the ruler's lap—despite having all this, her heart kept searching for something with an unending hunger.

The king, exhausted and thinking the pleasure Amirthamathi had given him was enough, would fall asleep. After that she would rise softly and walk with the steps of a cat. All this yearning was for the elephant keeper who had captured her heart!

<!-- source scan 51; printed page 49 -->

He would be waiting in the palace garden itself. Amirthamathi would approach him eagerly! Before she could do so, that elephant keeper would lash her mercilessly with the whip in his hand. That was the punishment he gave her for coming late! The blows of the whip would tear her clothes, and in the moonlight her limbs would appear and laugh rather than begin to weep! “Forgive me, my king,” the queen would say, embracing that elephant keeper tightly and showering a thousand kisses from the crown of his head to the soles of his feet. Then the two, fallen together on the green grass, would undertake every ‘journey to the other world’ and ‘tour of heaven.’ Pus and blood oozing from the elephant keeper's body would be smeared here and there over Amirthamathi's golden body. To her they would look like diamonds and coral! There was no other disease that elephant keeper suffered from—it was leprosy!

This, Udanpirappe, is the story of Amirthamathi! With a good husband—a king who ruled the country—a virile man—a handsome ruler—beside her, she abandoned him and, enduring even whip blows from a leprous elephant keeper, longed for amorous dalliance with him. In the same way, I wrote in anguish that some people, though good Tamil is available, abandon it, take words soaked in a stinking pool and write with them—and abuse with them.

<!-- source scan 52; printed page 50 -->

The censorship officers stopped even that letter from reaching your hands. We had to go as far as the court. It was a time when rights had been taken away—and because of that no one was even prepared to take up our case!

The letter was written to say that, just as a woman leaves a handsome husband and runs in search of a handsome keeper, it is wrong to abandon beautiful Tamil words and search for erukkam-flower words. Yet it came under some suspicious gaze and was completely stopped by the authorities! I have already referred briefly to this story in an earlier letter. Today I have written it in somewhat greater detail.

Earlier I quoted this story to point to the conduct of a few writers. Now, does it not seem to fit not only those writers but also some people who have come to hate us and gone elsewhere?

How much flavour epics and poems add; and at the same time, have you seen how useful they can be for the political ideas we need to convey indirectly!

Before beginning to write today's letter to you, I was leafing through the ‘Kuruntokai.’ There is a poet called Velli Veethiyar. She took me toward a rock and

<!-- source scan 53; printed page 51 -->

went. Which rock? In which town? Manapparai? Vaalpaarai? Don't start asking all that! It was a small rock near a mountain. She took me toward it and pointed, saying, ‘Look there.’ I too looked eagerly. I could not remain without telling you what I saw. I will tell you; it does not matter what event you connect it with—I will show you the scene I saw exactly as though taking a picture of it for you.

A small rock! On that rock lies a large ball of butter! A man is guarding that butter. He has no hands. His mouth cannot speak either—he is mute! At that moment the sun is rising higher. As the sun rises, the butter on the rock melts. If he wants to pick it up and move it elsewhere, the guard has no hands! If he wants to call someone for help, he has no voice! The butter keeps melting!

Udanpirappe, if a handless mute is ordered to guard butter, what will happen? Is not the scene I saw a clear answer to that question?

This is what Velli Veethiyar has painted in beautiful Tamil in the Kuruntokai:

“Beside the white rock scorched by the sun,
watched only by the eyes of a handless mute,
like butter melting away....”

What if a handless mute is placed as guard over butter, and the butter is then left in the sun?

Udanpirappe! If the scene Velli Veethiyar shows playfully in the Kuruntokai were actually to happen in the country, how painful it would become. Do you understand?
