# Aattakkavadi

“An **aattakkavadi** is not a devotional kavadi! Those people carry devotional kavadi as an act of prayer; what happens in front of them is a **kavadi dance performance**—that is all! Many who dance carrying an aattakkavadi keep dancing after pouring seven or eight measures of liquor into themselves. Are you asking whether those who carry devotional kavadi are any more disciplined? They are intoxicated by devotion itself, which is more dangerous than liquor! So listen to what I am saying, Kanimozhi! Understand clearly that the aattakkavadi performance I am going to conduct has nothing to do with devotion! There will be a kavadi on my shoulders; that is all—do not imagine that the feet of the Lord will also be in my thoughts! That intoxication of superstition belongs to the devotees who come before me with their cheeks pierced, carrying kavadi fitted with pots of milk. My kavadi will have ‘pāvalā’, not milk! There will be the art of dance—there will be no murder of reason! Peacock feathers will be at the front of the kavadi—but in my heart there will only be the thought of you, my maiden, not some foolish dream of Murugan riding the peacock! Give me permission, my dear! :

They say they will give me a hundred rupees—money I could never see all at once even if I worked till my tongue wore out for four or five months together!

<!-- source scan 32; printed page 23 -->

A hundred rupees! If I go to Palani and dance there, I may even find new customers. If I get two or three customers approximately, that will be enough—I can come back after saving at least three hundred rupees. Give me permission, Kanimozhi! I have to leave tomorrow morning. The people making the vow are very rich. I have already given them my word that I will definitely come; don't stop me, dear! If I disappoint them now, they will not find another aattakkavadi dancer. They will go on cursing me until they die! Say yes!—Come on, look at me! Won't you say yes?...”

Looking eagerly at Kanimozhi's face, Kandan kept speaking in a coaxing manner. Kanimozhi's eyes were clouded. She looked at him with sympathy.

“It is the desire for money that makes you talk like this! You have strength in your body—hands that can work. Compared with earning a hundred rupees by dancing kavadi in front of those who carry kavadi to a god's shrine, the ten or twenty rupees earned at the handloom is far nobler, aththaan! No; listen to me—the neighbours will mock us! ‘He said there was no god, no spirit, and had a Thirukkural marriage. Now he is going to dance carrying an aattakkavadi,’ the village will clap and laugh, aththaan!” Kanimozhi wept.

“Only people who don't know what aattakkavadi means will laugh!—It is just like a dance!”

“If it is a dance, then show that aattakkavadi dance on a stage! Why must you dance only in front of devotees carrying kavadi to Palani?”

“Money comes from it—money!”

“Honour goes because of it—honour!”

<!-- source scan 33; printed page 24 -->

“Kanimozhi! How many times have I told you? What I dance is aattakkavadi—theirs is devotional kavadi!”

“Let them dance their devotional kavadi separately! You dance aattakkavadi on another day! Show your artistic skill! Who said you should not? But don't join the two at the same time—disgrace and blame will come to us! I myself will arrange a programme to display your aattakkavadi skill. One day we will gather the villagers; pour out your artistry before them. I will watch and delight in it! Leave this alone; don't stain yourself for money!”

“Mad woman! Is a hundred rupees some ordinary thing?”

“What do we need a hundred rupees for now? Am I tormenting you by demanding a zari sari and gold jewellery? Gruel to drink is enough—your heart is enough for me to live in!”

“Kanimozhi!... Whatever you say, tomorrow I am going to Palani—that is final. I cannot change my decision!”

Kandan rose abruptly. Kanimozhi held his hand and stopped him.

“Think carefully. The village will ridicule you. All your close friends whose lives revolve around you will have to walk with their heads bowed in shame, unable even to face the villagers. At least for your friends' sake, change this decision!”

“All right! Just once! Give me permission! I won't go again!”

“Can I give permission to swallow poison just once?”

“Kanimozhi, I know that what I am doing is wrong—but even when we know, sometimes we have no choice but to do it!”

<!-- source scan 34; printed page 25 -->

“Once upon a time you were an expert aattakkavadi dancer. If you had simply continued with that, you could have gone not only to Palani but even to Tiruchendur, Tiruttani, Ettukudi and Kathirkamam. But in between we entered the realm of rationalism, aththaan—think of that for a moment! People who know something is wrong do not do the same wrong again!”

Kandan stood motionless. Kanimozhi's eyes remained fixed on him. Her thoughts, however, were turning the pages of the past.

<!-- source enlarged initial: க at the flashback paragraph beginning கனிமொழி... -->

Kanimozhi was a blue moon that rose in a weaver's home. Her father, Nellaiyappar, was a mature rationalist. He raised his only daughter not merely as a cherished child but as a garden of thought. With pride Nellaiyappar would say that Kanimozhi must become the first woman to combine the manners and bearing of the countryside with the richness of a treasury of rational thought. From childhood itself, Kanimozhi had become a student in the school of rationalism.

Deepavali! The whole street would be in uproar! Coloured fireworks—fireworks that displayed colours like the rainbow—she would not even turn to look at them. Sitting at the entrance, she would loudly read the book her father had bought her, **“Do Not Celebrate Deepavali!”** Against the Chinese crackers lit by the street children, this graceful little bird would raise a cry of thought!

Monday fasts—the festivals of Mari and Kali—the Draupadi Amman fire-walking festival—such celebrations that captivated the mind would come and go. Kanimozhi, remaining in that tiny village, would think about the novelties of the wider world and sigh deeply.

Kanimozhi became a lone tree. Her father died, leaving the maiden alone. Motherless already, she now

<!-- source scan 35; printed page 26 -->

became entitled to the name of orphan. The villagers kept predicting how her life might now turn. It was at that time that the sound of pambai drums and festive instruments was heard at her doorway.

Though she usually ignored such events, for some reason that day she turned toward the street. Once she looked, she struggled to take her eyes away. An aattakkavadi dance! An **athirupa sundaran**—a figure brimming with beauty and youth—was carrying the aattakkavadi like a hill upon his long shoulders.

The rousing beat of the pambai—and matching it, his energetic dance! ‘Ah—what a handsome young man—what wonderful art!’ she felt as though someone inside her were speaking. She shifted her eyes slightly—and started! The village mirasudar, Mirugandu Mudaliar, his virtuous wife, and their son who studied at a city college—the three of them stood with their cheeks pierced, carrying devotional milk-kavadi. Now Kanimozhi came to detest even the art of aattakkavadi.

‘You sinners! Such a fine art—and you turn it into water poured on barren ground! You mix sandal paste with sewer water! Whether poison is mixed into milk or milk into poison, are not both the same?’ Thinking all this, she stood at the threshold in wonder and distress. Only Mirasudar Mirugandu's gaze began to turn toward Kanimozhi. Kavadi on his shoulder! Kanimozhi-mantra in his mind! So devoutly stood the landlord!

“That fellow whose kavadi dance makes the whole village tremble—that is Kandan!” the woman next door told Kanimozhi. Pushed by confusion, Kanimozhi went inside.

Mirugandu Mudaliar too moved on with the kavadi—still looking toward Kanimozhi's door.

<!-- source scan 36; printed page 27 -->

The sounds of the pambai slowly faded away. But in some corner of Kanimozhi's heart, a sound continued in a faint voice.

One night Kanimozhi heard a knock and opened the door. Mirasudar Mirugandu Mudaliar rushed inside. He bolted the door himself.

Need the reason be explained? There was fruit—the bat had flown to it!

“Who are you, man—are you that ‘gentleman’ who stood the other day with your cheek pierced, carrying kavadi overflowing with devotion?” Kanimozhi became fire.

“Yes, my parrot! That piercing spike—a silver spike! Little parrot, stroke my cheek with your beak!” Mirugandu said, reaching out to touch her.

Kanimozhi was a rationalist woman, was she not! Had she not read the story of Kannagi! Her hand did not go to waste upon the landlord's cheeks! Even so, the maddened tiger—the fawn that would not stray from its path—danger drew near! A scream—a cry—a warning of danger calling for rescue—rose from that hut! At that moment aattakkavadi Kandan leapt in. Mirugandu was terrified. He was trampled under Kandan's feet. Kanimozhi escaped. Just as stories have made it customary for danger to approach the heroine, for the hero to arrive and save her, and then for love to arise, such a real incident unexpectedly occurred in Kanimozhi's life too. Kanimozhi laid down many conditions before she and Kandan could begin their journey through life.

One of them was that he must stop dancing aattakkavadi for temples! Would he refuse?—Aattakkavadi stopped. She became the teacher who brought reason to him. Kandan became the dark pupil of her eye. Only after that did the two marry. The whole village sank into astonishment! Along Kandan's path many

<!-- source scan 37; printed page 28 -->

young men began to walk. Kandan became the young leader of the village youth. Kanimozhi, like a full moon rising radiant from an ocean of joy, showed a glowing face and rejoiced. Kavadi Kandan became Handloom Kandan. The money from the handloom was not enough for his generous spending! He struggled. Yet he was afraid to act against Kanimozhi's firm resolve.

The brain of Mirugandu Mirasudar's son, Peethambaram, began to work. He wanted to smash to dust the ideological fortress that had risen in the village. He became Kandan's friend. He often lectured Kandan on the value and greatness of money! Pretending to accept Kandan's principles, he acted as though he were merely proposing a few corrections to them. One of those ‘corrections’ was that Kandan could dance aattakkavadi at Palani!

As Kandan explained that correction, Kanimozhi stood like a stone statue that night.

Kandan, who had been standing there, suddenly left the place at speed.

<!-- source enlarged initial: ம at the paragraph beginning மறுநாள்... -->

The next day his journey to Palani began. The aattakkavadi dance took place grandly in the streets of Palani. Just as he had expected, three or four customers came his way there. He got three hundred rupees. Taking the money, he returned to the village. With complete confidence that he could show the money and dissolve Kanimozhi's anger, he entered the house. Kanimozhi was not there. That rationalist little bird had flown somewhere. A roll of paper lay on the cot. Kandan picked it up and opened it.

“Public life is not like tasting sugarcane; it is like rubbing sugarcane with your hands! Only when you rub it will you know—your whole hand will be pierced by ‘suṇ’ splinters!”

After your decision, there are only two decisions left for me to make!

One is poison—

The other is divorce!

Unable to decide which of these to choose, I am wandering somewhere in some corner of the world.

<!-- source sign-off is centered/right-set on the printed page -->

Yours,

**Kanimozhi**

A rationalist woman.

Kandan finished reading the letter. As though a piercing spike had been driven into his eyes, he convulsed, fell to the floor and fainted.
