# Panangulai

“Are your father and mother alive?”

“No. Mother died! I don’t know whether Father is alive or not. They say that while I was still in my mother’s womb, a baseless murder charge was laid on Father and he ran away somewhere to a land beyond sight. They say there was a boy older than me. He too disappeared when he was two. Thinking of the thunderbolt that fell on our family happiness, my mother cried and cried until she too closed her eyes! Now I am an orphan!”

“Don’t say ‘orphan’! That was true only until yesterday! From today there is no place for that word, Kamalam! More or less, my life and yours are alike! At least you grew in a mother’s embrace. You saw your mother’s face, received the love she poured out, and grew up nourished by it. I do not even know what the comfort of lying in a mother’s lap or on a father’s shoulder is like. I do not even remember having seen their faces. The harsh hands of an orphanage raised me and made me a man. Today two orphans have come together. We have driven away the very label ‘orphan’ and left it without work!”—saying this, he embraced Kamalam.

Tears poured from her eyes. The happiness they had both awaited for so many days was theirs at that moment. According to season and circumstance, Kamalam’s mother had kept her daughter alive by running an idli stall, doing petty-market trade, selling tender coconuts, panai nungu and many such things. After her mother died, Kamalam had to take up the same trades. Since it was the season for ‘panai nungu’, Kamalam was engaged in that business. She had to buy palmyra fruit wholesale, sell it, and wash her stomach with the small profit that came from it.

It was then that one day Velan came to buy ‘panai nungu’ from her. The man who came one day came again the next. He came often. If he did not come and buy ‘panai nungu’, even she felt that no business had taken place that day. She would return home with a face as though she had suffered a great loss in trade. One day he asked her,

“What profit can you make in this business?”

She explained the details. “From tomorrow I myself will bring panangulai. Cut them and sell them. You will make plenty of profit,” he said. “No, I don’t want all that,” Kamalam said, shaking her head, and went home.

The next day Velan came carrying four or five panangulai. “What are these?” Kamalam asked. “I climbed the palmyra tree myself, cut them and brought them,” he replied proudly, standing there looking only at her face. “Is it your own tree?” she asked. “Who owns a tree that stands on poramboke land? Doesn’t it belong to everyone?” Before he could finish, a rough man shouted, “Hey, thieving fellow! Whose tree are you stealing from? Who do you think I am?” and struck Velan on the head with a large staff.

Velan crumpled and fell. Kamalam ran to attend to him. The brute gathered up all the panangulai lying there and walked away, mocking him: “The little fellow steals palmyra fruit for a girl he fancies? And from me?” The man who had started walking stopped. He watched Kamalam’s agitation. He saw her running here and there, sprinkling water on Velan’s face and fanning him with the end of her sari. A mark of lust showed itself on the brute’s face. He came back to Kamalam.

“Here, little one! If you wanted palmyra fruit, wouldn’t I give it if you asked me? Can fellows like this be trusted? Here, keep all of it! Why are you doing this business at such a young age? If you had asked me, I would have made you a grand stree-part performer in our drama company...” The brute bared his teeth.

“All right, go mind your own work!” Kamalam said, devoting herself wholly to tending Velan. The brute shook his head and left with some decision in mind. Kamalam knew very well that the rough man was Moga Vinayagampillai, owner of the Murugananda Mohanagana Sabha. She also knew that he was a great bandicoot who swallowed the town’s property. She knew only that he had been claiming rights over the palmyra trees on poramboke land. No one knew that very soon he intended to claim rights over Kamalam herself.

When Velan, who had fallen from the blow, regained consciousness, Kamalam took him to her hut. After the necessary treatment, when he thanked her and tried to leave, Kamalam refused to give him leave. He understood the reason. They spoke about their births and how they had been raised. The two had met as orphans. The title ‘orphan’ fled from both of them. They embraced fully and rejoiced. That night sang a song of pleasure and moved slowly, slowly—

onward. Though they tried to stop it, the cock crowed. Even if the cock did not crow, the sun said, “I will rise anyway.” A new delight in life! Excitement! The hut that had echoed the sighs of a solitary woman now rejoiced, echoing songs of love.

One night—many days later! Midnight. The lovers were sleeping in exhaustion. Someone knocked at the door. Kamalam went and opened it. Before her stood a strongly built, white-bearded old man who looked well advanced in years.

“Who are you?” Kamalam asked. By then Velan too had risen and come out.

“You ask who I am? Daughter! Kamalam! Don’t you know me? Of course you do not; you would not know this sinner! You would not know the wretch who ran away leaving your mother Meenakshi alone, burdened and with child! Amma, daughter! I am your father, Ayyasami!”

“Ah! Father! My father!....” Kamalam cried out. She clung to the old man and sobbed.

“Is this your elder brother Velan?—Velan, my beloved son! My precious treasure! Do you know how many tears Meenakshi and I shed after losing you when you were only two? Appa, thambi! How have you lived all these years! When did you find your younger sister Kamalam? I have been moving in hiding, afraid of the police. Only yesterday I came and made enquiries at the orphanage where you were raised. They told me everything. I had thought we had lost you forever. Later a friend of mine told me that you were growing up in an orphanage! I went there. I learned that you were alive and had grown well. Who stole you when you were a child? Do you remember, son! They say the people who took you were my enemies. Afraid of the police, they apparently left you in the middle of the road and went away. Only after that—

did the orphanage take you in and raise you. Ah...how big my son has grown—thambi! How did you seek:out your sister and come here?”

The old man kept talking, unable to contain his joy!—Velan cried, “Ayyo! Father!” That was all. He began running somewhere. He ran; he ran—ran into the darkness and disappeared. Kamalam fell unconscious. “Kamalam! Kamalam!!” the old man called. He lifted her and placed her on his lap. He held her tightly. He kissed her cheeks again and again. He seized her lips with his own. Some feeling came over the old man! He laid Kamalam down. He touched her feet. They had gone cold. Kamalam was gone. The old man rose in frustration. His mind spoke.

“Che! I planned one thing and another happened! I thought that if I falsely declared them brother and sister and separated them, he would run away—and I could carry her off. The disguise worked; but the little she-goat slipped away!”

With a long sigh the old man removed his beard and moustache. As the rough Moga Vinayagam Pillai, he left the hut. He went home through the poramboke land over which he claimed rights. Something fell from a tree with a thud. Thinking someone was cutting down a panangulai, he roared, “Who is that thieving fellow?” and went to the foot of the tree. What had fallen from the tree was not a panangulai!—it was Velan! He struck a match and looked closely. The head was shattered, like a cluster broken apart! That noble fellow who had smashed a family to pieces went home in an ordinary way, without the slightest movement in his heart, and began to sleep peacefully.

There are many such births in this world!
