“Jegaveera, I do not like Sathyavathi’s conduct, my son!”

“Mother!”

“Yes, my dear. Sathyavathi is a young woman and the crowned queen. It is not proper for her to speak with other men as she pleases. It is a disgrace to the very Rajput lineage that gave birth to women of distinction who entered the fire!”

“Mother, what are you saying? What has Sathyavathi done? With whom has she dallied? Tell me!”

“Do not get angry! I know that you love her as you love your own eyes. Even so....!”

“What is this ‘even so,’ Mother...?”

“I do not like the way your younger brother and she often talk alone, Jegaveera. I only say that if you rebuke her a little, she will keep within bounds.”

“Mother, do not speak wrongly like that about my younger brother Arthol! He is pure gold—a lamp of radiant light!”

“Jegaveera! You are going to Delhi. It will take time for you to return. I have warned you now itself. Do not think that I am finding fault with Sathyavathi—

—I only want to see that she does not stray onto a wrong path.”

“All right, Mother. Take good care of everything. I am leaving.”

Jegaveerasimman had once been the lamp of the hearts of the people of Orissa. In the seventeenth century he was the crown prince who enjoyed immeasurable influence among the people of that region. Emperor Shah Jahan’s coronation was to take place in Delhi. An affectionate invitation had come to Jegaveera. It was just as he was setting out for Delhi with a thousand warriors that his mother, Amirtharani, came and laid these complaints about her daughter-in-law Sathyavathi before him. The king who had come to take leave was struck by the spear of her words. There was no count of the tigers that had become corpses when he sprang upon them like spotted deer. Once he leapt into the battlefield, countless men would fall dead in the blink of an eye beneath the sweep of his sword. Yet such a mighty warrior was made to curl inward by his mother’s arrows of speech. He thought that she must have fired that warning shaft because some stain had begun to spread across his happy married life. He grieved over it. With a withered face he set out for Delhi.

Sathyavathi, who had now become the problem gnawing at his heart, was his beloved queen. So deep was her devotion to God that she could be called the new Mirabai of Orissa. The queen, who loved her husband Jegaveera and also loved her country, had only one unfulfilled concern. She had been waiting for the right time to remove it. Jegaveera’s father—and Sathyavathi’s father-in-law—King Veerasimman had been attacked by a tiger. As he lay dying, he entrusted his younger son Arthol to Jegaveera and Sathyavathi and asked them to protect him. He also requested, as a memorial to himself, that a temple to Lord Rama be built. Jegaveera promised then that he would fulfil his father’s command. The temple raised in his father’s memory had to be splendid—

—it would require a great deal of gold. With the treasury in poor condition at that time, Sathyavathi wondered how such a temple could be built. Yet she longed somehow to fulfil the promise made to her father-in-law. In keeping with his wish, she carefully protected his younger son Arthol. Because of that, Arthol regarded her exactly as his own mother. Sathyavathi repeatedly told Arthol of her desire to see the temple for the Lord built quickly.

“Arthol! The king will take a long time to return from Delhi, won’t he?”

“Yes, Mother. It will take at least four months.”

“If we somehow complete Rama’s temple before then, do you know how happy he will be when he returns?”

“Mother, that is true. We could raise the temple tomorrow itself. But there is no money for it in the treasury!”

“You once said there was a diamond mine somewhere. What became of that, Arthol?”

“Yes, there is one. I thought we could use those diamonds to spare the people the burden of taxation.”

“First the burden on my mind must be lifted, Arthol! Once Rama’s temple is built, no evil will arise in the country, and the evils already there will be destroyed. So attend first to the temple work!”

“We must gather diamonds from the mine in utmost secrecy. If Delhi learns that the mine is in our country, it will set out at cyclone speed, mouth wide open, asking where the diamonds are!”

“All right, Arthol. In Delhi there are festivals and celebrations now. In this favourable time, let us secretly—

—collect the diamonds and finish building the temple of Vaikunta Nathan!”

“Your command, Mother!”

Arthol left to carry out Sathyavathi’s wish.

Though he had been invited well in advance for the coronation, Jegaveera could find no rest for his mind there. Was Sathyavathi an adventuress? Or was the mother who had borne him making a false accusation? Or was it his brother Arthol who was betraying him? This battle raged inside him. At the very height of that inner war, a palm-leaf message arrived for him from Orissa. He opened it and read it; he widened his eyes and read it again. Yes—it was a letter from his mother.

“My dear son! I warned you that day, before the flood came! You said then that you would find out. Now the result has turned disastrous. Every midnight Sathyavathi sets out to meet Arthol. I can prove it. If questioned, she says, ‘We are drawing up plans to build the temple!’ Before the mine destroys our lineage, set out and come back!

Your mother

**Amirtha Rani”**

At the sight of the palm leaf, Jegaveera’s head spun. In the coronation hall of the Badshah of Delhi, the sweet resonance of the veena! In Veerasimman’s mental cave, the lifeless screech of snapped nerves twisting apart! There the roar of drums; here the turmoil of the Orissa king. At once he set out toward his own land.

It was night. A chest full of diamonds. That chest was to become a temple for Rama. Before the temple itself had been built, the image that would be installed in it was already ready. Before it sat Sathyavathi, singing hymns to Rama—

—while her thoughts rose in waves. Her father-in-law’s loving command was about to be fulfilled. Her husband was about to return. When he came and saw the temple, how happy he would be in astonishment! For the country to prosper, this Lord Rama was about to take residence in a temple. With such thoughts, Sathyavathi immersed herself in worship. The worship ended. To take the diamonds away and place them in hiding, Sathyavathi called Arthol.

“Arthol! Arthol!”

“Here I am, you sly woman!” Jegaveera said as he entered. Sathyavathi turned with a start.

“My lord!”

“Stand back! O kanjarang fruit that gives beauty to the eye! Great sinner who has brought shame upon this very earth! Stand back!”

“What are you saying?”

“Where is your lover Arthol?”

“Ah! Do not say that, my lord! Arthol is like a son to me—he is my beloved child.”

“A son, is he? Is that why you meet him alone at midnight and talk with him again and again?”

“Yes—what is wrong in that? I spoke with Arthol about raising a temple for the Lord. We drew up plans. The sacred work has already begun!”

“Fire! Enough—stop!”

“Look at these diamonds, my lord! They were gathered to build Rama’s temple. At least now believe me.”

“Those diamonds are not for building a temple, woman! They are a gift Arthol gave you for your embrace. I know!”

“Ayyo! Ayyo!”

“Why do you cover your ears? You shut your eyes, fall into the sea of lust, and worship the god Rama to deceive the town. Do not pretend!”

“My lord! Do not insult my chastity. Do not become prey to the curse of my heart.”

“What, you deceiver! If you have no illicit love for Arthol, then take this poison in your own hand and give it to him. Let us see.”

“To him? No! I myself will eat it and die!”

“No! Arthol must drink the poison from your hand.”

“Let it be so.”

Sathyavathi took the poison and ran.

Arthol, who had been working out the temple plans, had fallen asleep from exhaustion. It was then that Sathyavathi entered with a cup of poison in her hand.

“Arthol!”

“What is it, Mother?”

“Here—drink this milk!”

“Milk? At this hour?”

“Yes. To prove that I am a chaste wife, they say that you must drink this milk mixed with pashaanam from my hand.”

“What? You—?”

“The king has accused me of being your lover. To prove that it is not so, you are to eat poison from my very hand.”

“Ah! Mother! Who tied this slander upon us? Did the king believe it? My elder brother—believing these empty complaints, did he even dare to call his younger brother one of improper conduct? Brother! Brother, why did my brother speak so as to wound my heart? Ayyo! Mother! I cannot bear it!”

“There is no time to talk, Arthol! My beloved son, only if you consume the poison will the honour of the Rajput lineage be saved.”

“I will drink it. O poison! You are the one who saves my elder brother from slander. We began building a temple for Rama—we had even carved a beautiful image of Rama. Yet that Rama—or his retinue—did not dare come and tell my brother of my purity! Poison, at least you have come to wipe away the stain that those who do not understand have cast upon Mother! Thank you! Poison is sweet! Poison is very sweet! Sweeter than God! This poison is sweeter than Lord Rama, who does not extend a hand to those who trust him.” Saying this, he took the poison from Sathyavathi’s hand, drank it, fell—and died.

Sathyavathi bathed him in her tears. Only afterwards did Jegaveera himself realise his mistake. Amirtharani too wept afterwards. Everyone shed tears and lamented after the deed was done. To prevent such lamentation, Lord Rama could have come to Jegaveera and explained Sathyavathi’s purity! But how could he? Had he done so, Arthol would not have said that **Aalahalam is sweeter than God**!
