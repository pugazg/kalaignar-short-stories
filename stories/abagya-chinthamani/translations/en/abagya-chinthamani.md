# Abagya Chinthamani

<!-- source scan 181; printed page 172 -->

There—do you see the woman begging in the street? Do you know who she is? Do not imagine that I am going to test your patience by telling you an old story such as **“She was not born into a line of beggars; she lived as a wealthy woman; the wheel of time reduced her to this state.”** She is indeed a hereditary beggar. That is to say, even her grandmother was a beggar! How old do you think the beggar you see here might be? You say about forty, do you not? No, no; I am telling you exactly—she is only thirty-five! She simply looks that old. She might even be taken for forty or fifty. In these times we see people of fifty and sixty locking in their teeth like twelve-year-old youthful birds, filling the hollows of their faces with powder, fashioning kovvai-red lips with colour and crescent brows with kohl, climbing to the Everest of civilization and crowing in triumph. This poor beggar has no wish to change her age that way. Even if she wished to, she has no money. And so, carrying on her body the dust of age settled by the winds of youth, she wanders through the streets of Thiruvarur.

She stands at doorway after doorway and sings in a rasping voice. Do you understand what song she sings? If not, ask me; I will tell you.

<!-- source display: two centered/indented song-like blocks; lineation preserved -->

“Erpathigazhchiyendra
**our grandmother Avvai herself**
also sang, Aiyamittun!”

“Even if one must beg, learning is good;
even giving a leftover leaf is good!”

Thus she takes mere spoken lines, sets them to a new raga she herself has discovered, adds the needed brikkaas, and sings them differently to suit each house.

Do you ask how I know so much about her? One day her musical voice was heard at our house. Having waited many days for a chance to learn who she was, I began asking for her history that day. She began, and finished half an hour later. After hearing that story, it would be impossible for anyone not to give her alms. It happened also to be the memorial day for my younger sister at our house! So she received a good meal with payasam and vadai. “May you live like a Maharaja, sami,” she blessed me and took her leave.

When even being an ordinary man brings enough difficulty and commotion, why go and get trapped as a Maharaja and suffer still more? Thinking that to myself, I saw her off. That beggar woman’s native place—no—pardon me, her birthplace was the Chinthamani area near Tiruchirappalli.

Perhaps that was why her mother too named her Chinthamani! This unfortunate Chinthamani was born from the womb of a beggar, grew with a disease a day and a danger every moment, and began helping her mother in the work of begging. When Chinthamani—

completed ten years of age, her mother fell ill. The roadside five-foot strip in the Chinthamani area became the battlefield where Chinthamani’s mother fought with death. The impossible problem of boiling gruel and feeding her mother while somehow quieting the storm in her own stomach fell upon the head of that ten-year-old tender girl. For a day or two Chinthamani somehow struggled to hold back the breath that was trying to escape from her mother and stayed beside her, protecting the little life that had not yet gone out.

Would a beggar girl have no love for her mother? And could a beggar mother be without anxiety about her daughter’s future?

“My daughter, do not depend on me any longer, my dear! My time is more or less over! I consented to every man who showed his teeth and merely kept this ruined body alive. Look at that dog in the gutter, rolling about to soothe the itching of its mange-covered body; just like that, rabid dogs in human form came near me and ruined me. My dear! Do not become like me! Ours is indeed a beggar family; yet your grandmother lived with great dignity. She made a tremendous effort not to let me become a beggar, but to make me a member of the society in which we live. She educated me. I studied by depending on the favour of a great man.

“But, my dear, when my mother closed her eyes, my eyes too were closed. The schoolboys surrounded me because I was a beggar woman’s daughter. I grew proud, thinking how beautiful I must be for them to circle me so. I even expected some great war to break out among the students over me. Those bulls gathered like bears seeking a honeycomb. I lost myself. I lost awareness. This sinner became an example for the madmen who say women should not be educated. If one slips once on the bathing steps, there is not even time to think that one can plant one’s foot firmly on the next step—

and escape! Before that, must one not reach the bottom of the pond? And what is the fate of those like me who do not know how to swim? I slipped into the moss-covered ruined pond of society. Raining a vasai-maari—‘Can a dog be bathed and brought into the middle hall?’—the great man drove me out of the house. I took up my mother’s work. I begged for my stomach! I also gave alms to the lustful acts of young men! It was in such a life of mine that—you, my star, rose! Do not become like me. Live honourably, with dignity, like your grandmother, my child!”

At the doorway of death, after singing this sorrowful song mingled with the history of her experience, Chinthamani’s mother sang the mangalam and ended her concert.

Now motherless and orphaned, Chinthamani wandered for some days as a little beggar girl. Suddenly a stroke of “luck” came to her. She too found a place in the Ramalingasami Gurukulam, which sheltered orphan children and gave them education. The head teacher of that gurukulam was a seasoned devotee of Ramalingar. His name was Ramalinga Sivanandam. He was over fifty. Ramalingar’s Arutpa verses were committed to his memory. He was a tiger in the Thevaram and Thiruvasagam. There was hardly a moment when he did not mutter, “All religions are acceptable—praise to the Southern Lord Siva!” It was he who saw the orphan girl and brought her into the gurukulam. Chinthamani studied well and passed her examinations. Sivanandam’s eldest son, Jothiyanandam, had gone to America for higher studies. He completed his studies and returned home. An encounter that stirred his mind took place in the gurukulam. That encounter was Chinthamani! Chinthamani and Jothiyanandam became lovers. Need I describe that stage of love? Why remind you, who are already tired of seeing and hearing such scenes, of it—

and thereby cause great pain to bachelors and great Vedantic reflections in large family men!

Sivanandam, the devotee of the Arutperum Jothi, heard that Chinthamani had become the light in Jothiyanandam’s heart.

The mouth that used to mutter “All religions are acceptable!” began abusing his son. “Love, is it—love! If you bring a dog and keep it in the middle of the house, this is what will happen,” he shouted. Chinthamani truly was no dog!

There was a difference between the great man who had once helped Chinthamani’s mother and abused her with **“If a dog is bathed and placed in the middle of the house...”**, and Ramalinga Sivanandam abusing Chinthamani now!

It is true that Sivanandam gave Chinthamani refuge in order to gain public fame and to carry the garlands of praise offered by the people of the gurukulam.

But if she wished to become his own daughter-in-law for that reason, could he permit it?

He rebuked his son. He spread slanders about Chinthamani. He searched for thousands upon thousands of proofs that love was a false word.

“A cat that has found the pot will itself call the milk paala-pashaanam, appa,” Jothi replied. He would not listen. Chinthamani was expelled from the gurukulam, accused of prostitution. Thinking that Jothi’s family must not be ruined because of her, Chinthamani explained again and again and begged him to forget her. He did not listen. He followed her.

For some days in this way, she found joy even amid suffering. Jothi’s father became insane. His son’s course had disturbed his mind.

The whole family floundered. Jothi’s mother and sisters came to Chinthamani and, to save their family, begged her for a “madippichai”: “Give Jothi back!” “Jothi is the head of the family. If he too goes away, how can this family ever return to a good condition with a father who has gone mad? Show us mercy, amma,” they lamented.

“Could you not at least in this way show some gratitude to Sivanandam, who supported you when you were an orphan, cared for you all these years, and was like a father to you?” her friends from the gurukulam advised. Chinthamani gave no answer to anyone and went away silently.

Jothi, who had been out of town, came to Chinthamani’s house. It was a small thatched hut, the temporary palace they had built for their household. He knocked. The door opened by itself; he looked inside. A dim light showed in the darkness. Saying, “She has fallen asleep without even bolting the door,” he called, “Chinthamani!” No answer. He looked toward the bed, started, and cried out! What do you imagine? That Chinthamani had gone away? Or that she had killed herself? Had she killed herself, how could she have told me this story—so do you conclude that she must simply have run away? That too is wrong!

What Jothi saw was not that. Chinthamani was deep asleep, tightly embracing the body of a kattu-masthaan, teak-like young man. The young man’s face was buried in her soft cheeks.

“You sinner!” Jothi shouted. Chinthamani awoke.

Jothi sprang to throttle her neck. The young man stopped him.

“This has been going on for many days,” Chinthamani explained. Jothi struck his own head and returned home. “Prostitute! Prostitute!” his mouth kept babbling. He did not know the truth—that Chinthamani had entered upon that cruel act only so that, thinking her a prostitute, he would forget her.

He thinks she is a prostitute!

It was not a great flood of desire that made her a prostitute!

Only she knew that she had come to that decision in order to give relief to Jothi’s family.

It did not matter if she acquired the name of prostitute.

She did not want to carry the names of vengeful woman and chandala who destroyed a family; nor had she turned that hut into a hall welcoming everyone.

Once her purpose was fulfilled, she left that town. “My mother had the circumstances to live properly, yet did not live properly. I myself wished to live properly, yet circumstance did not allow me to live properly!”

That was the conclusion the beggar woman gave to her story. “What became of Jothi’s father?” I asked. “His madness cleared—but his son Jothi went mad and finally died,” she said through tears.

“Yes—but if Jothi had to forget you, you could have killed yourself. Why did you need to incur such a great disgrace?” I asked. In a sobbing voice she gave this answer:—

“I did think of killing myself. But Jothi’s token of love had been growing in my womb for three months. I did not want to destroy it. For that—

<!-- source scan 188; printed page 179 -->

reason alone I used that improper stratagem and changed Jothi’s mind. But the desire for which I wished to remain alive was destroyed. The male child born in a state without nourishment had no wish to see this world and was born already a corpse.” Saying this, she rose with a deep sigh.

The shade of sorrow showed in every one of her words. Look—there she is singing opposite that house; even in that sad cadence, is there no reflection of poverty? Is it not the failure of love that echoes?
