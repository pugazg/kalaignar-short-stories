Everywhere across the city of Chennai, crowds had gathered in front of shops where news from radio sets was being relayed. People moved through the streets with hanging faces, wiping their eyes—why, even sobbing and weeping.

That day was January 30, 1948! There was still no clear news that Godse had killed Gandhi. But the friends and well-wishers of a Hindu Rajya had given that Father of the Nation, who had fought for freedom, his “proper reward.” The body of that noble man, who had fallen after taking three bullets, had not yet even been laid out for public viewing.

It was at that hour that the young man walked slowly past electric lamp posts glowing like fireflies. In the dim light of those lamps, powerless to tear through the darkness, his

<!-- source scan 7; printed page 2 -->

tears could be seen running from his eyes. He was walking through an area filled with hundreds of huts. Along the roadside, pigs with many piglets wallowed in sewer mud. In the middle of the road, small children, unconcerned about anyone, relieved themselves. In one place where bricks lay piled up, several men and women who had barely enough clothing to cover half their bodies talked and laughed loudly, squeezing out the “pure Tamil” peculiar to Chennai.

His thoughts surged like waves! Gandhi had waged the freedom struggle and won with the hope that all these cruelties would disappear, that those hanging like bats below the poverty line would be set free to fly like skylarks. But they had finished him off—who were they? The departing white rulers? No; had they not guarded Gandhi like the apple of their eye! Was it not a group that demanded freedom for religious fanaticism in a newly free country that had killed him? He wiped the tears running down his cheeks. Only two days earlier, for the third time, he had finished reading the pages of Gandhi’s *Sathya Sodhanai*, and they now rolled across the screen of his mind: the Salt Satyagraha—the Dandi March—the Navakali journey—the struggle against racial hatred in South Africa—the Round Table Conference—fasts—

—condemnation of violence—counsel toward the good path—the peaceful smile with which repression was endured—forgetting all this, a lawless gang had laid that great man out as a corpse! A pain as though one path after another were being rolled through his heart seized him. Still walking, he suddenly saw something before him and stopped in surprise. A taxi came to a halt on a rise in that hut-covered area. A young man opened the rear door and got out. The taxi driver said something to him and entered the settlement. Among those huts stood a tiled house—fit for the comparison that, among the stars, that alone was the moon! The driver walked quickly to the house, knocked at the door, and a woman came out. He could see her following behind the driver.

Another rock fell upon the heart already made heavy by Gandhi’s end.

“Wretches! Even today this business?” his heart cried out. A song of sorrow was sounding across the whole world. He hurried toward the taxi, wanting to see at close quarters the human beast who, at such an hour, desired a song of lust.

“Gopi Krishna! Is that you?”

“Good heavens! Gandhidasan? What brings you here? On the hunt like me?”

“Shame! Aren’t you ashamed?”

“What is there to be ashamed of in this matter? A hungry bird comes to peck at a fish!”

“Leave shame aside—don’t you even feel grief? Today the whole world is drowned in sorrow! Above all, the man who made it possible for all of us to walk on free soil has now been made unable to walk anymore. Couldn’t you observe mourning for him at least for this one day?”

Gandhidasan could say no more. He began to sob uncontrollably. Gopikrishnan embraced him and was saying, “Don’t cry! I understand what you feel!” when the taxi driver and the woman came up to them. She had made an angled parting in her hair and tied it in a bun, with a garland of kanakambaram flowers wound around it. The scented face powder she had applied showed in patches in the dim light. A long green mark ran on her forehead; she wore a red sari with white checks. In her hand she held a folded yellow handkerchief.

When the driver opened the rear door for her to sit down, Gandhidasan stepped in and said, “Driver, sir! Wait a little!” Then he turned to the woman and asked,

“Why, amma, Gandhi has been shot today; must you do this wrong even today?”

<!-- source scan 10; printed page 5 -->

“What does it matter to us who gets shot, ayya? This is our livelihood!”

Not only did the answer come at once; the woman turned to the taxi driver and began rattling on: “Hey! Where have you brought and dumped me? Who is this man? Just because somebody died, he wants me to cling to him and cry! Only if I cling to some man every single day can my father, my mother and all the little ones at home fill their stomachs.”

“How much are you being paid for your work tonight, amma?” Gandhidasan asked gently.

“We agreed on fifty and I’ve taken an advance. If he feels like it, he might add another ten or five.”

By then the taxi driver had become angry. Turning to Gopikrishnan he flared up: “What is all this nuisance, sir? Who is this fellow? You bring me to that sort of place, and now he is reciting some holy greatness, while you stand there keeping silent?” Gandhidasan intervened: “Driver, I’ll tell you. I’m his friend. I have stopped what he was going to do today. Here—there are a hundred rupees. Give this to the woman and tell her to go home! The two of us will come in your taxi. Let’s go!” Saying this, Gandhidasan placed a hundred rupees in the driver’s hand. The driver gave it to the woman and,

said, “I’ll come and speak to you later. Go home now,” and was about to drive off. Gandhidasan and Gopikrishnan climbed into the rear seat.

“Where do you want to go, sir?”

“Take us to Marina Beach. After dropping us there, you can wait for us, can’t you?”

“If you pay, certainly, sir!”

Gopikrishnan remained silent. Until they reached the Marina, Gandhidasan too said nothing to him. They got out of the taxi, walked across the sand toward the surf, and sat down in one spot.

“Why, Dasu! I haven’t seen hide or hair of you for so long...”

“After all this time, I never imagined I would see you in a place like that, Gopi!”

“I’ve been hearing about you. They say Gandhidasan has changed into a man worthy of his name. They say he has decided to reform both the men and the women involved in prostitution and roams about like a ghost. They say he goes separately to such men and women, counsels them, reforms them and sets them on a good path. And for women who have no other way and do that work, they say he gets good jobs

for them somewhere and enables them to live decently. Is all that true?”

“I’m doing whatever little I can. Can the whole world be reformed? I have the satisfaction that I have reformed ten people, twenty people, and helped them live properly. Even so, without losing heart, I continue to carry on that service.”

“Yes, but where does the money for all this come from? Didn’t your father die when you were very young?”

“Young? He died when I was still a tiny baby!”

“Your mother is alive, isn’t she? Her name is Sornambikai, right?”

“Yes... She came and went only last month! She works in Bombay at a branch of Indian Bank. I finished college with the money she saved from her salary and sent me. I used to eat and stay here at my aunt’s house. She died before reaching old age; Mother came only to mourn her, stayed for two days, and went back.”

“Poor woman. Your mother was widowed when she was young... She must be about forty now, is she?”

“She would be four or five years older than that. By some good fortune, since Mother had studied, she got a modest job in Bombay at the very bank where my father had worked.”

“All right, Gandhi! Haven’t you tried to get a job yourself?”

“Isn’t that what I’m doing? Every day I stop at least one person from falling into the sewer, save him, bring him away as I brought you here just now, and work at reforming him.”

“Which government pays you a salary for that?”

“Gopi! It is unpaid work. But the money Mother sends me every month is enough for the expenses this work requires.”

“This work of yours is like twisting the mind into a rope. Does your mother actually support it and foolishly keep sending you money as well?”

“Not only with a white sari, Gopi, but with a white heart too, my mother accepts my principles, encourages me and often writes me letters... Here, look—this is the letter Mother wrote me from Bombay today.”

Gandhidasan took the letter from his shirt pocket. The small torch Gopikrishnan had with him helped Gandhidasan read it aloud.

“To my dear son Gandhidasan—Our country has been independent for only six months

now. In these six months, how many problems Mahatma Gandhi has had to take upon his head while struggling to establish unity through the path of non-violence. Contrary to the non-violence he teaches, only two days ago a bomb exploded near the bank where I work. By God’s grace no life was endangered, though property was damaged on a large scale. Do not be frightened after reading this. There is no need to panic that something may happen to the bank building where Mother works. Strong security arrangements have been made to guard the bank building.

“I trust that you will spend the money I have sent you this month too on fulfilling the sacred plan you have taken upon yourself. I do not have absolute faith that it is possible completely to reform both the women who rent out their bodies in the human marketplace and the men who ruin themselves by imagining that a brief pleasure is the bliss of heaven, and thereby create a moral society. Even so, if one draws a pot of water from a polluted pond and boils it, does not at least that one pot of water become pure? When I think that you have taken such cleansing work upon your shoulders and continue to perform it, my heart becomes a freshly blossomed red lotus, my dear boy!

“Valluvar said that prostitutes embrace men who pay them just as one embraces, in a dark room, a corpse whose identity is unknown. There is no use explaining that only to men. You must also tell the woman, ‘For money, are you not forced to embrace a man without feeling, as though embracing a corpse?’ and show her a good path. Three women asked you, ‘Ayya, after listening to you I will leave this profession. What way is there for me to live?’ You spent your own money, found good husbands for them and established orderly lives for them. That is indeed a great achievement.

“Along with this noble work you have begun and continue, should you not arrange some occupation of your own—or a job with a good salary? Would not the money you earn from it also help this work?

“After a few years, should I not come to Chennai and spend my last days with you and with my grandchildren?

“Write back at once! Your loving mother, Sornambikai.”

After finishing the letter, Gandhidasan looked at Gopikrishnan with a faint smile. Even that smile was shadowed by sorrow.

“Gandhi! Your mother writes that she wants to cuddle her grandchildren. Does that mean you are already married?”

“She wrote it that way because she wants me to agree to get married!”

“More than your stopping me, bringing me here and preaching to me, every letter your mother has written feels like a blow from a sledgehammer on my head. We have met after so long, and in such a strange circumstance. Right now, before this very sea, let us make a vow on your mother.”

“What vow, Gopi?”

“That both of us will marry only women who, because of circumstances in life, have slipped and fallen!” The two men clasped hands tightly and shook on it.

With an arm over each other’s shoulders, swaying as they walked across the sand, they returned to the taxi that had been waiting for them for a long time, climbed in, and left.

“This is the hotel where I’m staying,” Gandhidasan said, asking the taxi to stop at the entrance of a simple little building. “Oh, this is the place? I’ll come and see you in the morning,” Gopi said, and after saying so,

Gopikrishna prepared to leave in the same taxi while Gandhidasan counted out money for the fare.

“I’ll pay it myself. Leave the vehicle alone,” Gopi said, and the taxi drove away.

In the hotel reception area, the assistant manager, heavy with sleep, said, “Mr. Gandhidasan! A telegram has come for you from Bombay,” and handed him the telegram sheet. Gandhidasan read it. His head spun, and he was about to faint and collapse right there when the assistant manager caught him. At his shout, staff members came running, revived Gandhidasan and supported him to his room.

“Sornambikai has died of a heart attack. Leave immediately.”

The telegram had been sent by Dr. Vaidyanathan of Matunga, Bombay.

The next morning Gandhidasan boarded the train to Bombay. He also sent a telegram to Dr. Vaidyanathan in Matunga saying that he was coming to see his mother’s face one last time.

The following afternoon he reached Bombay railway station, went straight to Matunga, asked his way to Dr. Vaidyanathan’s address, and entered the house with eyes swollen from crying. There was no sign there of his mother’s mortal remains.

Vaidyanathan received him and said, “Your mother Sornambikai pleaded with me and gave you this house address. But she lived somewhere else.” Gandhidasan’s face changed in astonishment.

“Then how did you know Mother?”

“That? Once a week I am one of the doctors on the list who medically examine all the women in the Grand Road red-light area and give them certificates. That was when your mother begged me to let her give you my home address for your letters.”

Gandhidasan trembled. He struggled, unable to speak.

“Then Mother’s story that she worked here in a bank...?”

“Can any mother proudly tell the son she gave birth to about this kind of work she does? All right, all right! For your sake they have not taken your mother’s body away yet. Here is the address. Go immediately.”

Saying this, Dr. Vaidyanathan wrote on a sheet of paper the address in the Grand Road red-light district, including the house number, and gave it to Gandhidasan.

To see the body of that noble mother who, for sustenance, ate filth while trying to cleanse the water of the tank, he crawled away from there like a snail.
