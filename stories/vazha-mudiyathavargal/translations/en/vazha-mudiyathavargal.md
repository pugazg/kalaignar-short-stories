# Vazha Mudiyathavargal

‘Daan, daan’—the tower clock struck ten times. The doors of Natchathira Talkies opened. The red, green, blue and yellow colours released when the film ‘Thilothama’ ended lost their splendour under the sudden laughter of the mercury lamps. A troop of young people came streaming out, their own luxury still glittering even in that light, singing ‘Kamba rasam’.

“If women are to be given freedom, first all these men must be picked up and thrown into the Arabian Sea,” said one woman, the one in the red sari.

“When the world is about to be destroyed and they ask you for advice, tell them this opinion then,” shot back the woman in the green sari.

“How was Thilothama, da?” asked a twenty-four-year-old.

“Are you asking about the film?...or...” drawled a minor.

“The film is average. Direction is all right. It is the story I didn’t like,” inserted one intellectual into the conversation.

“What, sir, you didn’t like the story?” a cinema-mad fellow interrupted.

“They have made Brahma do kavaattaa, sir,” one man said in sympathy.

“They have taken the puranic story......just as it is.”

“Brahma...one of the Trimurthis. He created Thilothama! If he himself falls in love with her....father and daughter...chee, chee!...absurd!!”

“Hold on, don’t vomit,” said the mocker, ending the exchange that way. With reviews of the Thilothama film, the crowd moved on—scattered—grew smaller and smaller—wore away. Those walking golden beetles......became flying silk moths. The ‘college vedar’ group too turned another way.

Keeping apart instead of joining that crowd, Chinnachami and Kantha walked along. Kantha’s eyes kept circling like a lighthouse beam.

“Hmm......watch where you’re going!” Chinnachami snapped.

Chinnachami was a policeman, about forty. A strong body. A vaatta-matra stride. Broad, rounded shoulders. An upright chest. A dignified appearance. His eyes were always red. Black, kullaatha moustaches.

Unable to match his fast pace, Kantha struggled after him. Along the edge of the long road stood trees casting shade. There was a large tamarind tree. Because of the movement of people, the birds in its branches often padithu their wings. By that tree Chinnasami stepped down from the road. A little distance below the road lay the ‘Police Line’. House number 18 there belonged to Chinnachami.

“Kantha! Look down as you come. There may be insects and such.”

Saying this, he walked a little more slowly, turning to look behind him. On reaching the house, Chinnachami opened the lock and struck a match. Kantha lit the lamp and went toward the adukkalai. We know what a Police Line house is—a sparrow’s nest! Kitchen and bedroom are all one.

Chinnachami took off his sandals, lit a beedi and sat at the doorway. Along with the curling beedi smoke, his thoughts too made a circuit. Just then,

“Appa! I’ve served the rice,” Kantha called, and Chinnachami put out the half-smoked beedi, placed it in the matchbox and went inside.

Good old rice suited to the Margazhi cold; sharp sundakkuzhambu. The meal was over.

“You eat, ma,” said Chinnachami, and he came outside with the rest of the beedi in his mouth. The beedi smoke slightly changed the jeelu-jeeluppu of his stomach.

After Kantha finished eating, she washed the vessels and swept the place where they slept. She spread two torn mats and placed pillows that not only smelled of oil but had turned so black that they seemed to need no covers.

“Appa, shall I go to sleep?”

“Give me a tumbler of hot water. Is there any?”

“Only cold water is there. Where is the firewood to heat water?” she muttered, gave him a cup of water and lay down.

Another beedi settled on the lips of Chinnachami, who sat facing the street. Smoking, he sat without lowering his raised head, and to him,

perhaps the moon in the open sky, playing as it touched the clouds, seemed to be speaking; he remained still without taking his eyes away.

The moon! Even Chinnachami’s wife had been named Chandra. Ten years had passed since she became one with the earth. When Chinnachami was thirty, Chandra died, leaving Kantha—who had not yet completed ten years—motherless. While his father was alive, he had somehow arranged Chinnachami’s marriage to Chandra and then closed his eyes. In between, death stretched out its cruel hands, strangled Chandra’s neck, and left that pitiable policeman with the great responsibility of protecting Kantha. Many times he tried to remarry. How could he marry without even three hundred rupees! Relatives who would not lend a hand when he suffered—when he wept—when he cried that he had no refuge—would come smacking their tongues when there was a wedding. If no invitation was given, they would grind their teeth. In such a state, can a poor man have the extravagance of a marriage?......

There is no need even to ask about a policeman’s salary in those days; daily food itself was difficult. If illness came, the doctor would have to turn into a dharmavaan. If a thorn pierced the foot or something broke, the cart-driver would have to be a frightened man. Whatever extra income came would barely cover coffee and tea-water. Would an ordinary policeman get a ‘kimbala chance’ large enough to marry?

As the grief over his wife’s death faded, the question “Will there be no next marriage?” grew into longing and began dragging Chinnachami’s heart. Vaikasi—Avani—Thai—months rolled over; this year, next year, it kept being postponed, and all the women once considered for him had married and even borne children. If this was his fate, Kantha too, who had reached womanhood

six years earlier and remained at home, had found no husband. Kantha was not disfigured; she was moderately pretty. She was dark. She was a grapevine with a pleasing face and striking form. No one said they disliked the girl; “What will they give as seervarisai? The wedding expense belongs to the girl’s family, doesn’t it?” Unable to answer those questions, Kantha’s budding youth remained unspent. Chinnachami’s heart withered. **Chinnachami was one who could not live. Kantha was one whom he could not enable to live.**

That sorrowful figure, dazed by waves of thought, suddenly came to himself when the beedi ember burned his finger. The moon hid in the clouds. Chinnachami rose with a deep sigh, bolted the door, lowered the lamp a little and lay down on the torn mat. Sorrow spread across his eyes. From the way Kantha stretched and twisted her body in weariness, it was clear that a great struggle must have been taking place in her heart too.

What if a beautiful part of one’s lifetime simply goes to waste?... What if, at the age meant for tender words and celebration, one remains shut away in silence?... What if the youthful days meant for the pleasures fitting a love-song become crippled?... What woman could bear that pain!

In this dark world, the house of a human creature. There, two torn mats. Two hearts torn worse than the mats! Father—daughter. A worm suffering in embers! A tender shoot caught in an unbearable storm of pain!

Kantha’s long sigh, and after that sigh Chinnachami’s movement as he rose with a groan! Alas...a pitiable condition.

—and rosewater baths, almond paste and fresh milk—is that what they asked for? How many young women are sacrificed to the hunger of a rich man’s heart! Men who can throw money and become Indras wherever they wish; and forms of poverty that have salted dry their stomachs, hearts and bodily desires—has human intelligence become so dull that both are called **‘God’s creation’**?” The little house seemed to be shouting this aloud.

Chinnachami’s mind continued speaking:—

“If a man has no money, does he have no life? No feelings? God! Why did you create stomachs and hearts for the poor? If you had skill in the work of creation, should you not have created the poor merely as walking dolls, machines to work for capitalists?

“Even animals roaming in the forest have happiness! Paired pigeons—deer running and playing—cuckoos singing and flying—are the poor lower even than they are? What sin did I commit? Was there any shortcoming in my devotion to you? Did I fail to live in fear of you? Why this trial?”

No god was ready to answer the former man’s pressing questions. His sorrowful anguish made Kantha’s heart too into an oppari and it sobbed. Tears gathered in her eyes, ran down and dried in the hollows of her cheeks.

Nothing was caught in Chinnachami’s throat; even so, he lightly cleared it.

“Emmadi!” Kantha too bent and rolled over with that word, as though tired in sleep.

The dim lamp showed an indecent resolve flowing across his face. He lay down properly. Turned his face the other way. Closed his eyes a little.

“Chinnachami!....small-minded man!.......

What are you doing...wretch!”

A terrifying voice rose inside his own chest.

A minute of silence. Appo! In that one minute, what boiling ran through his blood! All his nerves trembled. There was no moisture on his tongue. His lips dried. Slowly he lifted his hand. Sweat poured into his palm. He wiped it on his veshti and gently placed his hand on Kantha. With a weary breath, she shifted in her sleep.

“Hey!....sinner!....utter chandala!....she is your daughter, da, your daughter!....
Your own daughter!....do not commit this great sin, fool!....
Madman whose head has gone wild!......”

A thousand sledgehammer blows struck his head. Furious punches. Cruel sickle-cuts. Chinnachami’s chest thudded. With a moistureless tongue he licked his dried lips once and lay without speaking. A little silence. Even that silence vanished like lightning. His damp fingers, trembling as though poisoned, fell again upon Kantha’s face and moved like crawling snails. Kantha’s trembling hand lightly caught those fingers.....Was that all? It slowly azhukkiyathu them.

As though many thunders crashed...as though many lightnings flashed...as though a sudden storm rose, an earthquake struck and the seas roared upward...two bodies bound by blood began to shake violently. Chinnachami kicked the lamp near their feet. It toppled, went out, and rolled as oil spilled across the floor.

God—the theevithi created in the name of chastity—the society made victim to that theevithi—the law shaped for that society—the two chandalas who, without knowing the proper way, shattered that law—people unable to live!

Dawn had not properly broken. Dim light entered the house. Chinnachami woke and turned to look at Kantha’s face. His eyes closed without his knowing it. Kantha was sleeping exhausted. Chinnachami rose quickly and came outside; he did not stop, but kept walking like a madman.

It must have been about nine in the morning. Kantha sat leaning at the doorway with her cheek in her hand. The advertising van for the film ‘Thilothama’ was passing along the street.
