# Nalayini

<!-- source opening rule: long horizontal rule directly beneath the story heading -->

<!-- source scan 16; printed page 7 -->

A beautiful woman. A radiant-faced young woman whose form had not withered. Clad in white, she added grace to her swan-like gait. Around her reddish neck hung a black-bead necklace. That maiden, her hair combed and tied, was walking down the street with a basket in her hand. A procession of the distinctive beauty of spotless womanhood! As she walked through the darkened street, one was tempted to wonder whether a star of the heavens, longing to come to the earth below, had descended there!

On the head of that golden statue showering light was a basket; inside the basket, a foul odour! Yes—upon the beauty's head sat a decaying figure. Her young hands, supporting the underside of the basket, trembled under the weight. Yet that lovely woman kept walking. Her beautiful face offered poets a vision of how even sorrow can possess a radiance. The decaying figure seated in the basket, meanwhile, wore a smile. It was a smile blossoming in expectation of some pleasure. Want to see concern in that smile? Those who wish may look at the sweetness of that figure. The figure in the basket was a man—a man who had lost more than the leg portion of the body that had once been his own;

<!-- source scan 17; printed page 8 -->

a maharishi! A great man named **Maut Kalyar**.

Just as there are “great” men, “great” persons, “great” rich men and “great” landlords, there is also such a thing as a “great” disease! That great sage had been afflicted by that great disease. And there he was, riding upon the head of his beautiful wife as he travelled down the street.

“Go on, woman—faster!”

“I am going, my lord!”

“Nalayini! Walk faster! But don't drop me for that! It doesn't matter if you fall!”

“My god! Why do you test me like this? Even if the life leaves my body, I will not lower this basket. Is there merchandise in this basket? Am I carrying household goods? Are you not my priceless jewel! Would I ever put you down? Sacrilege! Sacrilege!”

Bearing her leprous husband in a basket—and bearing sorrow upon a face like a lordly lotus—that walking golden beetle, Nalayini, went on. Where was she going? Did her husband wish to bathe in holy waters and undertake sacred pilgrimage, and had that slender woman taken on this task to satisfy his hunger for devotion? No! No! Then what? Was this journey being made to move from one hermitage to another, with the maiden herself serving as the vehicle? Not that either! Then where were they going? Not to perform penance! Not to witness penance! Then where was the ascetic going? To a courtesan's house! Why? To preach, “Prostitution must not exist! Dāsi-nāthīna-thozhu!”? Not even for that was the leper going. Not to turn the courtesan's house into a hall of devotion! Not to expound upon God! The bearer of the water-pot was going to the courtesan's bedchamber! The sage's desire was not to behold the three-eyed god! It was for four eyes to meet—to pay homage to that Nayanavalli-thathai!

<!-- source scan 18; printed page 9 -->

Nalayini, carrying her husband in search of the house of a woman who gives pleasure for money—a blooming rose! That rose was carrying a decaying beetle to a withered flower! There—the courtesans' street had arrived! Nalayini's bodily strain ended. Her husband's inner frenzy subsided a little. They had reached the place they sought. She lowered the basket. Once, she arched and stretched her aching body.

“A rainbow!” a colour-loving connoisseur nearby might have cried. “Could even a deer leap like that!” another might have said. “Stand bent like that for just one second; that is enough. Even if you ask the whole world as the price, I will paint it,” a painter nearby might have declared! Such a peacock-like woman rose to seek a companion for her husband for the night and approached the house of the fallen woman.

With ornamented hands she knocked on the door. The house opened before she had finished knocking. A face peered out even before it opened. **Mautkalyar** saw that face. “I have found pleasure! I have found pleasure!” he cried like a musician who had found his pitch. A terrible glow came over his wrinkled face! Deep hollows formed by a smile on cheeks that oozed blood! Lust-madness showed itself even on his snarling lips. Stretching out a fingerless hand, he greeted the prostitute with desire. Seeing the leper's excitement, the woman of the house asked, “What is the matter?” “It is all our own private matter!” replied the revered sage.

Nalayini began to speak.

“Woman! This is my husband. I am a wife who never disobeys her husband's word. He wished to come to your house. Queen among women, conduct yourself so that his heart is not disappointed!”

The courtesan was startled by Nalayini's words. ‘This is the first time in my life I have seen a wife bring her own husband to a courtesan's house!’ she marvelled to herself.

<!-- source scan 19; printed page 10 -->

Still astonished, she raised her eyes and looked with disgust at the leper's ugly face. Before she could reach a decision, Nalayini held out a bundle of gold before her. The courtesan signalled “I agree” with a glance. Lovingly taking up the leprous sage from the basket, she carried him away to an **Amsa-thoolika** couch.

With a deep sigh, Nalayini left the house. She placed the basket at the entrance and told the guard, “I will come in the morning,” and went away.

A voice stopped Nalayini as she walked like a dust-covered bouquet. “Nalayini, dear friend! Wait a moment!” A woman came running toward her. She was the beauty from the neighbouring hermitage. Wiping away the tears streaming down her face, Nalayini asked, “Ulaga, is that you! Where have you come from at this hour?”

“I went toward the grove. When I saw you coming, I came over. Yes—Nalayini! I want to ask you something. You won't be angry, will you?” Ulaga began cautiously.

“What is it, Ulaga? Ask freely,” said Nalayini, trying to summon a smile.

“However much love and devotion one may have for one's husband, to take him yourself to a courtesan's house and leave him there... I don't quite...”

“Ulaga! Enough—stop. For women, the husband is god. Fulfilling the husband's desires is the mark of a queen of chastity. My husband may be diseased. But to me he is a honey-sweet fruit, do you understand! Don't teach me how I should behave toward my husband! Look at me and reform yourself.”

“Nalayini! Forgive me! I misunderstood your love—and your devotion to your husband,

<!-- source scan 20; printed page 11 -->

forgive me, Nalayini! Nowhere in all the twice-seven, fourteen worlds could one find a holy woman of such renunciation—a devoted wife like you. You are a chaste wife—you are a chaste wife!”

“Ulaga, it is enough if you understand me—I have nothing to forgive you for. Be a queen of chastity like me! Follow wives like me! I am going.”

Saying this, Nalayini went toward her hermitage. Ulaga too, realizing her mistake, moved away chanting, “Nalayini is a chaste wife.” At that moment a young man ran up and covered Ulaga's eyes from behind. Laughing freely, the two of them disappeared toward the grove nearby!

Sobbing uncontrollably, Nalayini entered the hermitage and fell face-down upon the ground. Waterfalls poured from her deep blue sacred eyes. Her loosened black hair spread over her face like a cloud hiding the full moon.

<!-- source display structure: enlarged initial `ச` marks the paragraph opening `சோலையின்...` -->

A song from the direction of the grove drifted into the hermitage. Still crying, Nalayini listened. Yes—it was Ulaga's voice! Along with it another male voice was singing! ‘Ulaga, who accepted me as a chaste wife—who pressed my hands to her eyes—is in the fruit grove with her lover, with her illicit love, singing a song of pleasure. She is my age; I am more beautiful than she is. Yet I lie here with no one of my own. Her lover must be describing her as “honey! deer!”’

Nalayini thought all that could be thought in this way. Again she wept. The hermitage door opened. She stopped crying, but did not wipe her eyes. A woman opened the door and entered. Nalayini and the newcomer looked almost exactly alike;

<!-- source scan 21; printed page 12 -->

if they stood together, it would be difficult to decide, “Which one is Nalayini?” The woman who had come in sat beside Nalayini. Nalayini too sat up. “Come, Idhaya, come!” she welcomed her between sobs.

“Nala! Why do you keep crying like this all the time?”

“You ask for the reason, Idhaya! Don't you know that I suffer like a worm in burning embers? Can you too hear the music of those birds calling in pairs? Don't you know what pangs of separation a lover's veena-song from the grove can awaken in a young woman in the bloom of life! You know! Idhaya! You know everything! Even though you know, you remain silent like this only to console me—isn't that so!”

“It is true, Nalayini! Ulaga praised you as a chaste wife; I too heard it while standing hidden. You must be content with the light of that fame alone!”

“Idhaya! Tell me truthfully! Am I a chaste wife? How? How?”

“Yes! You sent your leprosy-stricken husband to a courtesan's house!”

“Don't mock me, Idhaya! I have no desire to lie with a man afflicted by leprosy. Yet he calls me to him with desire. My hand trembles even to embrace him. That is why I sent him to a courtesan's house. I thought it would be enough if his desire were satisfied without danger coming to my beauty. Don't you know this trick, Idhaya!”

“Is there anything I do not know, Nalayini! But Ulaga believes in your devotion to your husband! She thinks carrying the leper on your head to a courtesan's house was supreme devotion to one's husband.”

<!-- source scan 22; printed page 13 -->

“You too say I carried him on my head! Correct yourself! I put him in a basket and carried the basket on my head. Am I mad, to touch a leper with my hands and carry him on my head? I was careful to put him in the basket before carrying him.”

“Nala! Even when your husband uses the harshest words, Ulaga was praising you to me just yesterday as a queen of chastity who never answers back.”

“When my husband abused me, I did not reply—that is true! I dismissed it, thinking, ‘It is only a leper who is abusing me.’ If I too spoke to him, the conversation would grow. Because of those words, I would have to stand close to him and talk. That is why, girl, I let that diseased fellow go on ranting!”

“Hmm... somehow you have earned the title of chaste wife! Ulaga has praised you as a wife renowned throughout the world—as one overflowing with devotion to her husband!”

“What does Ulaga know about the volcano inside my heart? Is the title ‘chaste wife’ enough for this pitiable woman whose youth is dripping ripe? Tell me, Idhaya! Tell me! Show me a path to a life of pleasure!”

“I will show you a path, Nalayini! I grant you a new boon. Finish this birth quickly and take another birth soon! In that birth you are going to be born as Draupadi! Though you have one husband now, you have neither excitement nor delight. Your heart has become a field of pain! So in your next birth I grant you the wonderful boon of living a joyful life with five husbands.”

“Idhaya! Is this true?”

<!-- source scan 23; printed page 14 -->

“No lie! A new life is beginning for you! New life—rebirth—both mean the same thing! Understand that, my friend!”

“I understand!” cried Nalayini, leaping with delight as she embraced Idhaya. Her tears of sorrow had turned into tears of joy! In that hermitage, a song of pleasure began!
