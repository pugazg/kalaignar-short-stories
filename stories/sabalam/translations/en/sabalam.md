# Sabalam

<!-- source opening rule: long horizontal rule directly beneath the story heading -->

<!-- source scan 24; printed page 15 -->

Cutting through the wind, the ‘Indo Ceylon Express’ was flying toward Tiruchirappalli. Those who had boarded the train in Chennai at eight o'clock that night were still sitting hunched over, fighting the cold. At the Chennai railway station there were many little boys who rented out places to sit or sleep. They had grown accustomed to making their living that way.

To those who had boarded the train—or were wandering around the Egmore platform wondering whether they might find a place—a little fellow would appear, like God becoming manifest in a story. “Sir, come here—there's a good place,” he would say. Follow him and that was enough: he would show you a fine place inside the carriage, pick up the ‘silk mattress’ he had spread on the bench, and tie it around his head as a turban. To sleep in that place—apart from the ticket fare—you paid him four annas in rent. Many such boys ran this rental business. Passengers who had fallen into their eyes and become objects of their mercy were sleeping soundly. Many who had not received that blessing, unable to resist the sleep that entered their eyes and tugged at their eyelids to close them, kept bumping against one another. With no strength left in the neck to hold them upright,

<!-- source scan 25; printed page 16 -->

their hanging heads rested against their knees, the shoulder of the person beside them, or the window frame. It was a pitiable sight.

Even in this ‘world of Kumbhakarna’, there were a few who remained wide awake. At least one in a hundred could be found. In the sixth compartment, many were snoring with difficulty, some even stretching their legs a little out through the windows. At the end of a long bench sat a man who, without concern for anything around him, kept looking this way and that in some strange agitation and glancing under the bench. Opposite him, on another bench, sat another young man with his eyes closed. His eyes were closed, but it was clear that he was not asleep. Even his eyelids were only three-quarters shut. Through the remaining quarter his gaze moved back and forth between the young man sitting opposite and a woman lying under the bench.

He and Chandira had boarded in Chennai with their child. A short while after they sat down, the sleepless young man came and sat opposite them. He had not happened upon that place by chance. He had inspected every carriage once, then a second time, before finally boarding here.

The child in Chandira's arms seized the grey hair of an old woman nearby with its tender little hands. The old woman freed her head from the child, scattering a toothless smile. Nor did she stop there. It is the nature of old women, is it not, to keep talking about something—good or bad? And if it is a child, need one even ask? “What is your name, dear?” the old woman cooed. “Moorthi,” the

<!-- source scan 26; printed page 17 -->

child pronounced in a ringing voice. “What's your father's name?” asked the old woman. “My father's name is Kumares. What's your name?” the child asked. The old woman's delight knew no bounds. Kumares and Chandira looked at each other and smiled. At that moment a laugh burst from the opposite bench too. Chandira noticed it. Even while smiling, the man who had laughed was staring straight at her.

The moment Chandira saw him, she started and froze. But she steadied herself and turned her attention to the child's play. Yet from time to time her small blue eyes kept swallowing the young man opposite. He was handsome. Wide eyes; a budding moustache; a gleaming silk shirt that added to his looks. Whenever his large eyes looked at Chandira, they seemed intoxicated. Chandira too, as though forgetting that her husband sat right beside her, kept looking at him again and again. Kumares understood this from the signs. The train went on. Chandira talked now and then with her husband, played with the child, and at the same time kept looking at the man opposite. Kumares's heart seethed with the urge to say something. His wife—sitting right beside him—admiring another man's beauty... he could scarcely bear the thought.

It was for her beauty that he had married Chandira. After she came to his home, he had rejoiced to discover that she was also full of intelligence. Their family life had been running along happily; this train journey alone had come like an obstacle in its path. Such was the battle in his heart. But Chandira did not stop casting glances at the opposite bench. Kumares boiled inside. His decision to watch everything to the end and come to a conclusion was what kept him quiet.

The child had fallen fast asleep. The train was nearing Villupuram. The people in that compartment

<!-- source scan 27; printed page 18 -->

were swaying in drowsiness.

From the mouth of Moorthi's new railway grandmother came a ‘poos, poos’ breathing sound. Chandira tried to put the child down to sleep. She took the towel from Kumares's shoulder, spread it under the bench, and lay down there herself. She placed the child beside her. Even as she lay down, she gave Kumares her usual gentle smile before settling. But Kumares did not smile back. How could she know what was raging in his heart?

The man opposite now had to lower his head. What special beauty he saw in Chandira's face as she tried to sleep with her eyes closed, no one knew. He simply drank in the sight! He could not sit still. What would happen if a plate heaped with pongal were placed before a man starving with hunger...? That was his state. The fact that his mind had no steadiness flashed plainly in every movement. Chandira's occasional glance had already spun him like a top. That day had brought him a fine opportunity indeed. He had searched carriage after carriage and found nothing like this; and now, as though he had aimed at a star and the moon itself had fallen into his hands, he had found this place. He admired the sleeping Chandira—admired her—admired her without end!

At this very stage, Kumares was watching everything with three-quarters of his eye closed. He waited feverishly for Chandira to be caught red-handed. The man opposite kept looking at Kumares, gently touching her hand with his foot, then looking all around and sitting still again.

The train had passed Nellikuppam. Chandira was sleeping deeply. Her golden arm lay around the child Moorthi. The young man let out a deep breath with some newfound courage. He looked sharply at Kumares too. Ready for his detective work, Kumares pretended to be overcome by sleep. The young man's hands stroked Chandira's cheek. Chandira woke. Trembling, he withdrew his hands. The speed with which Chandira woke made the child whimper too. Chandira raised herself and looked at her husband. He appeared to be sleeping heavily. She did not see the terrible eyes hidden in the narrow slit between his eyelids, waiting to reach a conclusion. With the same eyes that had looked at her husband, she looked at the young man. He swallowed. She kissed the whimpering child, patted it, and went back to sleep.

Kumares had seen her kiss the crying child and put it to sleep countless times before. Yet this kiss was like pouring oil into the fire consuming him.

A little time passed. The young man's hands touched Chandira's cheek again. Chandira woke. Still patting the child, she closed her eyes once more. Kumares could no longer remain seated there. He had become a volcano! His heart became an earthquake! He rose with a terrible decision. He lifted the child into his arms; the child cried. Chandira woke, saw the child in her husband's arms, and began to drift back to sleep. Carrying the child, Kumares moved five or six benches away. The young man's joy overflowed.

The train ran on with the speed of a storm, breathing heavily. Kumares looked all around. Into that dense darkness, through the window,

<!-- source scan 29; printed page 20 -->

he flung the child from his hands. In what forest did that tender little one fall? On what rock did it strike and break into a hundred pieces?

Kumares's mind became a little calmer. ‘My connection with an adulterous woman ends today. So that the child will not carry my name and waste the future, the child too is gone. From now on I am alone. Let this courtesan go with him!’ his mind said. He waited for the next station where the train would stop.

Meanwhile his eyes went back to where he had been sitting. The young man's hands now moved toward Chandira's cheek without trembling. With uncontrollable desire he pressed the fullness of her cheek with his fingers. Chandira woke with a start. Now he smiled. With a loud cry of “Ee!” Chandira sprang up. Her hands played back and forth across the young man's cheeks. Everyone woke. Kumares too came near. He felt as though he would faint. “Aththaan!” Chandira cried, embracing him.

“From the beginning, the way you kept looking...” Kumares began angrily, meaning to say something more.

“He looked like my elder brother who died when I was little... that is why I looked... this sinner...” she sobbed. The people in the compartment cursed the young man thoroughly.

Cuddalore station arrived. That young ‘minor’, seized by sabalam, got down and left. “These fellows board trains only for this,” a voice remarked.

In the sudden shock Chandira had even forgotten the child. “Where is the child, aththaan?” she asked anxiously.

“It is nearby; get down here,” said Kumares. His face had darkened.

<!-- source scan 30; printed page 21 -->

“Why?” she asked.

“Get down—I'll tell you!” Kumares said, lifting the bundles and setting off. She too got down. The moment she did, she asked, “Where is the child?”

With tears streaming down his face, he answered only this: “It is two miles behind us.”

“Hey! Hasty Kumares!”

“Hot-headed fool!”

Those cries rang in his ears like the crack of whips from all four directions.

The ‘Indo Ceylon Express’ departed from Cuddalore.
