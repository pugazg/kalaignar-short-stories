# Nanbana?

Dear Sekar! You must have received my wedding invitation. I expected you to come for certain; I was disappointed. You have taken revenge on me for not attending your wedding. You know there was no fault of mine in that. But even though five years have passed since your wedding, it is indeed my fault that I still have not met you and offered my congratulations. Circumstances conspired against us. The struggle to earn a living separated you and me by a great distance. Since you married a woman employed in the city of Delhi, your family must be moving along without being shaken on the economic front. You lucky fellow! The woman I have got also once lived in Delhi. She works for a company in Chennai. Through her, three hundred rupees come in every month. Are you thinking, “Not bad at all, the rascal!”?

Hey, Sekar! At least send me a photograph of the two of you! You cannot have been sitting idle all these years, can you? Put the caption ‘Two Blossoms, One Bud’ on it

<!-- source scan 105; printed page 95 -->

and send me your family photograph! Only one bud, right? Or perhaps five or six?

I know very well how angry you must be while reading this. “The fellow who did not come to my wedding—not only that, the fellow who did not even bother all these years to ask whether I was alive or dead—now that he has tied a log to himself, he acts as though he has suddenly acquired a great sense of family responsibility and friendship!” I can almost hear your rough lips muttering all that. The last time you came to Chennai, I was in Sri Lanka. The moment I heard that you had come, I hurriedly finished even my work and came rushing back from Sri Lanka.

But by then, it seems, you had seen the anti-Hindi agitations taking place in Tamil Nadu, become frightened, and rushed away to Delhi! I wrote to you at once. That letter I wrote praising your bravery deserves a place among the poems of the Purananuru. If you missed it, please rummage through your old rubbish and look for it.

“Marriage should not exist!
It is a shackle on the leg!
A prison that snatches away human rights!
A lock on happiness!
A ticket to Maanalogam!”

I, your dearest friend who used to speak so bitterly in that fashion, have changed my mind and set foot on the threshold of marriage, heading toward the place called family—

<!-- source scan 106; printed page 96 -->

I have entered its corridor. Could you not at least have come to my wedding just to make fun of me for that? Did you not even have the curiosity to ask me, “How on earth did this change come about?” and hear the details?

“To punish those who have done you harm,
do them good, so that they are shamed.”

Did you forget this Kural saying as soon as you went to Delhi? Then why, my friend, did you take revenge on me like this? If you could not come, could you not at least have sent a greeting? Your anger is with me! Could you not have sent wedding wishes at least to my wife—that is, to your younger sister? Let it go. At least write a reply to this.

Your loving friend  
Babu

□ □ □

Within a day or two, a reply came from Sekar to Babu. It said:

Dear Babu! I received the wedding invitation you sent with the bride’s photograph. Your letter followed it. You ask whether I could not at least have sent wishes to my younger sister! How am I supposed to send them? Does Saradha, who divorced me, immediately become my younger sister?

Your friend  
Sekar
