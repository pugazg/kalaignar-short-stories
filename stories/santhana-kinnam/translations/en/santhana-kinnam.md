# Santhana Kinnam

> **English translation of the verified Tamil archival reading.** Controlling source: 1977 anthology `கலைஞர் கருணாநிதியின் சிறுகதைகள்`, scans **47–56**, printed pages **38–47**. The canonical Tamil remains authoritative. Page-boundary comments are retained for traceability.

The leader began to speak. He praised the bride, like a new moon, and the bridegroom sitting there with joy overflowing. He warmly congratulated the young man who had lit the flame of rationalism in that rustic village where the footprints of antiquity had still not faded. Hearing the leader praise the lover who was to bring light into her life, the new bride, seated there in shyness, felt her heart become a stream of delight.

The leader poured out explanation after explanation on why priestly marriage was unnecessary and how revolutionary marriage had grown. The bees who had gathered there drank that honey of sweet Tamil and rejoiced.

He also explained what a well-matched marriage meant and what age was necessary and suitable for marriage.

With evidence, he mocked the claim that old and new should never clash.

There is a people called Dravidian. Evil people utter “Dravidian” as an insult. He gave evidence to answer them: “Dravidian indeed—for the evil ones!”

<!-- source scan 48; printed page 39 -->

In Tagore's song with the phrase “Dravida utkala vanga”; in the *Manonmaniam* author's line “the southern land, and within it the noble sacred Dravidian land”; in the history book titled *History of the Dravidians*; in the Mahabalipuram sculptural inscription saying it was carved “in the Dravidian style in the time of Mamallan”; and in the title “Dravida Vidyabhushanam” bestowed on Saminatha Iyer—the leader explained, in a breeze-like style, how the honey-flower called “Dravidian” spread its fragrance.

He urged the bride and bridegroom to come forward to protect such a Dravidian land through the poetic lines: “In love and in poetry, speech that goes to the field—Dravidian itself the breath of husband and wife!”

Then, describing the bends that cross the path of life, he encouraged them to possess the courage to bear anything. He showed that family life was not a childhood game of building a sand house and placing a wooden doll inside it, and blessed them again and again: “White doves beating their wings to fly in the sky of life! May you live and receive every abundance!” He declared that many mothers like those who move through the *Purananuru* were needed. He polished a beautiful poem told concisely and vividly in the *Purananuru* and rendered it in simple language like a song. That song itself was a story.

<!-- source enlarged initial: கு in குடிசைதான் marks the opening of the long poem -->

A hut—on one side, sharp spear and sword,  
set in rows,  
as though forged weapons  
to seize the world  
and finish fierce enemies—gleaming, flashing.  
A tiger's cave has no ornament; nothing novel in that!  
Fear, shuddering flesh, and the nature of stooping low  
never show their faces there: the dwelling of honour,  
the warrior's mansion!

<!-- source scan 49; printed page 40 -->

At the doorway of the house, with rice in a bowl,  
mixing in a little jaggery and sending it into her belly,  
an old grey-haired woman  
opened her toothless mouth  
and put in a handful of food;  
a warrior came running. “One piece of news, grandmother,” he said.  
“You are panting like a little girl who has danced her way here—  
are you a man, little brother?  
Let there be a pause between one breath and the next—then  
begin your speech!” said that  
old Tamil woman who had gone to war with *kandal*.

“This is no time for jokes, grandmother; leave  
your usual teasing.  
Your son died on the battlefield,” he said. Her heart  
broke; she straightened herself once.

“When playing dice, pieces are cut down—  
the battlefield is the same.  
Was the wound on his chest or on his back? Tell me,” she said.  
“On his back,” he said.  
The old woman trembled, her heart burst,  
she took up a sword,  
and sped toward the direction from which the war-drum sounded!

“I gave milk to a coward—lying  
face-down, fallen,  
and that *mozhaikku* bears the name warrior! Once, long ago,  
when a spear came flying,  
my undying husband showed his chest  
and lay fallen after answering it.  
Was this one born to him? Alas! Where is honour?  
He has fallen lower than a crumbling wall.  
At the Himalaya's boundary valour laughs; here  
music trembles on the strings of the veena;  
and even that cries out honour, honour!  
Are you of a dishonourable line  
that lives on liquor and sura, you wretch?

<!-- source scan 50; printed page 41 -->

You have scattered stain upon the martial Tamil clan!  
I gave you my breast; raised you as my son—  
where are those shoulders that grew fat and arrogant?  
Did they not itch for battle? Alas!  
Coward who could not show the spear its path! Show me the way  
to the milk you drank from my heroic breast!”  
So wailed the poor old woman nearing eighty.  
She went there to the battlefront and turned over  
the young bulls of classical Tamil lying scattered. There  
a flood of blood ran to protect our Tamil land!  
She walked panting among the heaps of corpses.  
There was not such joy even at the wedding pavilion;  
even when her son was born, joy had its limit.  
He lay dead, **showing his chest to the spear!**  
She saw it—her heart cooled.  
“Whatever else I see now, I have no grief;  
my son died a hero!” she said.  
“I was ready to cut away the breast from which he drank;  
alas!  
where is the scoundrel who lied before knowing the truth?  
Here is my sword! Where is his tongue?”—

The leader finished this heroic song. The wedding pavilion shook with applause! When the clapping subsided, the leader said with enthusiasm, “I did not write this song. This bridegroom sitting here wrote it!” Again the pavilion shook! The young cheeks of the new dove beside him turned rose-red. Who could know how her heart blossomed? The leader finished his congratulatory speech. The ceremony of presenting gifts began. Seated in that pavilion was one person with a power to understand the bride's heart even more deeply than the bridegroom beside her. That person was the bride's friend Vijaya. Kamala and Vijaya had been inseparable not only in college but from their youth. Vijaya was already travelling the road of married life. Kamala was stepping onto it only that day, holding Kandan's hand. That her friend had found a loving companion worthy of scholars'

<!-- source scan 51; printed page 42 -->

praise gave Vijaya immeasurable happiness! She had forgotten herself in that joy. Only Vijaya could understand the way Kamala looked at her with sidelong fish-like eyes whenever her husband was praised on the wedding dais. The giving of gifts was nearing its end. Vijaya, who had forgotten everything in the flood of happiness, suddenly rose. In agitation she hurried to the wedding dais and opened a rolled sheet in her hand.

<!-- source display block: Vijaya's seven-line gift inscription is visually inset and isolated from the surrounding prose -->

“Dear Kamala...! You are fortunate.  
Accept this, as a sign  
of our love! Whatever hardship may come,  
do not forget this token  
of affection! This sandal bowl—the sign  
that your heart is a bowl  
filled with happiness!”

Saying this, she embraced Kamala. Tears of joy poured from her eyes. Kamala too was moved to tears by happiness. The opening festival of life had blossomed.

<!-- source enlarged initial: கு in குடும்பம் marks the new paragraph -->

Family life began in delight. Kamala was somewhat possessed by old-fashioned ways. For some days the quarrels between the lovers arose from clashes between old and new. Then both of them began to mock the gods.

“Devotees carry Paramasivan and Parvati every day in a palanquin to the bedchamber...! Yet apart from Ganapati and Murugan they never had any other children! Why?” Kandan would ask.

“Don't you know? They practised birth control!” Kamala would laugh.

“The white man saw fish in the sea; we too saw them.........” Kandan would begin.

<!-- source scan 52; printed page 43 -->

“The white man saw fish and made the submarine; we made the Matsya avatar!” Kamala would finish.

Thus their happy life grew. Though Kandan's mother rejoiced at the sight of this new couple, anger slowly began to rise in her: “Kamala too talks like this, insulting God! Why do those who ought to remain by the hearth need all this trouble?” Afraid that openly rebuking her would hurt her son, she began to rebuke Kamala indirectly through hints.

Whenever such criticism arose, Kamala would console herself by thinking of her lover's sweet face. Not only that: her friend Vijaya had often told her, “Do not behave in a way that pains the elders at home! Adjust yourself and live with them.”

Kamala would remember those golden words. At once she would run and take the loving gift Vijaya had given—the sandal bowl—and press it to her face. Along the rim of the sandal bowl she would see the living lips of her dearest friend; in the shine of the bowl she would see her friend's laughter and spring up in delight. When her lover was away from home, the sandal bowl itself was Kamala's companion in conversation! Even when the mother-in-law's anger toward the daughter-in-law came strongly into speech, she would not say too much. Her childhood friend and school companion Vijaya would show her cheek from inside the bowl and keep speaking secret words to Kamala. The letters “Vijaya” shining on the bowl would tell Kamala so many old stories; Kamala would close her eyes and listen to those happy tales.

<!-- source enlarged initial: தி in திடீரென்று marks the transition to the language-agitation passage -->

Suddenly sparks flew across the country. Those who sought to destroy Tamil gathered an army. The people who had ruled the land set out to stop them. The imperialism of the northern Hindi language was imposed upon the Dravidian land. Against it, the language struggle flared up with heroic fury.

<!-- source scan 53; printed page 44 -->

“The flowering grove where the mango-cuckoo sings is the prison that seeks to lock us in!”—crying out this verse, the Tamil battalion set forth. Lions entered prison. The cruel mouth of repression swallowed many Tamil mothers together with their infants. Two who went to protect the language—the great warriors Thalamuthu and Natarasan—were made unable even to preserve their own breath by the Hindi fanatics. Across Tamil Nadu: boiling anger—anguish—a storm!

<!-- source enlarged initial: க in கமலா marks the return to Kamala's narrative scene -->

Kamala stood looking toward the street through the window. The sandal bowl was in her hand. She had finished talking to it; now she was waiting for Kandan to return from the office.

Her eyes had begun to ache; Kandan was nowhere to be seen. With a sigh she tried to move away from the window. In the quiet street a wave of sound arose. In excitement Kamala looked through the window. A Tamil procession came marching, singing its war-song: “Long live Tamil! Down with Hindi! Long live the fame of Thalamuthu and Natarasan!” An old woman like the aged Avvai walked majestically, carrying a flag; spectators marvelled at how the crescent-bent hunch of her back seemed to straighten as she led the procession. A tall, broad-shouldered man who made one think that Cheran Senguttuvan himself had arrived—fearless of heart—strode as the commander of the force. The thunderous cries of those tiger-like people filled Kamala with courage.

She remembered the poem the leader had spoken on her wedding day: “In love and in poetry, speech that goes to the field! Dravidian itself the breath of husband and wife!” A voice suddenly startled the young woman turning in waves of memory. “Long live Tamil!”—in the gait of a breeze! “Down with Hindi!”—in the gait of a storm! The voice rising in both gaits was Kandan's! Kamala ran outside. Kandan,

<!-- source scan 54; printed page 45 -->

holding a flag in his hand, marched fiercely in that procession. Kamala ran into the crowd and caught hold of Kandan, stopping him. Kandan understood nothing. Alas, he worried that Kamala might give the ideological fighters in the crowd a wrong impression about him. Kamala demanded angrily:

“Who told you to go to the anti-Hindi protest?”

Kandan stared at her on hearing this. But Kamala had not finished her sentence......only now did she finish it: “......leaving me behind?”

The moment they heard, “Who told you to go to the anti-Hindi protest, leaving me behind!”, the old woman ran over and embraced Kamala. Again the Tamil slogans continued. The procession passed beyond that street. The police pounced—like a tiger into a herd of spotted deer! Gunshot sounds, cries of pain. The war-drum cry: “Long live Tamil!” Official arrogance welcomed them all to the prison with a command to “Come!”

Inside the prison Kamala and Kandan murmured, in *mohanam*, “Dravidian itself the breath of husband and wife!”

The court of repression weakened. The Hindi fanatics trembled, thinking, “Our effort will no longer succeed.” Compulsory Hindi shut up shop! The Tamil force won. Heroes sang victory processions; compulsory Hindi, like the proverb's donkey worn down to a mere stump, came to nothing! The language struggle was won!

<!-- source enlarged initial: க in கமலாவும் marks the post-struggle return-home paragraph -->

Kamala and Kandan returned home wearing garlands of victory upon the broad shoulders that had defended Tamil. An office order had already reached the house before him: “You took part in useless work; there is no job for you any more!” His mother was a smoking volcano.

“Mother, if harm came to you, would I not leap up? It was the same, mother—I opposed those who insulted my mother tongue. Is that a crime, mother?”

<!-- source scan 55; printed page 46 -->

Kandan wept. The mother was angrier with her daughter-in-law than with her son.

No job—no way to find a job. A life of pain.  
Jobless Kandan devoted himself full-time to movement work. He was the secretary in that town. There was no one who did not know Kandan. His fame grew. The movement was holding a public meeting in the town. He had made all the arrangements. Twenty rupees had to be sent to cover the speaker's travel expenses.

No money had been collected. He agonized that the meeting must not be stopped. He wandered restlessly at home.  
In prison Kamala's health had deteriorated for lack of proper food and protection. Poverty made the illness continue. There was no money to buy the medicine the doctor prescribed. They had already sold and eaten through the silver articles in the house. Only the sandal bowl remained among the silver! “Sell that at least and buy her medicine!” said his mother. He could not bring himself to sell it. That sandal bowl had been given by his wife's friend. So he refused. But now, when there was no money to send the speaker, his eyes sought that bowl. Sick Kamala was asleep. His hands trembling, Kandan took the sandal bowl. He pawned it and sent the money he received to the speaker.

<!-- source enlarged initial: கூ in கூட்டம் marks the final public-meeting action paragraph -->

The meeting was arranged as planned. Kandan set out for the railway station to receive the speaker! As he left, his mother was scolding Kamala. He listened carefully. The accusation was that Kamala had lost the sandal bowl. Suffering with fever, Kamala said nothing and wept. She shed tears thinking that her dear Vijaya had disappeared. Wiping his own eyes, Kandan ran to the railway station. The train arrived. But the speaker who was supposed to attend the meeting did not arrive. Without moving,

<!-- source scan 56; printed page 47 -->

Kandan stood there and burst into sobs. If someone who had promised to come for a matter concerning his own household failed to come, that would not matter. But this was public work—he anguished over how he could deceive the public after collecting money from them. He went to the platform and asked the people for forgiveness. The crowd dispersed, murmuring.

He returned home. Kamala's fever had grown worse. The light in her eyes had faded. His mother sat nearby and wept. Kandan ran in crying, “Kamala! Kamala!”

She slowly opened her mouth. Her lips moved: “Athan... that sandal bowl...”

“Kamala, I was the one who stole it and pawned it—we can redeem it when money comes,” he cried. Amid his cries Kamala closed her eyes. She never opened them again. Kandan wept; he went on weeping!

<!-- source enlarged initial: இ in இப்போது marks the retrospective closing section -->

Now Kandan is no longer poor. Even though he is not a rich man, he has enough money.

The sandal bowl that he pawned for twenty rupees can be redeemed by paying thirty rupees with interest.

<!-- source standalone transition line -->

But,

after redeeming it, to whom can he give it? Is Kamala there for him to give it to her? The prison of the Hindi fanatics had slowly, slowly killed her, refusing to release its hold even after her release!

Kandan is ready to redeem the sandal bowl. When? When, after it is recovered, another victory festival in the struggle for the ideal brings an occasion to offer sandal paste to the fighters—then!
