# The Mother Who Sold the Child She Bore

At the wedding pavilion, Varadachari, who chanted the mantras, did not forget to bless the couple with “the growth of progeny.” As he ladled ghee into the fire again and again, he pronounced “may you be blessed with sons” only in Sanskrit. Not even by mistake did he say it in Tamil. The blessing certainly bore fruit—but there was no child to be seen in the Zamindar’s household. One year? Two?... Nine years!

Lady Shenbagavalli’s husband was the Zamindar of Sethubhavanam. To him she was a laughing champak blossom; to her he was a treasure. Their life entered a flower garden as though spring and the breeze had signed a pact to live together. They never imagined that a cyclone could blow through the grove. The marriage had been arranged with great speed not merely because Shenbagavalli was beautiful, but because Varadachari, after examining her horoscope, had declared her fortunate and blessed with an imperishable marriage bond.

“What do you lack, Zamindar? You have everything in abundance. If only Bhagavan would grant you the blessing of a son!”

This was the refrain Varadachari sang whenever he met the Zamindar.

“‘The flute is sweet; the lute is sweet,’ say those who have not heard the babbling speech of their own children.”

Chellaiah Pandithar, who had “created” the sacred history of the Shiva temple at Sethubhavanam, never forgot the Zamindar’s financial help. This was the Tirukkural he would recite whenever he saw him.

“The pot-procession for the Kaliamman temple festival—the protective thread has been tied. You must help! Only if something is done for Mother... will a child be born. The lineage will grow.”

This was the veiled threat made by a festival collector as he held out a collection notice to the Zamindar.

“Master! Help this poor man. Your children and little ones will all prosper.”

A beggar’s blessing at the doorway! It gave the Zamindar one more chance to mutter, “Blind fool.”

“You got a match worth gold, girl—a fine husband. You have everything, yet at the proper time you won’t give even one little child, you wretched God!”

Thus did Shenbagavalli’s mother worship God.

“Nine years have passed and there’s still nothing, Shenbu!”

Thus did a friend tease her.

“Won’t the Lord grant us a child to carry our name?”

“Our lineage ends with us! I too have performed every kind of charity... Hmm; who knows what God has in mind?”—a bundle of sorrow shed by the Zamindar and Shenbagavalli.

They had bathed in the sea at Kanyakumari, bowed before Kashi Vishveshvara, fallen at the feet of men wrapped in saffron, and begged for the boon of a child. After tiring themselves out in search of blessings, all that remained was the label that she was barren. As the Zamindar’s age rose, so did his grief.

A vast and splendid estate, beautiful bungalows, a fine motorcar, servants.

“There is no child of my own to enjoy all this status”—he would think. One could not say merely “often”; every day this yearning echoed through the inner apartments.

One day Varadachari met the Zamindar. The result?

Decorated pavilions... fire-pits everywhere... Brahmins seated around them... pots of ghee for the fire... echoes of “namaha” mantras...

A single flood of devotion.

Thirty-three crore gods, forty-eight thousand rishis, Gandharvas, Kinnaras, Kimpurushas—“etcetera, etcetera!”—danced on the priest’s tongue. The Lady gave away pitambaras, silk saris and cows, and the Brahmin worthies departed with their entourages after receiving them.

The spectacle was beautiful. The gifts were many. The expense was certainly great. But must the result of the putrakameshti sacrifice be “zero”? The Zamindar floated in sorrow.

One day there was a meeting between Marudhamuthu, priest of the Mariamman temple, and the Zamindar. The result...

He endowed the family goddess Kamalayi with a pair of standing lamps and fashionable new diamond ornaments.

The Zamindar began to lose faith even in divine power.

“There is a God, there isn’t a God... let us settle it once and for all, Zamindar! Let God’s ways be mysterious, but if we simply sit still, will anything happen?”

“What am I to do?”

“One thing occurs to me.”

“What?”

“Do you know what happened in Dasharatha’s putrakameshti sacrifice?”

“What happened... Dasharatha’s wives, with some rishis...”

“Chi... chi... not that Ramayana!”

“They say that is how it is in Valmiki’s Ramayana.”

“Valmiki or whoever—look at the Ramayana sung by our Tamil man Kambar!”

“That... would a human being read it, sir?”

“What?”

“That Tamil is beyond us.”

“I’ll tell you, listen! Divine beings emerged from the fire... gave the avirppagam—the sacrificial portion—to Dasharatha, and he gave it to his wives... and embryos formed in their wombs.”

“Avirppagam?... Nothing like that happened in our putrakameshti sacrifice!”

“That is exactly the point. Nobody knows the secret of the avirppagam. There is a wonderful way to prepare it without waiting for God’s grace.”

“What are you saying, Swami?”

“I have discovered the method for making avirppagam.”

“Is that so? Amazing!”

“Amazing indeed. Success assured! Results in your very hand.”

“Truly?”

“I have decided to prepare the avirppagam myself.”

“You?”

“What water, what fire! Just watch my skill!... Zamindar! Avirppagam is a rare divine lehyam, a sacred medicine. Filled completely with God’s gracious glance, it becomes like panchamirtham.”

“Why have you not made it until now?”

<!-- anthology scan: 11; printed page: 9 -->

“Must not the proper time arrive?... Sri Rama entered the womb only when Dasharatha’s time had come. Zamindar! Look at our good fortune... Yesterday I cleaned out the loft in my house. An old palm-leaf manuscript turned up there... It contains the secret of avirppagam.”

“The method?”

“It is no ordinary thing. Very difficult.”

“It is only money that will be spent, isn’t it?”

“Leave the money aside; the human labour involved is tremendous. Work for the brain. Work searching for herbs... all divine herbs!”

“The trouble can be rewarded suitably.”

“Well said. I don’t want a paisa for myself. If a child is born to you, it will be as though I received a crore sovereigns!”

“Are the herbs alone enough?”

“No... no... half ten-and-a-half-touch gold, half fresh leaf juice. It must be matured for ten days in sunlight.”

“Money... however much it costs... all I want is...”

“Everything will end in victory, you’ll see—the result will be in your hand.”

Varadachari collected two thousand rupees from the Zamindar to prepare the avirppagam and departed. “The preparation... is secret... even your wife must not know. If the matter gets out... the medicine will lose its power,” he warned, stitching the caution firmly into the Zamindar’s mind.

Lines of triumph appeared on the Zamindar’s face. Laughter burst and scattered.

* * * *

“Put this shirt on the child. Hey, driver—take the little fellow in the car and drive him around town. Oi, goldsmith! This chain isn’t good enough for the child. Make him a diamond necklace like the one Krishna Bhagavan wore. It must be exactly like it. An exact copy. Yes, I’ve said it. Look, Shenbagam, the child is laughing. My darling boy, give me a big kiss. A motorcar of silver for you. A horse of gold. A little bicycle of diamonds—enough? Not enough? All right... a cradle of gems. My little darling... Don’t laugh, Shenbagam... the greatness of our Aiyar’s avirppagam! What greatness—”

“What are you babbling?” cried Shenbagavalli, waking in alarm beside him.

“Nothing,” said the Zamindar, rubbing his eyes, still intoxicated by the dream. That same night, near the Mariamman temple, there was a terrible darkness, like the heart of a rich man! The sky looked as though it were raining blackness.

Now and then, diamond-cuts of lightning tore a few lines of fire across it. In that sudden light... a figure could be seen moving. It had only two arms and two legs! So it was not Mari herself wandering about. The figure came to the temple door and stopped. The door opened. The figure entered the sanctum. In the light of the golden lamp donated by the Zamindar, the figure could be identified as none other than the priest Marudhamuthu.

The lamp went out.

The next morning the Zamindar awoke. News reached him that the Mariamman temple had been robbed. Property worth three thousand rupees had been stolen. By evening, a force of red-capped policemen had gathered at Sethubhavanam. Mariamman did not come to give a statement. That, of course, was only to be expected. The priest had entered this affair boldly enough.

“Until the thief is found, the Zamindar won’t let it go... He suspects an important person... They say he even suspects the priest”—such was the village rumour.

“Priest! I’ve told the Zamindar. You alone must show me a way.”

“What is it, Swami?... A serious matter. I have a medicine... even a barren woman will bear a child if it is taken. But the man must take it.”

<!-- anthology scan: 14; printed page: 12 -->

“Besh!... I’ll tell the Zamindar himself to take it. Marudhamuthu... will a child definitely be born?”

“Don’t look down on it just because it is a poor man’s medicine...”

“Here, take twenty rupees! If the medicine works... I’ll get you thousands and thousands.”

“If the medicine works... it’ll be as though I myself have found a new life... What can I say? Mahamayi is there.”

“But Marudhamuthu... don’t tell the Zamindar that you gave the medicine.”

“This isn’t something I’d tell anyone, sir! You take the credit yourself.”

After Varadachari left the priest’s house, Marudhamuthu sat down with a deep sigh and laughed once. That laugh was the monstrous laugh that would make all Sethubhavanam weep.

The Zamindar mixed the avirppagam supplied by Varadachari into milk, secretly swallowed it, rinsed his mouth so that no smell remained, chewed betel, and, just as Varadachari had instructed, walked toward the women’s quarters.

That night the Zamindar was overflowing with delight. Shenbagam listened with pleasure to endearments she had never heard from him before. He would offer her a cup of fresh milk, snatch it back before it touched her lips and tease her, then lift her bodily and toss her onto the bed—nothing but play. The clock struck twelve.

“Shenbagam!... If we have a child, what shall we name it?”

The Lady laughed.

“Why are you laughing?” She laughed again. “I felt like laughing, so I laughed.” Her laughter would not stop. The Zamindar embraced her and lifted her up.

“My... sugar-candy!... ocean of milk!... golden creeper!...” His tongue slurred as he poured out these modern-age descriptions. He lifted Shenbagam and ran... that was all. Both collapsed to the floor. The Lady sprang up in alarm. Smiling anxiously, she raised the Zamindar’s face. His mouth looked like a waterfall of blood.

The “medicine for getting a child,” which Varadachari had said he would prepare and had obtained from Marudhamuthu, had finished its work. Like a lion struck by a bullet, the Zamindar opened his mouth once... and never opened it again.

Dawn came.

Sethubhavanam wept. The priest Marudhamuthu laughed. No one knew why the Zamindar had died so suddenly. No one knew that it was the work of the medicine Marudhamuthu had made so that he would not be trapped in the theft case! Nor did Marudhamuthu know that the two thousand rupees Varadachari had given as dowry for his daughter was money taken from the Zamindar on the promise of making avirppagam.

<!-- anthology scan: 16; printed page: 14 -->

A small smile from the priest...! A great laugh from the priest...!

The bank of smoke rising from the cremation ground after consuming the Zamindar’s body seemed, as it mingled with the wind, to murmur in a low pitch: “A child! A child!”

Through the greatness of the boon-prasadam Varadachari had given, the charming life of the Lady of Sethubhavanam set like the sun. But even in darkness there is a moon. That moon was the child Kokila.

This is what had happened before Prema came to sell Kokila...

The Zamindar of Sethubhavanam, who had withered for want of a child—and Prema, who withered because she had no means to protect her child—were signs of the ugliness in creation. Prema cried, “A child! A child!” In the tune with which greens are hawked in a market—“Greens! Greens!”—Prema created a human market. Yet she had not come there intending to sell her child. She had hoped to take a servant’s position in the Lady’s bungalow. That was the only way she had to raise the baby. But the Lady’s compassion crossed its bounds. She asked for the child itself. The desire she showed for the baby drove Prema toward cutting away her own life. In truth, that was not what the Lady had intended. Philosophers of Vedanta say nothing happens merely as we imagine. “Whatever happens is God’s doing,” said Prema. Yet Prema’s hollow life makes one ask whether it was really the doing of that sinner. Only at the very end

did Prema cease laying everything at God’s door. She understood that the pain that had entered her life was something she herself had sought; only after understanding that did she come to Sethubhavanam.

Had the Lady accepted Prema as a servant, Prema would at least have clung to life for Kokila’s sake. But when the Lady forcibly took over her responsibility, what desire to live was left to her? That was why Prema did not accept the fifty silver coins the Lady offered. Only as she took leave of the Lady did Prema look at Kokila three times. She did not wish to look again. There were no tears in her eyes. Only her throat heaved. Even a volcano pouring out its last flood of fire would not hold as much flame as there was in Prema’s deep sigh. Her heart shook like an earthquake that had sworn to overturn the world.

Between Prema and the child Kokila ran a thin electric wire. For a few seconds Prema stood frozen.

It seemed as though baby Kokila were speaking. As though she were telling a story. Prema listened to that story. Only she could hear it. For a while she became absorbed in the history narrated by a three-month-old child! Kokila’s kovvai-fruit lips moved; the story began.

Prema was fifteen years old! The villagers said she was very beautiful. Whenever she looked at herself in a mirror, she too would usually feel a little proud.

“Prema! Yes, Prema indeed! A soft-spoken girl, not a Bhadrakali who speaks syrupy words only to take revenge! She turns love itself into song when she speaks. Two lips coated with delight! Eyes brimming with light! The very life-vein of existence is the word Prema—the name Prema is a breeze that stirs one’s thoughts! Why should the beauty flashing on her cheeks become a spear that pierces the heart? ‘Flower-breeze! Golden creeper!’ and so on—Kannapiran described her thus. Kannapiran was a writer. The son of Varadachari, the Vaidika! Varadachari was a man of stature who lived in an upstairs house! Some sort of spiritual teacher! What wonder was there in his son Kannapiran earning the title of writer?”

The description Kannapiran composed about Prema was borrowed merchandise! Something somebody had written for somebody else! Kannapiran merely followed the method practised by many writers of the day. But this thieving profession of his was hidden by birth, money and influence in those times!

Prema was madly infatuated with Kannapiran. Kannapiran too made plans to live as Prema’s lover.

<!-- anthology scan: 19; printed page: 17 -->

Prema was poor! But she had the courage that came from thinking she was going to become Varadachari’s daughter-in-law. Sitting in her leaking little house, she would imagine herself strolling upstairs with Kannapiran. Prema was a peasant girl; Kannapiran was the son of a thoroughly orthodox Vaidika. Between them stood caste! Kannapiran had written an article titled “The Saturn of Caste.” That article was witness enough that he would not deceive Prema. He had written many letters declaring that he would never yield to caste arrogance. “I have none of the pride of being a Brahmin of a high caste, Prema! The two of us have been sanctified by the flame called love. The footprints of those embodiments of sacrifice who gave their lives for love are what deserve our worship. Fate has bound us together. It cannot separate us. It will not even dare.” Saying such things, Kannapiran drew Prema onto the path of love. Varadachari did not interfere in Kannapiran’s affairs. Though Prema’s mother was an infirm old woman, she warned her many times. “Kannapiran is an Iyengar. Even with a ladder, our people could never reach his. Don’t trust him and ruin yourself for nothing, Prema,” she would say often. “Everything must happen according to fate. Please don’t stop me,” Prema begged. To her, the old woman’s words were like calling fruit bitter and nectar poison.

<!-- anthology scan: 20; printed page: 18 -->

Prema’s castles of thought rose until they struck the sky. There was no shortage of frequent meetings, tender words and revelry.

“Prema! I have trusted you with all my heart. But who knows whether I’ll end up like the parrot waiting for the ilavu fruit?”

“Why, Kannu! So much fear? Are you trying to get ahead of me?”

“No, Prema. Ever since what happened with Kamala in the house opposite, I have become terribly afraid even of being friendly with women.”

“The story of Kamala from the house opposite is certainly interesting! Shantharam had been certain that he would marry Kamala. She said the same. But the garland she finally placed was around Manoharan.” That was the crucial turn in the story. That was what Kannapiran kept hammering home to Prema.

Prema would lose herself in Kannapiran’s embrace. She thought that itself was heaven. “What power love has! If the son of high-born Varadachari swears that he will marry a mere peasant girl, why should one not call love divine?” she would ask herself.

Love grew; it blazed into flame; the restraints around the bud fell away; the flower shed fragrance;

<!-- anthology scan: 21; printed page: 19 -->

pollen scattered. Fresh petals dusted with gold opened wide.

The struggle of Prema’s mother, who in her infirm old age had been fighting against time, also came to an end. Her time ran out. “Act wisely, Prema!” she did not forget to say even on the verge of death. “It will happen according to fate, Amma,” Prema did not hesitate to answer.

Loneliness became a bitter medicine in Prema’s life. How long could they remain secret lovers? She planned for marriage. One day she told Kannapiran so.

“Is marriage the only way for us to live? Is pleasure staying away from us now?” Kannapiran replied.

Prema’s face changed. It seemed as though the foundation of her hopes had begun to shake. She managed the moment. Just then Kannapiran gave her cheek a light pat. To her it was like pouring cool water onto boiling fire.

“O writer! O poet who sees life itself in love! O bard who described the word Prema as containing the pranava mantra! You have leapt from a mleccha straight into a Maharana!” Prema began to mock him. In truth, the mockery itself turned into an inner revolt.

Before the marriage of Prema and Kannapiran could take place, the stage arrived at which a child was born. Prema gave birth to a baby girl. But Prema was still unmarried.

Prema and Kannapiran had not married—but the auspicious wedding ceremony of Kannapiran and Meenakshi had been completed.

News appeared, with a photograph, of the marriage of national writer Sriman Kannapiran to Meenakshi Ammal, daughter of Ranganatha Iyengar of the great Mylapore estate.

Before this wedding could be completed, Prema had written thousands upon thousands of letters to her lover. Varadachari’s son turned his attention to the dowry question. He surrendered his heart to Meenakshi’s photograph. To paint her portrait in words, he searched foreign poems for some description he might borrow. Prema’s letters—her cries—went unheard. Carrying the newborn infant, Prema set out. Only then did her former would-be father-in-law Varadachari say, “Fate is playing.” Patriot Deivasigamani said it was the disorder of a lack of freedom. Comrade Pommurayar proclaimed it an economic stagnation.

There was no one to pity Prema. “The arrogant little wench not only ruined an Iyengar’s son; now she carries a child around looking for some other household to ruin,” babbled the village.

<!-- anthology scan: 23; printed page: 21 -->

Kannapiran’s sexual betrayal did not come to light. His manhood hid it. Caste threw another curtain over it. Kamala of the house opposite was not called immoral for deceiving Shantharam and marrying another man. The reason was that many people needed the favour of Kamala’s household.

Prema was a destitute poor woman! She alone was made into an immoral woman. To serve as an explanation of her “immorality,” there was a child in her arms—but no tali around her neck.

For Kamala to have been the object of Shantharam’s desire and embrace was merely an innocent mistake! For Prema to have loved Kannapiran with all her heart was immorality! Kannapiran’s deception of Prema was caste pride—social protection. Shantharam’s love for Kamala alone was depravity—this was the village verdict. Why? Is not the whole world like that? Where is justice here?

If an ordinary fellow in a grain shop takes a little black gram and puts it in his basket, that is theft. If the shopkeeper measures three-quarters of a measure as a full measure, that is business skill!

If an ordinary man tells a lie, it is a great sin. If a lawyer tells it, it is talent! If Kuppan, who lives in a hut, gets drunk and dances, he earns the title “drunkard.” If the very same thing is done by those spoken of as great men, it is “enjoyment.”

If an ordinary man gives his wife away to another, it is a shameless act! If Iyarpagai Nayanar does the same thing, it is a flood of devotion.

This is the world that calls Panchali a chaste wife. From where, in such a world, could a just verdict come leaping down for Prema alone?

Kamala deceived her lover. Kannapiran deceived Prema. This rotten society refused to give an even-handed judgment between them. But the child had to be raised. That worry engulfed Prema. In the light of the child’s eyes, Prema would see Kannapiran and her tears would overflow. She would mutter, “caste arrogance.” The advice of her dead mother would return to her mind. “Fate! Fate!” she would repeat again and again.

One day, somewhere in a bazaar street, Prema was walking with the child. An advertisement hanging in the market caught her eye. As a result, she joined the “Civilized Drama Company.” Her natural knowledge of music stood by her.

Prema, who did not properly understand the world at all—Prema, who had lived believing in fate—Prema, who had regarded the Aiyar caste as a race of immortals—Prema, who took everything white to be milk—how could she know that in the modern theatre world there were poisonous springs that cultivated the art of prostitution?

Two months passed. The second chapter of Prema’s life began. The pages of her life turned through a section of putting on costumes... a section in which fame spread... a section in which garlands fell around her neck. In theatre advertisements the name “Premayini” was printed in special colours.

“Prema, your acting today was first-rate.”

“Yes, that’s what they said.”

“My wish is that you should rise still further.”

“No. It is enough if my life gives me the means to protect my child.”

“Don’t you understand how foolish it is to make protecting a child the sole purpose of life?”

“What other support do I have for living in this world?”

“Do you imagine that the man waiting to end your widow’s sorrow and lay all kinds of pleasures before you is a mere ‘moringa milar’?”

“I would not think of calling him that. I think of him as a deceiver who smears poison on the tongue.”

“Prema, look here. I am the owner of this company.”

“Sir! I know. Please don’t pursue me.”

One night there was this quarrel between Prema and the company owner.

Prema would never agree in her heart that the owner had won. It was on the very day she earned a good name in the play Nalayini that this other drama took place.

From the owner to the rajapart actor, the comic actor and the scene-puller, everyone stood wagging his tail in whichever direction Prema’s breeze blew. Had Prema wished, she could have become a queen of the stage. She could have appeared as an enchantress of the cinema. She could have made any number of Kannapirans prey to a sidelong glance. But her life hung upon only one need. Kokila! Yes, her child! For that child the madwoman kept herself alive. Yet an army of ants began to line up to taste her. One day Prema suddenly disappeared from the company.

She wandered here and there—thought of all sorts of things. Hunger! Hunger! She herself could perhaps endure that cruelty, but what could the infant do? Only if she had enough milk could the baby be fed. The child would cry and cry; she would join it. The child would look at its mother’s face. Prema would look at her daughter’s face. There was no one to look at the faces of either of them. “That sinner Kannapiran,” she would begin to think. It would seem as though her infirm old mother were lamenting, “Did you listen to what I told you,

<!-- anthology scan: 27; printed page: 25 -->

Prema?” She would imagine the universe laughing at her with indifference. It would seem as though a demon called Fate stood before her, clapping and laughing, taunting: “Did you trust me and get deceived?” She would shut her eyes and open them again. Two Shivasamudram waterfalls would be bathing her cheeks. Again she would look at the infant, her dry lips moving. The baby would push aside its mother’s upper cloth and then look at her face. The sorrowful gaze of that innocent child seemed to ask, “Couldn’t the tears running down Prema’s face be milk?”

One day passed! Two! Three! Prema obtained an interview with the Lady of Sethubhavanam. She applied for a servant’s position. But the Lady said she wanted the child for herself. It was as though the fruit had slipped straight into the milk! Prema’s wish was fulfilled. A great burden fell away.

The “A child! A child!” business ended successfully indeed.
