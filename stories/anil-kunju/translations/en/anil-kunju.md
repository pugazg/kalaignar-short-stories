Like a flock of pigeons scattering when a stone is thrown, the rail-gong at St. Mary's School was struck, and the young children came running out with the bundles of books they had already tied up slung over their shoulders. Without waiting to be asked, their feet hurried toward their homes, half running and half walking. A little distance from St. Mary's School, as the streets branched off in different directions, the children too split up. The little sparrows that had set out as a flock soon became groups of three or four, and a little later one or two at a time, walking along the paths that led to their homes.

One of those children was Paruk—about ten years old. Checked trousers—a green

<!-- source scan 20; printed page 15 -->

shirt—and a white cap embroidered with floral work, covering only the crown of his head rather than the whole of it!

The moment the bell rang he had come running out brimming with excitement, then begun to walk, and after that he moved along the street like a cloud. Before long even that walk slowed, and he sharply pricked up his ears at something.

The sound of a loudspeaker from a meeting nearby. Paruk stopped. He moved to the side of the street so that he could hear the speech clearly.

“Was not the demolition of the Babri Mosque, with a history of four hundred years, a terrifying spectacle of religious fanaticism? Can we give even the slightest room to such fanaticism? Think about it. Demolishing the mosque in the name of karseva has opened the way to so much violence and turned the whole country into a blood-soaked field. Therefore let us all join hands and march together in declaring that religious conflict has no place on our Tamil soil! Let us preserve peace!”

Applause rose in welcome of that appeal.

“This shawl is being presented to our Thambithurai, who has been raising these slogans until now.”

After thanks were offered over the loudspeaker and the shawl was presented, songs began to play. Listening to the song, Paruk once again walked toward his home

<!-- source scan 21; printed page 16 -->

while the words Babri Mosque—karseva—alone surged through his mind. He had crossed two more streets when another loudspeaker caught his attention, and he stopped out of curiosity to listen to that one too.

“There is evidence that the Babri Mosque was built on the very spot where Rama's temple stood in Ayodhya. True devotees of Rama did not accept the argument of those who said, 'Let the Babri Mosque remain, and let a Rama temple be built beside it.' Therefore there was nothing wrong at all in demolishing and removing that mosque.”

Even as the speaker was saying this, the loudspeaker fell silent. With no curiosity to find out why the speech had stopped, Paruk slowly walked away.

Babri Mosque—karseva—religious fanaticism—Rama temple—Ayodhya: although he had often heard these words lately, he had never thought deeply about them when he heard them. Even now, after hearing the speeches of two different meetings through the loudspeakers, he did not particularly want to know the whole matter in detail.

<!-- source scan 22; printed page 17 -->

Still, the two words Rama temple—Ayodhya now joined the two words Babri Mosque—karseva that were surging through his mind.

Though the gentle evening breeze touched Paruk's body and cheered him, he remained confused, thinking about the two different views that had come from those loudspeakers. With only two streets left before he reached home, he tilted his head slightly toward the sky and, on a tree-lined street, began moving his hands and legs in a slow dance, remembering some film he had seen.

“What's this, Paruk? Do you think this is a street or a drama stage? A tap dance? If you want, shall I join you and dance too?”

An old vegetable seller, returning with her empty basket after finishing her business, asked the teasing question as she came toward him. Paruk shot back at once: “Then lift me up and dance with me, Grandma! If you can't, Grandpa will be waiting at home. Go there and dance disco with him, Grandma!”

The vegetable grandmother felt both laughter and anger rising in her. Imagining herself dancing with her husband even made shyness gnaw at her. Lightly tapping Paruk on the cheek, she said, “You little rascal, son of Ibrahim Rowther! What mischief!” Then, overflowing with amazement, she added, “Born the son of an uneducated Rowther,

<!-- source scan 23; printed page 18 -->

how quick-wittedly you talk!” She kissed him and went on her way. Yet Paruk's love and affection for that grandmother remained settled in his heart. She lived in the nearby colony, and Paruk kept watching as she took a shortcut leading off from that street. Just then he heard something fall from a tree beside him with a “thud.” Startled, he turned and looked toward the foot of the tree.

A baby squirrel had somehow slipped or lost its footing from a branch, fallen to the ground, and lay motionless from the shock of the fall. Paruk's sympathy for it welled up. He immediately ran over, picked it up, and comforted it. The baby squirrel gave a faint “keech, keech” whimper. With his young fingers he stroked it gently. It rolled about in his palm, proving that life was still in it. Holding it with great care, the baby squirrel in one hand and the embrace of the other around it, he hurried home.

He must keep this baby squirrel at home and raise it. He must give it fruit juice. Why much fruit juice? One spoonful or two would be enough. Even when he went to school, he should carry it safely in his bag. As Paruk walked along building such castles in his mind, he remembered

the story about the squirrel that his schoolteacher had told them. With that sweet memory, and with the baby squirrel warm in his hands, Paruk entered the house. “Amma! Has Vappa still not come?” he asked eagerly. His mother replied, “Vappa said he'd collect all the money owed to the butcher shop and come back in the evening. He'll be here now!”

Just then his father, Ibrahim Rowther the butcher, came in, stroking his large moustache and tightening the green canvas belt fitted snugly above the lungi tied around his waist. “So, young master, you're back? What was special at your school today?” he asked as he entered.

“Vappa! Look here, Vappa—a baby squirrel! It was lying under a tree. I'm going to raise it.”

Paruk's mother and father stared without blinking at the baby squirrel in his hand. They too stroked it affectionately.

“Vappa, they say this baby squirrel isn't an ordinary creature. In the Ramayana, when Rama and the monkeys with him built a bridge to Lanka and marched against Ravana, a squirrel also helped Rama build that bridge. So Rama praised it by stroking the squirrel's back with three fingers.

<!-- source scan 25; printed page 20 -->

That's why it has three stripes on its back—our teacher told us in class last week!”

The moment Ibrahim Rowther heard what Paruk said, anger surged through him. His great moustache quivered! It seemed he had linked everything together—Rama's name, the squirrel Rama had stroked, the bridge to Lanka, Ayodhya, the demolition of the Babri Mosque. Until then he had never heard the story Paruk had told.

“Hey, Paruk! This baby squirrel must not stay in our house even for a minute! If it stays here, they'll come to demolish this place too, saying this is the house of a Rama-devotee squirrel! Take it respectfully right now and leave it wherever you picked it up. Go!”

“It is a voiceless creature. What can it do, Vappa? Poor thing—let it stay. I'll raise it carefully, Vappa!”

“Hey! Are you talking back to me? After you yourself said this squirrel helped Rama, and after saying the three stripes on it were put there by Rama, you still want to raise it here after that? No. Take it away right now and leave it there. Go!”

Paruk stood with tears in his eyes. He looked pitifully at the baby squirrel and then pleadingly at his mother. His mother proposed a compromise for both sides.

<!-- source scan 26; printed page 21 -->

“Let it be for tonight. How can he take it somewhere and leave it now in the dark? Paruk is a good boy; he will certainly listen to us. As soon as dawn breaks, he'll take it and leave it there. Isn't that right, dear Paruk? What I'm saying is right, isn't it? In the morning you must take it and leave it there!”

“All right, Amma!”

That night Paruk inverted a well-ventilated cane basket over the baby squirrel, spread a mat right beside it, and lay down there to keep it safe. He did not sleep all night. He poured orange juice into a small spoon, pushed it through a gap in the basket, and watched with delight as the baby squirrel tasted and drank it.

In obedience to his father's order, Paruk took the baby squirrel and came back toward the old tree where he had found it, thinking about where he could leave it safely.

“Where are you going so early in the morning? Don't you have school today? Why are you wandering about instead of studying?” asked the man coming toward him. It was Aravamudha Ayyangar, one of the town's Sanatana men. Paruk stopped without answering him.

“What's in your hand? What are you holding and crying over?”

“A baby squirrel! I took it home because I wanted to raise it...”

<!-- source scan 27; printed page 22 -->

Before Paruk could finish speaking, Aravamudha Ayyangar became agitated.

“What? Raising a squirrel—and you raising it at that? A squirrel is a creature that has received Rama's grace! Even your touching it is wrong!” he snapped.

At that moment Ibrahim Rowther also arrived there.

“What is it, elder? Why are you getting angry with the boy? What wrong has my son done?”

“Ibrahim! Don't you know the matter? He says he's raising a squirrel! It is an animal disciple of Rama! What right do people of your religion have to raise it? Isn't it a great sin?”

Hearing these irritated words from Aravamudha, Ibrahim Rowther too became furious. He began to raise the voice of rights.

“Why? What if we raise it? In Ayodhya you demolished our mosque saying it shouldn't be there. Are we not even allowed to raise a squirrel? Fine justice this is!”

“What is this, Rowther? Have you come to argue justice with me? Why are you tying the baby squirrel to Ayodhya?”

“You're the one tying the baby squirrel to Rama!”

“You're the one talking about Ayodhya and hurting the feelings of us Hindus!”

<!-- source scan 28; printed page 23 -->

“Speak respectfully. If you address me in the disrespectful singular, I'll speak to you the same way!”

As the words between the two grew heavier and heavier, hundreds of Hindus and Muslims gathered on the two sides under the tree. Hearing that a scuffle that could lead to a major riot was about to break out, Paruk's mother came running there headlong. Pulling her head-covering farther over herself and hiding behind a tree, she shouted,

“Hey, Paruk! All this trouble has come because of you! Throw that baby squirrel down and come back! Otherwise ten people will be killed in this town.”

The enraged people on both sides turned toward the direction of the voice. Paruk too cried, “Amma!” and ran to the tree, hugged his mother, and, with tears streaming, gently placed the baby squirrel on the ground among the trees.

Suddenly a hawk... that had been sitting on a tree branch beat its wings rapidly and swooped down upon the baby squirrel. In that rush it flew into the sky carrying the tiny creature with it.

The two sides dispersed. The baby squirrel had preserved the peace of that town by making the sacrifice it was able to make!

Paruk alone remained standing there, shedding tears as he watched the hawk carry the baby squirrel away.
