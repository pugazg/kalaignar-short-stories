# Two Shadows

> **English translation of the verified Tamil archival reading.** The authoritative 1987 title is `இரு நிழல்கள்`; the older directory slug `iru-nigazhvugal` is retained only for repository continuity. Controlling source: lower scan **20 → 21 → 22 → upper 23**.

<!-- anthology scan: 20; printed page: 19; lower shared span -->

Two shadows are mentioned in literature. One appears in Kalidasa's Shakuntala. The other appears in Sangam literature.

In the Shakuntala written by Kalidasa, he describes the summer.

The heat is unbearable. The fields are cracked and split open. With no shade to be found anywhere, a snake moves and writhes here and there. Even while moving, unable to bear the heat, it plants only its tail on the ground and holds its whole body up; the snake's shadow falls below. Then, without knowing what that shadow is, something goes and shelters beneath it.

<!-- anthology scan: 21; printed page: 20 -->

<!-- source illustration occupies the upper page and depicts the literary-shadow examples: snake/frog and deer -->

The frog does not know that the place where it has taken shelter is the shadow of a snake; simply needing some shade, the frog shelters in the snake's shadow.

This is the scene described in Kalidasa's Shakuntala.

A Tamil poet composed a verse in Kalithogai in Sangam literature. It is one of the poems in the Pālai Kali section of Kalithogai.

<!-- anthology scan: 22; printed page: 21 -->

As in Shakuntala, it is a summer season. In that summer an antlered male deer and a female deer are travelling through a desert.

Unable to bear seeing the female deer suffer in that unbearable heat, the male deer stands there and makes the female lie where his shadow falls, giving her shade. This is the shadow found in Sangam literature.

Why do I tell you about these two shadows? In Shakuntala, the frog goes and stands in the snake's shadow without knowing it is a snake's shadow. You know what will happen if the snake becomes aware of it.

But in Sangam literature, the shade is given knowingly. The male deer stands and gives shade knowing that he is giving shade to his companion. The female lies below without anxiety or fear.

I would say this to the minority communities. For a short time in the intervening period, you went in search of the snake's shadow. It was about that shadow that our friend Sulaiman Sait and others spoke in vigorous attack.

Now, even if he himself has no shade, it does not matter—

<!-- anthology scan: 23; printed page: 22; upper shared span -->

—the shade shown by Kalithogai, which says that one must give shade to one's companion, has become available to the minorities. That is the shade called the DMK.
