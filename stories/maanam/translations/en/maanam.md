## Honour

Velan was only ten then.

He was of school-going age. He had a beautiful form that made one want to drink in its loveliness. His parents, who cherished him as the nectar-fruit of childhood, never passed a moment without fondling the lamp of their lineage. Vaidegi, twenty-six years old, and Singaram, past thirty-five, had conducted a married life in that village that stood as a beautiful example. The villagers would call it a ‘Singaram life’ in their conversations! A husband who gladdened the heart—a wife flourishing in excellence—a treasured young son—what want could there be in that household! That family fell into death's eye.

Death came as though to read them a eulogy: theirs was a life of such character that even enemies would praise it! Death entered that sandalwood grove! It does not spare a cow that has come to graze merely because it has become lovely arugampul; nor does it spare the tiger that came to spring upon it merely because the tiger has become a gentle cow. Death too is like that.

It carried off Vaidegi's husband, Velan's father, the accomplished Singaram. And society, crueller even than death,<!-- source scan 74; printed page 74 --> crowned her with the ‘Sir’ title of “bad omen” and rejoiced after putting her on display upon the rubbish heap.

◆     ◆

Drunken Karuppan had set his sights on the kundumani bead of the rubbish heap. A field that has lost its fence and a woman who has lost her tali—are they not the unprotected ones in this rough world! Vaidegi understood the snare Karuppan was laying. She had already decided that henceforth her work was simply to send her son to school and, when he returned, delight in seeing her husband's radiance in the boy's smiling face. Now there came to join that life the torment of wondering how she could escape Drunken Karuppan's aim. She tried to settle it in her mind by blaming a world that makes both skylarks and vultures wheel in circles. But she suffered, painfully aware that this was not the way by which she could escape. She had only one consolation. Even that was incomplete! When her son grew up, she told herself, he would bend wicked Karuppans like bows and fling them away. That was her consolation. Yet whenever she thought of it she would mock herself: “Tch! What is this? What an untimely wish!” Even so, the hope that had sprouted in one corner of her heart began to grow. She counted the days. She tried to chase the days forward so that her son would quickly become a grown man. She did not know that a prisoner's urgency<!-- source scan 75; printed page 75 --> was something “Day” could not understand inside a prison—and that the same “Day” existed in the outside world too. She believed that if Velan grew quickly he would become a fortress protecting her; a son protecting his mother; a warrior protecting honour.

Whether Velan grew or not, Karuppan's cruelties grew at dreamlike speed. Vaidegi lay in fear, wondering when that scorpion would sting her, when a message sweeter than honey came to her ears on the breeze. Drunken Karuppan had become involved in a murder case, received a sentence of fifteen years and gone to prison. For Velan's mother, the urgency called “Day” disappeared.

◆     ◆

Years rolled by. Velan had gone to another town on business and had still not returned! Vaidegi waited, planning that once her son came home she would speak to him about marriage and establish a “democratic mother-in-law kingdom” the very next month. She heard a knock at the door. Thinking her darling had returned, she opened it. It was not her darling. Karuppan, released from prison, entered.

<!-- source scan 76; printed page 76 -->

“Ah! Vaidegi—even at forty you are still quite beautiful! See how much affection I have for you!

“They opened the prison—and I came straight here. ‘To see the bronze statue,’” he said, twirling his moustache, streaked black and white. Vaidegi screamed. He covered her mouth. He wanted to tear apart this flower, crumpled, shed and fallen. The situation became terrifying. She was caught in his grip. Vaidegi stared wildly. He laughed and embraced her.

A face appeared at the lattice. Velan's face. “Velan! Have you come, son? Save my honour, my boy!” she cried. By then Karuppan had closed her mouth. Velan moved to spring forward. At that same instant he saw the gleaming knife that had passed from Karuppan's waist into his hand. Turning toward Velan and raising the knife, Karuppan barked, “Honour—or life?” Velan ran. Not toward Karuppan—outside.

Karuppan clasped Vaidegi tightly. He extinguished the flame of her ideal too.

The mother who had believed that once her son grew up she need fear no one and nothing now wept, lamenting, “Unable to protect my honour—<!-- source scan 77; printed page 77 --> cowardly son!” That mother's mind told her that Karuppan, who had robbed her of her honour, was far better than the son who had not protected it. But she could no longer deliver a sermon on honour. A corpse does not speak, does it! At the end, she had written in charcoal upon the white wall of the house: “The mother of a coward who did not protect honour embraced death.”

Velan, who had fled, was afraid even to return to the village. The broad knife Karuppan had shown him that day continued to frighten him. Velan wandered about for several days. One day a striking item appeared in a daily newspaper: “A train overturned. Four hundred people died.” Among those dead whose bodies could not be identified was Velan too. The life for whose sake he had allowed the very blood that had nourished him to be shed—that life was gone. If he had sprung upon Karuppan that very day and stood facing his knife, he might have protected his mother's honour and his own. Now he had lost honour. He had lost life too. The mother protected her lost honour through death. But the son? Alas, what pity! The village spoke only of his loss of honour. No one knew that he had died among four hundred others.

<!-- source scan 78; printed page 78 -->

Death may be held off today. But one day or another it grazes upon man and goes its way. What use is there in fearing death? Can this human race not possess even the ordinary thought that we should fear losing honour? Honour can be protected even in death. Life will one day inevitably depart. O humanity, struggling to protect a life that cannot be protected! Is it just that you let the honour that can be protected fly away upon the wind?
