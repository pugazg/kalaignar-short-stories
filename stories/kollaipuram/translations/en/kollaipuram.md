# Kollaipuram

It was a time when the rulers of princely states wielded absolute power. Bathing in the sweat of those who laboured, drinking their blood like liquor, and imagining that this world had been created solely for their pleasures and perverse pursuits, those intoxicated rulers held court in their states without anyone to question them. One such state was the princely state of “Kollaipuram.” An apt name: a state where plunderers sat enthroned. All the tax money that the people of that state had poured out in tears for medicine, education, roads, drinking water, and every other public necessity was transformed into cushioned dolls in the king’s pleasure halls, and into jewels of nine gems adorning the graceful body of the Maharani from

<!-- source scan 30; printed page 25 -->

foot to hair. She bathed in milk. She rinsed her mouth with fruit juice. Not only the king and queen, but the families of every official serving in that palace were sunk in such uninterrupted intoxication in that pleasure-world that hardly a day passed without merriment, celebration, and drunken revelry. The people of that soil, labouring like cattle and wearing themselves down to the bone, decided that Mahesan had written precisely such a fate upon their heads and writhed like worms in fire.

The ordinary people of the state had no right to cry out that they were hungry, or to lament that they were starving. If anyone spoke of it—or was even suspected of thinking about it—a harsh law existed by which they could be seized and thrown into prison for life. Under that law, more than ten thousand people lay reduced to skin and bone in prison fortresses, enduring terrible punishment.

The British Empire, which kept the princely states under its domination and watched only to make sure that their tribute payments arrived properly and regularly, was not concerned about the injustice and atrocities taking place in Kollaipuram.

Yet among the people of Kollaipuram, toiling like bonded slaves, a few who were prepared to risk their lives arose as sparks for a revolutionary fire about to blaze, with the question: “How many more days are we to endure these sufferings and sorrows and remain like tiny insects?” They gathered people here and there and began to teach them at least the minimum freedom to think about the disorderly rule of the princely ruler—and about the conspiratorial designs of the British Empire, which saw those abuses, pretended not to see them, permitted them, and devised ways for domination to endure.

That fire slowly began to burn, and questions of “Why?” and “For what reason?” arose everywhere. Even then, the king and queen of Kollaipuram, together with their retinue of officials, treated the heat of that fire merely as a pleasant warmth suited to the chill of their amorous pleasures. They passed their time without removing even one item from their old list of revelries. An arrogance stood tall among them: they believed that God had granted them the total lease on every pleasure and luxury.

But the British Empire’s senior officials in India, who held that state in their grip, began to think about the future. They watched what was happening there and the fact that a section of the people was preparing for revolt; if they continued to neglect it

<!-- source scan 32; printed page 27 -->

further and further, they realised that not only the authority of the princely state but their own imperial authority might also collapse.

The strength of the Empire must not diminish; its growth must not decline. At the same time, the king of Kollaiyur, ruler of the state, had to be put under some pressure so that the Empire might earn a good name among the citizens. Consultations began on how such a plan could be devised. Accordingly, a judicial tribunal was established in Kollaiyur, the capital of Kollaipuram, to learn the grievances of the people, conduct an impartial inquiry into the accusations they made against the king, the Maharani, and the chief officials, and prepare and send complete reports. The British themselves were appointed as judges of the tribunal. When the tribunal, consisting of a chief judge and several judges assisting him, was formed, the citizens of the state breathed a sigh of relief.

They set aside some of their anxiety, believing that the scorpion-stings of authoritarian laws would no longer strike them. They rejoiced in the hope that if any of the state’s chief officials treated them harshly, they could immediately lodge a complaint with the tribunal and obtain justice.

The king might lie exhausted on the Amsathoolika couch in the embraces of beautiful women, enchanted as though this were the bliss won through penance. But the Maharani did not sink completely into that world of pleasure. Because she had the habit of waking from it now and then and watching what was happening around the government, she was capable of recognising the dangers sprouting around them. So she left the king fallen under the intoxication of liquor and women and frequently held secret consultations with the palace chiefs—choosing times when their own intoxication had cleared!

She could foresee that the anger of the people, smouldering and rising, and the disease of hunger burning like fire in their bellies might one day bring down the princely government and its ruling class. If Kollaipuram escaped its bonded-slave condition and passed into the hands of the people, even the Empire above them would no longer have the strength to protect them. Why, the Empire itself might cease to exist. Her thoughts took wing over how this could be managed.

The result was that a “people’s army,” subordinate to the palace chiefs, was prepared! The task of this people’s army was to “infiltrate” the genuine people’s force and create disorder—to cripple its important members in every possible

<!-- source scan 34; printed page 29 -->

way, even to end their lives! The assignment given to some of the chiefs was one of the finest of the Maharani’s stratagems. If tomorrow the country slipped from their hands, they must still possess, in many places throughout the state, cattle, houses, mansions, gardens and groves, and ponds for bathing as their own property. Whoever owned such things was to be intimidated into submission; whatever land and property they had was to be seized at extremely low prices in the names of the king and Maharani. Accepting the queen’s command, the revelry-loving chiefs set out at once to perform that duty. They seized without count.

Those affected carried their appeals to the newly established tribunal. However much the British might be imperial rulers, there was a reputation that most of them, even when courts imposed punishments according to law, acted according to justice and fairness. How far that reputation was true was another matter! But to the chief judge and assistant judges who had taken office in Kollaipuram, all the practices of the state were strange. One day, a citizen brought a case before the tribunal of an assistant judge.

He complained that he had been pressured to sell his valuable land, garden, and swimming pool cheaply in the Maharani’s name,

<!-- source scan 35; printed page 30 -->

and that because he had refused to sell, he had received a notice from one of the state’s senior officials saying that they would occupy them. He asked the tribunal to cancel that plan of occupation and give judgment in his favour. The Maharani’s “people’s army” tried to prevent the man bringing the case from even entering the tribunal gate, causing a major clash. The genuine “people’s liberation fighters,” who stood organised against both the state and imperial domination, confronted and overcame the Maharani’s people’s army and made a way for the plaintiff to enter the tribunal.

The assistant judge began hearing the case. Those arguing for the Maharani and king declared categorically: “This entire case is false! It is true that, as the plaintiff says, the government sent notice that it would occupy the property. But there is no swimming pool there, no cultivated land—everything is merely grazing ground!,” Alarmed, the plaintiff stepped before the judge and showed photographs of the swimming pool, the cultivated land, and the flourishing garden. “Your Honour, if you would kindly come and see the place in person, you can know the truth,” he pleaded emotionally, even shedding a tear. The assistant judge announced, “At the petitioner’s request, those places will be inspected in person.” No sooner had he given that ruling than—

<!-- source scan 36; printed page 31 -->

stones rained down in a barrage upon the judge’s desk. Four or five employees suffered bleeding injuries. The judge immediately sprang from his seat in agitation and angrily asked those arguing for the state, “What kind of barbarism is all this?” They knew that the stones had been thrown by the palace’s hired force. Even so, they composed themselves and told the assistant judge:

“Our state has a culture all its own! There was once something called the Stone Age. This is a cultural campaign demonstrating that it still exists today!”

The assistant judge’s face reddened at that evasive reply. “This cannot be left alone! Come. Let us go to the chief judge and complain about this. Give him your explanation there!” he said, and set out with them to meet the chief judge.

In the corridor leading to the chief judge’s tribunal room stood ten or fifteen men. As the assistant judge and the plaintiff approached them, the men stripped off their clothes and displayed themselves, shouting, “Hey! Withdraw the case!” and leaping about. Shocked, the assistant judge turned to those arguing for the state and rebuked them: “What is this obscenity? What is this indecency?”

<!-- source scan 37; printed page 32 -->

But they replied calmly:

“Sir, Assistant Judge! Stones fell upon your desk—that was a culture referring to the age when stone appeared in human history! What you are seeing now is a live picture of the culture from the age ‘when stone appeared before earth appeared’!” Not only the white judge’s face but his entire body reddened with fury.

At that moment the plaintiff, who had filed the case over his lands, interrupted and told the judge:

“Your Honour, Assistant Judge! You have seen the culture of the age when stone appeared—and now the culture of the age when ‘stone appeared before earth appeared’! In the next corridor you are going to see yet another culture: ‘the ancient culture that appeared before the age when stone appeared before earth appeared.’”

The assistant judge flinched. “What on earth will that culture be like?” he asked in alarm.

“The women’s regiment of the palace army is waiting there!” came the reply. That was enough. The assistant judge abandoned his journey to complain to the chief judge and, without looking back, ran toward his own tribunal room.

Not only the justice due to those who filed cases, but also the revolution waiting for its moment to erupt against Kollaipuram’s “culture,” remained waiting!
