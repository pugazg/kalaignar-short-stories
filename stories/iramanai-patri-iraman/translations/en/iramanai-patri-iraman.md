As far as the episode of Vali's killing is concerned, no one has accepted Rama's conduct; all these are characters

<!-- source scan 67; printed page 65 -->

and must be viewed with that awareness. It is neither proper nor good to look at them by inventing something else. In that characterization, rather than I or anyone else saying that Rama's act of striking Vali from concealment was wrong, Rama himself says so. Where? In the episode of Vali's killing? No. He says it after Ravana has died. Those who have read the Kamba Ramayanam may ask, “What new story is this? I do not remember him saying that.”

Ravana lies dead on the battlefield. Rama comes with Ravana's younger brother Vibhishana and looks at him. As he looks, he sees a scar on Ravana's back. Rama says, “Alas! Did I kill a man who had a scar on his back? It is not fitting for a warrior to kill a man wounded in the back, a man bearing such a scar. Did I kill one who had a scar on his back?” He grieves.

“Though he died, the fame that stands in the world
would not resound in splendour;
my enemies, delighting in their feast,
would smile and feed upon my fame.
O chest made for ornaments!
A victory gained over a man
who seemed to have turned his back in battle—
even that victory is false,” he said.

<!-- source scan 68; printed page 66 -->

Then Vibhishana says—or rather, Kamban makes Vibhishana say: “Rama, that scar was not caused by showing his back in battle. Once before, when my elder brother Ravana fought an elephant, the elephant's tusks entered his chest, pierced through it and emerged above his back. That is the scar they left.”

“Four heads broken,
unseen by those who came near,
like a split mountain,
the great elephant at the far boundary—
its striking tusks entered
and sank through the cruel back;
that alone was the scar, not one
made by an enemy's weapon—what could such weapons do?”

“All those marks upon his chest
lay there like ornaments; when the warrior's
mighty arm resounded in battle long ago,
when Death was met amid the fight,
even the diamond-hard arrow
with all its force, and the thrust
that came upon him,
went on toward the back.”

Kamban says that Vibhishana thus reveals Ravana's valour. And so Rama himself is afraid.

“We have killed a man who bore a scar on his back; blame will come upon us!” Those who argue for Kamban and those who argue for Rama should think about how many days Rama—who lays down the rules of valour—must have suffered inwardly over having killed Vali from concealment. That is why I say this.

Here too I am arguing on Rama's side. For he suffers so much: we have killed a man who had a scar on his back; our valour has been shaken! We have incurred blame! One must also think how much that same Rama would have grieved over the fault of having killed Vali while hidden.
