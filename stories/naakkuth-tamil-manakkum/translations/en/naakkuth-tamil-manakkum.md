You must have heard of the poet Kalamega Pulavar! He was a great master at composing poems with wordplay. Once, while travelling on pilgrimage through many towns, he is said to have reached Nagai. The poet was very hungry. Thinking he might buy rice at some eating place and eat, he saw some young men playing marbles near the temple and asked, “Brother, where is rice sold?”

<!-- source scan 78; printed page 76 -->

At once a mischievous youth replied, “Rice gets stuck in the throat,” and laughed. Angered, Kalamega Pulavar picked up a piece of charcoal to curse the young men and wrote on a wall:

“For the betel-chewing boys, the tongue...”

Before he could finish writing, the temple bell rang. In his hurry to go to the temple, Kalamegam left what he had written on the wall and went away. After buying food at the temple and eating, he came out and returned to the wall with the charcoal to complete the curse he intended for those youths. But before he arrived, the young men had already completed the line:

‘For the betel-chewing boys,
the tongue is fragrant with Tamil in good Nagai”

So it stood!

If even Kalamegam, who had intended to punish those youths, was delighted by their command of Tamil, is it any wonder that in that good Nagai, when fine young people whose tongues were fragrant with Tamil came to the stage from many regions carrying Anna torches, showing through the light in their eyes their affection and attachment to the Kazhagam, and raising resounding cries of enthusiasm,
