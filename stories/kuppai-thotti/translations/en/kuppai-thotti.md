# Kuppai Thotti

> **English translation of the verified Tamil archival reading.** Controlling source: 1977 anthology `கலைஞர் கருணாநிதியின் சிறுகதைகள்`, scans **39–46**, printed pages **30–37**. The canonical Tamil remains authoritative. Page-boundary comments are retained for traceability.

I have been doing penance for a long time by the roadside, right beneath that upstairs house. My penance is not meant to summon any god and obtain some extraordinary boon! Only yesterday did I learn in detail how many kings performed penance so they could rule the whole world; how demons undertook terrible austerities so that no one would be able to destroy them; and how sages plunged into penance to win boons with which they could avenge themselves on enemies through curses. Penance means sitting in one place with a single thought, thinking of nothing else, longing to see God without food or sustenance, and then, when he appears, asking for what one needs! As far as I am concerned, other than sitting unmoving in one place, I possess none of the other marks of penance.

Even those who truly undertook penance found it difficult to keep their minds fixed in one state. In the celestial world there is an entire women's battalion ready for the sole purpose of wandering about, finding out who is doing penance, where and when, and ruining that penance. Its notable stars are Menaka,

<!-- source scan 40; printed page 31 -->

Rambha, Urvashi and Tilottama. Whether those women go and spoil the ascetics, or whether the ascetics themselves perform penance expecting those women to come and spoil them—both are matters worthy of investigation!

Yesterday, into my belly, where all sorts of things keep falling, there fell an old purana. It was not in book form. A few leaves here and there! More than half had been eaten away by insects! It was from those pages that I read and learned the explanations and stories about penance: Menaka's exploits when she came to ruin Viswamitra's austerities; the amorous play she performed after the sage fell into her net; the games of love they carried on by forest waterfalls; how the hermitage meant for thinking of God came to look like the dancing hall of Kama; how, before Maheswara arrived, the flower-arrowed god came and delighted in rolling the great sage about in the woman's lap and at her feet; how the hand that had held a rosary became trapped between Menaka's thighs; and how the sage's trembling lips yearned to drink the honey of Menaka's soft lips. I was reading all this with great relish when, just then, that garbage-cart man had to come and spoil everything.

The fellow came, went *mana-mana*, emptied my belly, picked up that torn purana too, threw it into his cart and carried it away. For days at a time my belly will be swollen and I will sit there struggling; at such times the municipal garbage cart will not show its face on this side for four or five days. I never expected it to throw dirt on my purana-reading like this. Perhaps some new officer has come this way; I do not know. For the past two or three days there has been unusual zeal about cleaning the street. How long will that last? There is a proverb: “When the mother-in-law first arrived she played ball; as time went on the mother-in-law became like a donkey.” It will end up just like that.

<!-- source scan 41; printed page 32 -->

Four or five houses away from where I stand is a bhajan hall. Purana discourses are often held there. I listen through the loudspeaker. Only after seeing that old purana did I realize that the Bhagavatars had been coating over and smoothing out many things in their telling. I learned by reading it that Ahalya, even after discovering that the man who entered her house in her husband's absence and violated her was Indra, embraced him in contentment, saying, “Ah! So this is pleasure! Only today have I found the delicious feast of delight for which I have longed all this time.”

There were more than a thousand ascetics in the Daruka forest! Their wives were ten-and-a-half-touch gold in the gleam of their bodies! Unplucked parijata blossoms! Young vines longing to be embraced! The fullness of their youth hooked even God and drew him in. One or two? He crushed and cast away a thousand flowers of worship! The wives of sages asking for boons! The Supreme One who had to grant them! They embraced until their arms reddened! He played at desire until his eyes reddened! Do you know how delicately the Bhagavatar at the bhajan hall narrated this remarkable source tale?

He said that Paramasivan had bestowed grace upon the wives of the Daruka-forest sages and taken possession of them. The bhajan group listened, expressed their appreciation with “Ah! Ah!!”, ate the prasadam, and then threw their used leaves into my belly. One of those devotees, perhaps unable to find a leaf on which to eat his prasadam, seems to have used a page from a book. I looked closely at that crumpled page, stained with rice and curry. Even I felt ashamed. Some emotion began to grow within me. Does being a garbage bin mean I have no feelings? You too must be curious to know what was on that page. I shall tell you! A brass lamp is burning. From that, the time

<!-- source scan 42; printed page 33 -->

may be concluded to be night. A woman is praying to the central man in an event that happened at night! We normally imagine that a woman's prayer would contain none of the indecencies of the night. But the flavour of this prayer rises high enough to deceive us. Let us look again at the description. In the light of the brass lamp, a bed appears. And what a bed! Upon it, a soft mattress! A mattress strewn with jasmine, mullai and rose blossoms! Would the scene be enjoyable if there were only lamp, bed and flower-strewn mattress? Even if there are thousands of stars, what beauty has the sky without the milk-white moon? As that milk-white moon, there lies a woman upon the bed. She is a maiden with flower-laden tresses! Her name is Nappinnai!

Even bed, mattress and lovely maiden are not enough to make the picture complete! With his chest resting upon that maiden's mango-like breasts lies the man to whom the prayer is addressed. Who is that man? A mere man? No, no—the Lord! And the woman praying to that Lord is Andal! Andal, wishing to call to him, “O flower-chested one, open your mouth and grant me grace!”, does not call him to where she is; she goes straight into his bedchamber. And what she sees there is the festive scene in the room lit by the brass lamp!

<!-- source display: four-line quoted verse/prayer stanza; lineation preserved -->

“While the brass lamp burns, upon the cot with carved legs,  
on the soft, fivefold bed,  
with flower-tressed Nappinnai's breasts beneath you,  
O flower-chested one lying there, open your mouth!”

That is the verse I read on the crumpled page. Alas, could not this drain-side where I sit suddenly become a *sabarakooda* couch? Could not a female garbage bin come and join me here? If such luck came, how happily I would live with her! I would speak all kinds of new words of love. Instead of calling her “darling,” I would embrace her and call her “garbage-darling.” I would gossip about the whole town with her. Then we would never know how time passed. Andal's poem created all these romantic thoughts in my heart. Poor Andal—whose daughter was she? They say the parents who bore her threw her away, and some Alvar picked her up and raised her! Why that lady mixed so much erotic flavour into worship of God, I do not know. One song alone has put me through all this; if I got all her songs, they would have to take me and leave me beside an asylum.

<!-- source scan 43; printed page 34 -->

Do I always get such pages and books? Most of the time it is a used food-leaf, a banana peel, orange peel, kamala-orange peel—something of that sort keeps coming. Sometimes they even throw dead rats into my belly. I sit holding my nose and waiting for the municipality cart to come. After midnight, a servant boy from a house at the end of the street comes near me.

He looks all around, throws into me the caps from whisky, brandy and other liquor bottles, and hurries away. All night my body trembles: what if the police suspect me, carry me off and put me inside under the Prohibition Act? Then I console myself: after all, what does it matter where a garbage bin is!

One day a man who drove past me in a car from the third house tore up a letter from his shirt pocket, threw it into me and went away. I read it eagerly. It had been torn irregularly, so I could not read it continuously. Some cheat had promised to get a woman a schoolteacher's job, taken in advance as payment for it

<!-- source scan 44; printed page 35 -->

three hundred rupees, and cheated her. I did not know his name. I did not know the woman's name either. Neither name remained in the letter; those parts had been torn away. Whoever they were, I understood one thing: it was business belonging to our “department.” The person to whom she had appealed also seems not to have cared; as proof, he had thrown the letter away to me. Alas! What misery! What became of that woman? I would rather never see her. Not only helpless women like her; more and more, I have begun to hate the very sight of human beings. They are impatient—possessed of hasty minds—frenzied—enemies of reason: I feel like saying all this. Merely thinking of one ungrateful man who lived in my own shelter makes anger boil in me. One day he came from somewhere; he could barely walk. His whole body trembled. In spite of myself I felt an overpowering *thoorattram* toward him! Thinking, let it go, I tolerated it and asked, “Who are you, man?” He looked at me and said, “Can't you tell by looking? I am one of the kings of this country. I have come to administer my kingdom from beside you. Will you give me a little shade?” I said, “Am I so grown and prosperous that I can give shade? If you like, take shelter behind me and rule your kingdom!” And so for ten days he sat—lay down—slept—ate behind me! From there his government ran. Showing his teeth to passers-by, he collected the taxes his government required, in coins or cash.

For ten days I too passed the time well with him. I forgot myself enjoying the cinema songs and folk songs he sang. One night this wandering Maharaja brought a woman with him. I pretended to sleep

<!-- source scan 45; printed page 36 -->

and watched what happened. As she came, she kept muttering that she had to leave quickly. “Hey, don't fly away; here, take the money!” said the Maharaja, giving her an eight-anna coin. I shut my eyes tight and kept only my ears stretched open.

<!-- source display: three isolated quoted lines; separation preserved -->

‘Darling!’

‘My nose!’

‘Oh, my king of love!’

A few such words of endearment came out of that hoarse throat. That wretched Maharaja had not even the shame to remember that I, his friend, was right beside him! A little later I opened my eyes. My friend lay curled up asleep. Forgive me—the king of this country was in royal slumber. The Maharani was nowhere to be seen. Perhaps she had gone to the next kingdom. The next morning two or three policemen were talking with my friend. They were speaking at a distance, so nothing reached my ears clearly enough to understand. Afterwards my friend came near me and said, “Don't be afraid on seeing the police! Don't think they have come to arrest me! Some great minister from abroad is coming, it seems; they are calling this king to meet him.”

In astonishment I asked, “Is that so? Where will you go to meet that minister?”

“I will go and stay in my usual jail-palace. He will come and meet me there. You too may even see him, because his procession is coming along this very side.”

Saying this, he climbed into the big car the police had brought and went away. As he had said, the next day the foreign minister did indeed come along the street where I stand. But that friend never came back to see me. One of the kings of this country, after all—

<!-- source scan 46; printed page 37 -->

I do not know behind which garbage bin he went to take shelter! Ungrateful fellow.

What can I say... In my lifeless life I have encountered everything. How many wedding invitations have I read—how many feasts have I consumed—how many invitations to auspicious events and inauspicious events! How many secret letters torn up and thrown away by lovers—I have read them and laughed thinking of them. Everything rejected, everything hidden, has been lovingly welcomed by my two arms.

I am one step more patient than the pure ascetics who endure everything with a smile.

So many people in this country suffer without even a bellyful of rice. My belly, the more it is emptied, keeps filling again. Oh! While I am talking to you, some woman is coming furtively; she hides herself for a moment; yes, there is a child in her hand. A newborn baby! It has only just been born. Alas, its neck is all blood! Is the child sleeping? No—it is dead.

Its neck has been broken—yes, she is the child's mother! She is putting the child into my belly and leaving—she has thrown it in; she is running away; she has left me to get caught by the police and is running—why is she running? I understand! I understand! There is no *thali* around her neck.
