# Ayyo Raja!

Do you know the Kodambakkam “railway gate” in Madras? There is hardly anyone in the city who has not cursed that gate. From actors, actresses, producers and workers rushing to studios to devotees going to the Lord of Vadapalani temple, people in cars, carts and rickshaws and people on foot will all be held up there, waiting for the red light in front of the gate to go out. After standing there in penance for a quarter of an hour, sometimes nearly half an hour, only then can they move. Comedians sitting in their cars will sing to themselves, “Kāmi Sathyabhama, open the door!” Devotees will make even the man beside them move aside with “Move a little, child—the sanctum is hidden!” and stare eagerly at the red light, waiting for it to go out.

Meanwhile comes the invasion of Tamil Nadu’s little darlings. With the self-need song, “Ayya sami! Give me a quarter-anna, sami!” they surround the cars too.

Trusting entirely in that “railway gate,” a few traders have spread their shops along both sides. When the gate is shut and time drags on, those traders alone are seen with faces growing brighter and brighter.

Perhaps a few others might be lovers whose love is still in bud. Apart from them, everyone else wears a face dripping with irritation. Sometimes the gate opens and vehicles and people have only just begun to stream through when the bell rings again to close it. Not even a quarter of the waiting line will have disappeared.

A writer, it seems, had promised a film producer that he would deliver a story outline in the morning. The producer sent a car to bring the writer along with the outline. The writer had not even thought about the story. He got into the car, deciding that at least he could go in person and pacify the producer.

They reached the Kodambakkam “gate.” The writer was delighted. Sitting in the car, he wrote the story outline and even dialogue for a couple of scenes. Only after that did the gate open. The film producer’s “money purse” opened for the writer as well.

A lady, having had her head shaved at the Lord of Vadapalani temple, is said to have been returning home in great devotion. On the way the “gate” closed. By the time it opened again, the lady was supposedly seen with hair long enough to braid into a plait!

There are many such amusing tall tales about that “gate.” But the central character of one sorrow-flavoured tale among them is that Muthamma there, at the corner of the gate, selling murukku and masala vadai.

Muthamma cannot be more than thirty. One can tell merely by looking. Every day, for hours at a time, she sees film-star women who pass that way hiding their age. They too have watched Muthamma closely.

For the very sight of Muthamma selling murukku and masala vadai has a beauty of its own. Her glass bangles go *kala-kala*—

—and as they ring, who could help surrendering his attention to the artistry with which she drives away flies and protects those stale vadais and lifeless murukkus?

If a film suddenly required someone to play just such a role, would not Muthamma be an unpaid teacher for it?

Among the causes of the stomach disorders, poisonous fevers and other cruel illnesses that roam that neighbourhood, Muthamma’s snack stall too had become one.

What could the poor woman do? Her husband, who had worked all day and brought home four annas or eight annas, left her a child and then, unable to bear their poverty, put his head before an electric train.

She had many times heard, through the gramophone box in the neighbouring shop, the song “Everyone must live......Muthamma!” That song served only to amuse the baby bouncing in her lap.

“Everyone must live; yes, everyone ought to live! But who lets anyone live?” Such questions did strike Muthamma’s heart. Who but she knew the misery she endured every night, after her husband’s death, at the hands of labourers in that area? Pulling the mouth of the child at her breast away while it cried and writhed, for the sake of four annas and eight annas—ayyo......that Muthamma......what other way was there? Her husband had found his “good way” and gone. Love soaked in the blood of her womb had taken the form of a child and now smiled at her face. To keep it alive, she had to smile at the faces of many men. Whether there was life in that smile or not, she had to show her teeth. Such was the trade she had taken up.

By day, murukku and masala vadai; by night, Muthamma was a shop selling pleasure to rough men. All of it was for her child.

The child’s name was Raja. Perhaps she called him that so that, at least in name, he might be a king. Was he not her king? For that Raja she had been forced to become a queen by night. She did not like that condition; but what was she to do? When flies swarmed over her basket of snacks, she kept driving them away without caring about the ache in her hand. But could every fly be driven off? One or two touched a snack and only then flew away.

A snack touched by a fly has no chastity to lose; it does not become ruined for that reason—only its taste is spoiled. For Muthamma, both the taste of life and chastity had been spoiled. What did it matter whether flies swarmed on a rubbish heap, or ants swarmed, or even a dog lay on it?...... The “jewel among Tamil women” had come to such a conclusion. Her one aim was that Raja should grow up, have a family of his own, and that she should live to see it with delighted eyes. She sold her body in order to protect her own blood.

Her strongly built body helped that trade. During the day, admirers who came by the railway “gate” would approach as though buying murukku or vadai, in order to fix a date and time with her. Alongside one business, a time would be fixed for the quoted “base event” as well. Without understanding the meaning of those cruel conversations, her beloved Raja would press his face to her breast and stare about wide-eyed. Muthamma was indeed a widow. But that costume did not suit her trade.

Only the *thali* cord was missing from her neck. Every other sign appeared with the special adornment of a married woman: vermilion on the forehead, flowers in the hair, and on the hands—

glass bangles—a black blouse—a white sari. On some days, a white blouse and a black sari. Even dolls held in the hand and played with have to be beautified before they can be sold. Only when dolls of desire are decorated can the show go on.

For little Raja she had bought a clay doll. It taught her that lesson. After that lesson, the finery increased by another degree.

Raja! Raja!! Raja!!! That dear name was always rolling like a dewdrop over her bruised lips. “Raja! A rose among thorns!” she would say to herself, raining kisses on him. After her husband died, she had never kissed anyone else with the emotional tremor of her lips. All those were dry kisses. Raja’s cheeks alone were pressed by her lips with tenderness and affection. One day danger came to that Raja who was dearer than life. As usual, Muthamma did not come to her business at the railway “gate.”

Raja had suddenly developed a fever; his body had become like a block of fire. Through the night she tried whatever remedies she knew. By morning the child’s illness had become severe. His very face had changed. The smile that had rested on those little lips had run off somewhere and vanished. Muthamma did not know what to do.

Nearby stood the Lord of Palani temple......she prayed. After that the child lost consciousness too. Suddenly she remembered something. There was a doctor she knew in Thyagaraya Nagar. She expected that he would treat the child without asking for money. In this cruel moment, do not ask how she happened to know him.

She lifted Raja onto her shoulder and left the house. Raja sagged limply. She realised that she too could not walk such a distance—

—for she had stayed awake all night with the child, and in between had stayed awake for the money another man had given her, thinking, could that money not become medicine for the child? Muthamma was utterly exhausted. A rickshaw-puller came toward her. He was someone she knew. “Will you take me as far as Thyagaraya Nagar? I have no money in my hand,” Muthamma cried.

“I’ll come to Thyagaraya Nagar. And I’ll come to your house too—is that all right?” the rickshaw-puller said, grinning.

Raja had to live! Weeping, Muthamma said “all right” and climbed into the rickshaw. Perhaps he too wanted Raja to survive. After all, was he not poor himself? He pulled the rickshaw very fast.

Muthamma sat holding Raja against her breast, sustained only by the hope that he would recover.

The Kodambakkam “gate” came across their path. The rickshaw stopped. Muthamma saw the red light burning. She had seen that red light countless days before. Never had it shocked her like this.

“Ayyo! It looks as though my Raja’s life will go out before that light goes out!” she cried. The rickshaw-puller tried to console her. He touched Raja’s body. The child’s breathing had begun to come in a strange way. Muthamma wailed aloud.

The light went out. The gate opened. The rickshaw moved. Raja’s life had not yet gone. Muthamma kept crying, “Muruga! Muruga! Palaniyandi!” The rickshaw too sped onward.

Look—the doctor’s house she had to reach was now only another furlong away. Policemen stopped the rickshaw.

It was not only Muthamma’s rickshaw. Many rickshaws, many carts, many cars, hundreds of people had been stopped, and traffic itself had been brought to a halt.

The rickshaw-puller asked a man, “What’s the occasion?”

“The King of Nepal is coming. That’s why,” he replied.

“How long will it be like this?” Muthamma asked through her tears.

“Don’t they have to gather all the people in one place and show that the whole country is welcoming the King of Nepal? That’s why even those who don’t want to stand here are made to stand and watch the spectacle,” said a young man, pacing this way and that. Time kept passing. Perhaps Muthamma’s tears too had dried up.

She had lost herself, holding Raja face to face. After a delay of three-quarters of an hour, amid the grand preparations of the police officers, the King of Nepal passed in procession, receiving dry smiles as his welcome.

After that king had gone, Muthamma looked at Raja’s face. She started. The child had gone limp.

“The King of Nepal has gone, hasn’t he? Even now, are we not allowed to go?” cried Muthamma. The rickshaw raced to the doctor’s house. Just as Muthamma had hoped, the doctor examined the child. The only information he could give was that the child had been dead for half an hour.

Realising that her Raja’s life had gone even before the King of Nepal went past, Muthamma rolled on the ground and wept. “If you had come half an hour earlier, I would at least have tried somehow to save the child,” the doctor said sorrowfully.

What else could Muthamma do but cry, “Ayyo Raja!” Before her eyes, the Kodambakkam “railway gate” and the ring of police officers who had blocked traffic appeared again and again. The rickshaw-puller said sympathetically that he would collect the “fare” he had asked for on another day, and took leave of her.
