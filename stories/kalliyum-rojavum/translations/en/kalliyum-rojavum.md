# The Cactus and the Rose

<!-- source scan 22; printed page 21 -->

If we imagine the Tamil land in which we live as a flower garden, we see the rose of *Silappathikaram*, the champak called *Civaka Cintamani*, the bouquet of *Kundalakesi*, the jasmine of *Manimekalai*, and the newly blossomed mullai called Akam-Puram spreading fragrance. We see the poems of Bharathiyar and Bharathidasan flourishing as the breeze that gathers and carries the fragrance of those flowers in abundance. In such a garden, rich with splendour, there is also a

<!-- source scan 23; printed page 22 -->

cactus patch in one corner. Nor is a scentless kanakambaram absent. Nor are there absent great anthills inhabited by snakes that frighten one out of taking a leisurely evening stroll.

I call it a cactus. To some it may seem harsh. Because people have not entered and looked within, *Kamba Ramayanam* has grown as a cactus in Tamil's flower garden. The cactus flower has beauty. Just as Kambar's story has poetic grace! The cactus flower has no fragrance. Just as Kambar's picture has no ethnic consciousness!

... *Ramayanam* is an obstacle to Tamil feeling!

I called *Silappathikaram* a rose; because the rose has thorns.

There may be events in *Silappathikaram* beyond imagination. But when the anklet sounds, the character of classical Tamil sounds. Cheran's valour, Cholan's generosity of mind, Pandiyan's integrity — in all, references to the three great lands ruled by the three crowned kings — abound there. In that treasure of delight given by Ilango! *Silappathikaram* is the golden rose fashioned by Cheran's younger brother!
