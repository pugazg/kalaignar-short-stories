# Pretha Visaranai

Dr. Pichumoorthi was turning the pages of a book on good conduct while spreading the smoke of a ‘Wills’ cigarette throughout the room. Compounder Kandasami Naidu arranged the medicine bottles neatly and locked the cupboard. After adjusting his blue coat once, he went toward the ‘operation room’, switched off the ‘five-hundred-power’ electric lamp burning there, and tapped a kannappu-kannathapadi powder tin. The clock struck ‘taan taan’; it was nine. “Lock up quickly and let us leave,” the doctor said, throwing away his cigarette and tossing the book onto the table as he rose.

From the street came the beat of a drum, followed by a man’s voice. “Tomorrow morning at eight o’clock, at Vinai Theertha Swami Temple, Harijan temple entry will take place with great ceremony. Devotees, come...come.” It was an advertisement for temple entry. “What, Compounder! The town is in an uproar—surely all of you agree with it?” The doctor asked the question and looked eagerly at Naidu’s face.

“What is it to us, sir....those who sow millet....will reap millet....hm....the world is going to turn upside down, go on,” Naidugaru said wearily, taking the bunch of keys and beginning to move away while spinning it on his finger—

as he walked. Dr. Pichumoorthi switched off the ‘office light’ too and came outside.

The two were coming out. In the darkness, only the light near the entrance could be seen dimly. Near the hospital doorstep a figure came forward with faltering steps. “Ah....ayyo.....appa sami....” It came a little faster and climbed the hospital steps.

The doctor shone the ‘torch’ in his hand. A woman under thirty. A torn red sari. Dry, disordered hair flying in the wind. She closed her eyes against the light. Fully pregnant. Her trembling, convulsing hands and legs showed how terribly she was suffering in labour. She could not stand. Holding the front of the doorway, she bent over. “Ah....ayyo....God....sami, save me....you will earn a crore of merits.” She did not speak; she wailed.

“What cursed nuisance is this?” the doctor muttered.

“Where is your husband? You come here with no one and make this oppathiravam?....” The compounder complained as he switched off the street light too. “I have no one, sir....you are like God, you must save me....ah....ayyo....appa.” The pregnant woman began wailing again.

“Hm....quickly lock the ‘gate’,” the doctor ordered the compounder, then shone the ‘torch’ once again on the woman’s face....“An orphan, she says, an orphan....and an orphan wants a child!”....he mocked. “Everything is for money,” the compounder said with a laugh, offering these words before the doctor and feeling proud that he had said something very clever.

“Hey....you....what caste are you? Do you have money to give the doctor?” Naidugaru asked, twisting his moustache as he looked at her.

“I am....a paraichi, sami—where would I have money?”

She had not even finished.....“Pthoo....para dog....drive the donkey away....hm....stand aside!” Dr. Pichumoorthi hurried down the steps. The compounder let out a dreadful laugh that seemed to scatter sparks....“A Harijan woman? Deliver in the hospital, at midnight! Tomorrow morning go and do temple entry!” He finished this little sermon too....and switched off the ‘board light’.

“Vinai Theerthavar Village Charity Hospital. Treatment available at all hours.” The advertisement board disappeared into the darkness.

The doctor and compounder began walking quickly.

“Oh God....have you no mercy?” the paraichi cried aloud; those words, mixed with disappointment, struck the wall of the nearby Vinai Theertha Andavan temple and echoed back.

With a ‘girrr’, a motor car stopped.....

“Doctor sir....Ayya at the pannai has a headache. He has asked that you be brought urgently.”—the driver finished his message.

“Compounder! Do you have the medical case?” the doctor asked as he climbed into the car and sat down.

“I have it,” Naidugaru nodded and sat in the front seat. “Is it very serious?” Pichumoorthi asked the driver, urgently awaiting an answer. The motor car flew away.

Here....the paraichi, holding the doorstep and withering in pain. “Ah....ammadi....ayyo God....” The sound did not stop. But ‘those’ words were still falling like whip-lashes on her heart. Like a diamond struck by a sledgehammer, her thoughts shattered.

The compounder’s mocking question....“Where is your husband?”....‘Husband—’ Unable to bear the agony—

she writhed like a worm in fire, and her mouth muttered that word, but now with some anger.

‘Husband’—she did not cry! She stood like a tree. Before her heart a page turned. A history of three years!

Karuppayi was Kathamuthu’s wife. Kathamuthu was a pannai labourer on the big estate of Solaiyur village. The landlord Annamalai Mudaliar was a man who had earned the name ‘Dharmadurai’. If it was a matter of building temples and conducting kumbabhishekams, Annamalai Mudaliar had enormous enthusiasm. “Ah, how much charity? How many choultries? He is a noble man in whom dharma itself has taken form!” This was the litany of Parthasarathi Iyengar, who lived in a portion of the pannai landlord’s property.

“That is a temple built by Annamalai Mudaliar, sir. It must dance as he makes it dance; yes! Temple entry or mannavaathu! He certainly will not agree to letting Pallu-Parai people inside. He will not consent to such an unjust thing, you know?” This was the roar of Kannayiram Pillai, who kept accounts at the pannai!

Three years earlier, the renovation of the Mushika Vinayagar temple had been carried out on a grand scale. In the present temple-entry season, Bhagiratha-like efforts were being made to let Harijans enter it. While the temple was being built, a black stone being raised to the sanctum fell and killed Karuppayi’s husband Kathamuthu.

The solid, handsome Kathamuthu was crushed and destroyed. For the great Ganapathi’s temple he broke his head like a coconut and conducted the first kumbabhishekam with his blood. Leaving behind Karuppayi, dearer than life, he became a sacrifice in the Lord’s own temple. By sacrificing Kathamuthu, Annamalai Mudaliar completed the Ganapathi temple. A year passed.......

Karuppayi lived on. One day Karuppayi was clearing dung in the pannaiyar’s cattle shed. Annamalai Mudaliar entered, asking, “What, is the work finished?” Karuppayi was startled by the Mudaliar’s unprecedented visit....The Mudaliar’s lust ran wild. Poor Karuppayi was the sacrifice.

Days rolled on. In her bitter life Karuppayi tasted the sweetness of sugar candy. Who receives the master’s favour? Karuppayi was pregnant. She was ashamed to show her face outside. The Mudaliar issued his final warning: if she remained in the village, he would cut off her head. An emergency law; could that ‘appeal’ paraichi withstand it? Was it not the master’s order? Karuppayi was subjected to desa-prashtam—banishment.

The very same Mudaliar who had eagerly entered the cattle shed ordered his lover, “Run away somewhere toward the forest!” She not only delighted in thinking of the master’s compassion, his broad-minded refusal to recognise caste distinctions, his socialist principle of showing affection to the poor—she also took pride in the beauty of her own strong, well-built body. Dreaming that with the Mudaliar’s favour she might repair her ruined life to some extent and at least raise her child, Karuppayi finally began wandering the country. The end of that wandering? She stands in pain at the doorstep of Vinai Theerthavar Charity Hospital.

Morning came. Arrangements for temple entry were taking place with great pomp. Dr. Pichumoorthi, wearing a khadi cap, was moving here and there with the temple trustee.

There was a great crowd on the bank of the temple tank.

“Lift...lift...slowly, slowly...lift gently.”

“Haanng....that’s it....”

“Hey...you too, join in....”

“The step will be slippery; get down slowly....”

The crowd standing on the bank was warning the men who had gone down into the tank in this way.

“The deity itself couldn’t bear this business of letting a chandalan into the temple...an obstacle of ill omen has occurred.” Thus one man laid down the preface to a sermon.

“A custom unseen for generation after generation. Would the Lord tolerate this outrage? They have polluted the sacred tank itself, appa.”—the conversation of two more people went this way.

“Of all days, did she have to fall and die today? Ah, what a pity.”—one compassionate soul spoke thus.

“She was pregnant, appa,” an old woman sympathised.

“Two souls......today Ganga Devi has claimed them,” declared a knower of the soul!

The corpse of Karuppayi, who had fallen into the tank and killed herself, was brought to the bank. The police inspected the corpse. Arrangements were made for the post-mortem inquiry. The same hospital that had driven the paraichi away the previous night saying there was no place for her now admitted that paraichi’s corpse freely. Pichumoorthi began his work in aid of the post-mortem inquiry. To him and his compounder, the cause of Karuppayi’s death?...

The town said it was God’s divine play! The doctor acted as though he were discovering the cause! Does the world know the truth—that the defect in the social order, the sickness in the way the country runs, turned into poison and killed Karuppayi? Who knows? Only if that is known can the post-mortem inquiry be said to have succeeded, can it not?
