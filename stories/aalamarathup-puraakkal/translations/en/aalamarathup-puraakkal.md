# Aalamarathup Puraakkal

The murmuring sound of waterfalls, the songs plucked by cuckoos with that sound as their sruti, and the dances shown by peacocks in tune with them made the whole forest shine like a beautiful city.

In the middle of the forest stood a great banyan tree. In the hollows of the banyan were many lovely little pigeon nests. Jewel-like pigeons peeped out from those nests and enjoyed the liveliness of the forest.

“Did you see the peacock dance?” asked one purru. Mocking the intoxication of the ‘minor’ purru, another sangeetha-purru said, “Listen there, to the cuckoo’s song!” A young purru preached, “Have you heard about the dry-minded turkey which, after trying to dance on seeing the peacock and failing, shouts that dancing itself should be forbidden?” Another young purru cried longingly, “All that is fine, but today neither I nor those who depend on me have found food!” One purru came singing a lament: “A hawk snatched away the food I found!” Another rejoiced, “Good fortune—you escaped without becoming the hawk’s feast!” While they were speaking in this way, something flew in with a whirr and settled on a branch of the tree—the

<!-- source scan 152; printed page 143 -->

valluuru! The moment they saw it, the shocked pigeons would shriek and flee into the forest. Some purrus, stumbling and colliding in their panic, would be pushed into the valluuru’s stomach, after which it would belch. In that belch, the proud words “I came, I deceived” seemed to mingle. This had become a regular event in that banyan tree—yes, in the kingdom of the pigeons.

The purrus began to think about this cruelty. “A life without peace has become an everyday event! Is there no remedy?” they cried in anguish.

Two purrus first set out to put an end to it. One was a black purru. The other was a white purru. They devised two plans to oppose the valluuru. Though the two had already had a small quarrel over the matter of “kolvinai-koduppinai,” both girded themselves and set out under the policy of driving away the valluuru. Seeing this, the banyan-tree purrus joyfully sang a “viduthalai viruththam” and began beating a “vetri murasoli.” With jubilant uproar they waited for orders from the black purru and the white purru.

One day at that time, a kavar-purru came to the banyan in a great hurry, fluttering in distress. The reason was that a hunter had taken aim at it. The hunter did not want a purru for food; he thought, “This kavar-purru is beautiful! If I catch it and take it away, I can sell it for a good price,” and followed it for that reason. Seeing the kavar-purru enter the banyan, the hunter began circling beneath the tree, determined somehow to catch it alive. The kavar-purru was terrified. It went to the banyan-tree purrus and asked for refuge. Those purrus said, “We cannot oppose the hunter.” Somehow, by intensifying its cry of distress, the kavar-purru brought some of the purrus in the tree to its side. The black purru and the white purru saw this situation. As though opposing the valluuru were not enough, the work of driving away the hunter had now been added; and the reason for this—

—was the kavar-purru’s action, was it not? The white purru regretted that if the kavar-purru had not come there, this new trouble would not have arisen.

Meanwhile the kavar-purru took the black purru aside and spoke to it about something. The two firmly planned together to drive away the hunter. Then the kavar-purru ran to the white purru and called, “You too come to drive away the hunter!”

“I will come to defeat the hunter. But afterward, will you cooperate in driving away the valluuru and making the banyan a mansion belonging only to the purrus?” the white purru asked as a condition. The kavar-purru did not agree.

Standing beneath the tree, the hunter saw the purrus gathering and talking among themselves. Thinking he could tell those very purrus to help him catch the kavar-purru, he shouted up at them, “Drive it out; drive out the kavar-purru!”

Finally the hunter decided to climb the tree and catch the kavar-purru alive, and began climbing. The kavar-purru feared that if it flew away it would become prey before the hunter’s eyes, so with the black purru’s support it prepared to oppose him. Even at the last moment the kavar-purru called to the white purru.

“You cannot come only to get your own business done; what about our cause?” asked the white purru.

At once the black purru advised the white purru, “Whatever has happened has happened; if we let the hunter go on, won’t he kill all of us?”

Hearing this, the white purru declared proudly, “I know how to bring down the hunter. I do not need the kavar-purru’s help for that.”

The black purru became angry with the white purru and called it a kangani. The white purru was not troubled by that.

The hunter had climbed the tree. The black purru and the kavar-purru held sticks in their beaks and prepared to fight him. At that moment the white purru went around behind him and pecked hard at his leg. The purrus allied with it also pecked at his leg until blood flowed. Unable to bear the pain, the hunter fell from the tree, struck his head and died.

When the hunter fell, the black purru and kavar-purru laughed in victory. The white purru rejoiced that it had completed its duty.

The next morning the valluuru struck down and killed ten purrus. Not only that: on the banyan tree the valluuru wrote, **“This is the valluuru’s tree.”**

The black purru and the white purru set out to erase those words. The black purru called the kavar-purru too. Offering one excuse or another, the kavar-purru expressed regret that it could not take part.

The white purru threw itself intensely into the work of driving the valluuru from the banyan.

The black purru too entered that effort. It called the kavar-purru to join it.

“The valluuru is certainly a cruel bird, but we can include it too and all live together in the banyan as an ishtapoorvamaana family,” the kavar-purru began to explain. The black purru became furious. It also felt ashamed, thinking the white purru would laugh at it. “The white purru told me this even then,” it lamented.

“I helped you; why shouldn’t you help me?” The black purru pecked with its beak. It struck with its “thala.” The kavar-purru too gathered its “sakthi” and opposed the black purru. Thus the two pecked one another with their beaks and beat one another with their wings, throwing the whole banyan into confusion.

The valluuru kept right on doing its own work. The white purru stared, unable to decide whether to laugh or cry at this strange spectacle. Yet it kept building its strength to attack the enemy.

For small miscellaneous helps, and when it was unwell and needed medicine, the kavar-purru would come to the white purru. The white purru would give those helps. But now the black purru would not give the kavar-purru even those helps.

All the birds in the forest who heard this story laughed and were astonished. At the same time, seeing the white purru’s intelligence, they praised and acclaimed it. Did the white purru have time to listen to praise and acclaim? Its entire aim was **to drive away the valluuru!**
