The murmuring sound of waterfalls, the songs plucked by cuckoos with that sound as their sruti, and the dances shown by peacocks in tune with them made the whole forest shine like a beautiful city.

In the middle of the forest stood a great banyan tree. In the hollows of the banyan were many lovely little pigeon nests. Jewel-like pigeons peeped out from those nests and enjoyed the liveliness of the forest.

“Did you see the peacock dance?” asked one puraa. Mocking the intoxication of the ‘minor’ puraa, another sangeetha-puraa said, “Listen there, to the cuckoo’s song!” A young puraa preached, “Have you heard about the dry-minded turkey which, after trying to dance on seeing the peacock and failing, shouts that dancing itself should be forbidden?” Another young puraa cried longingly, “All that is fine, but today neither I nor those who depend on me have found food!” One puraa came singing a lament: “A hawk snatched away the food I found!” Another rejoiced, “Good fortune—you escaped without becoming the hawk’s feast!” While they were speaking in this way, something flew in with a whirr and settled on a branch of the tree—the

<!-- source scan 152; printed page 143 -->

valluuru! The moment they saw it, the shocked pigeons would shriek and flee into the forest. Some puraas, stumbling and colliding in their panic, would be pushed into the valluuru’s stomach, after which it would belch. In that belch, the proud words “I came, I deceived” seemed to mingle. This had become a regular event in that banyan tree—yes, in the kingdom of the pigeons.

The puraas began to think about this cruelty. “A life without peace has become an everyday event! Is there no remedy?” they cried in anguish.

Two puraas first set out to put an end to it. One was a black puraa. The other was a white puraa. They devised two plans to oppose the valluuru. Though the two had already had a small quarrel over the matter of “kolvinai-koduppinai,” both girded themselves and set out under the policy of driving away the valluuru. Seeing this, the banyan-tree puraas joyfully sang a “viduthalai viruththam” and began beating a “vetri murasoli.” With jubilant uproar they waited for orders from the black puraa and the white puraa.

One day at that time, a kavar-puraa came to the banyan in a great hurry, fluttering in distress. The reason was that a hunter had taken aim at it. The hunter did not want a puraa for food; he thought, “This kavar-puraa is beautiful! If I catch it and take it away, I can sell it for a good price,” and followed it for that reason. Seeing the kavar-puraa enter the banyan, the hunter began circling beneath the tree, determined somehow to catch it alive. The kavar-puraa was terrified. It went to the banyan-tree puraas and asked for refuge. Those puraas said, “We cannot oppose the hunter.” Somehow, by intensifying its cry of distress, the kavar-puraa brought some of the puraas in the tree to its side. The black puraa and the white puraa saw this situation. As though opposing the valluuru were not enough, the work of driving away the hunter had now been added; and the reason for this—

<!-- source scan 153; printed page 144 -->

—was the kavar-puraa’s action, was it not? The white puraa regretted that if the kavar-puraa had not come there, this new trouble would not have arisen.

Meanwhile the kavar-puraa took the black puraa aside and spoke to it about something. The two firmly planned together to drive away the hunter. Then the kavar-puraa ran to the white puraa and called, “You too come to drive away the hunter!”

“I will come to defeat the hunter. But afterward, will you cooperate in driving away the valluuru and making the banyan a mansion belonging only to the puraas?” the white puraa asked as a condition. The kavar-puraa did not agree.

Standing beneath the tree, the hunter saw the puraas gathering and talking among themselves. Thinking he could tell those very puraas to help him catch the kavar-puraa, he shouted up at them, “Drive it out; drive out the kavar-puraa!”

Finally the hunter decided to climb the tree and catch the kavar-puraa alive, and began climbing. The kavar-puraa feared that if it flew away it would become prey before the hunter’s eyes, so with the black puraa’s support it prepared to oppose him. Even at the last moment the kavar-puraa called to the white puraa.

“You cannot come only to get your own business done; what about our cause?” asked the white puraa.

At once the black puraa advised the white puraa, “Whatever has happened has happened; if we let the hunter go on, won’t he kill all of us?”

Hearing this, the white puraa declared proudly, “I know how to bring down the hunter. I do not need the kavar-puraa’s help for that.”

The black puraa became angry with the white puraa and called it a kangani. The white puraa was not troubled by that.

The hunter had climbed the tree. The black puraa and the kavar-puraa held sticks in their beaks and prepared to fight him. At that moment the white puraa went around behind him and pecked hard at his leg. The puraas allied with it also pecked at his leg until blood flowed. Unable to bear the pain, the hunter fell from the tree, struck his head and died.

When the hunter fell, the black puraa and kavar-puraa laughed in victory. The white puraa rejoiced that it had completed its duty.

The next morning the valluuru struck down and killed ten puraas. Not only that: on the banyan tree the valluuru wrote, **“This is the valluuru’s tree.”**

The black puraa and the white puraa set out to erase those words. The black puraa called the kavar-puraa too. Offering one excuse or another, the kavar-puraa expressed regret that it could not take part.

The white puraa threw itself intensely into the work of driving the valluuru from the banyan.

The black puraa too entered that effort. It called the kavar-puraa to join it.

“The valluuru is certainly a cruel bird, but we can include it too and all live together in the banyan as an ishtapoorvamaana family,” the kavar-puraa began to explain. The black puraa became furious. It also felt ashamed, thinking the white puraa would laugh at it. “The white puraa told me this even then,” it lamented.

“I helped you; why shouldn’t you help me?” The black puraa pecked with its beak. It struck with its “thala.” The kavar-puraa too gathered its “sakthi” and opposed the black puraa. Thus the two pecked one another with their beaks and beat one another with their wings, throwing the whole banyan into confusion.

The valluuru kept right on doing its own work. The white puraa stared, unable to decide whether to laugh or cry at this strange spectacle. Yet it kept building its strength to attack the enemy.

For small everyday help, or when it was unwell and needed medicine, the kavar-puraa would come to the white puraa. The white puraa would give those helps. But now the black puraa would not give the kavar-puraa even those helps.

All the birds in the forest who heard this story laughed and were astonished. At the same time, seeing the white puraa’s intelligence, they praised and acclaimed it. Did the white puraa have time to listen to praise and acclaim? Its entire aim was **to drive away the valluuru!**
