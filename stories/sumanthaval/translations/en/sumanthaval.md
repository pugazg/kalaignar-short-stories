“She who bore me; who, the moment I was born, forgot the pain she had suffered and showed me a lotus face; who smiled when I smiled, cried when I cried, and fed me nectar—the noble woman who is my mother, my deity—if one day she should leave me...

“Mother! My heart has not even the strength to think of it. Even at this advanced age, how active she is! How many family worries! Do those bony legs not get even a moment’s rest? Backyard—hall—kitchen—street entrance—stairs—somewhere or other, if even a splinter lies on the ground she darts about picking it up and throwing it out to keep the place clean. How does she manage it?

“That sacred form, worn to a shell by labour upon labour, must one day enter a sleep from which it will not wake—how am I going to bear that pain? Even when that old body of skin and bone lies there, it makes the whole house seem as though lamps have been lit everywhere. That aged woman is the pillar holding up a family crowded with little ones! How can the heart consent to lose the cool shade we find beneath that browned leaf?”

As these thoughts filled me, I wandered along the veranda. Imagining a dry future like a desert without the embrace of my mother’s loving hand, my heart seemed spent and gave out a thin sigh. A house without mother. Food not served by mother. Ears unable to delight in her affectionate speech. Eyes unable to see her gracious face. I, without mother. Would not all these be storms—earthquakes—cyclones—great whirling upheavals in my future?

Why does love for one’s mother alone toss a human being about like this? Why does it torment him? Why does the word *mother* possess such greatness? Such value? Who, in truth, is a mother? What are the qualities proper to motherhood? Does giving birth alone make a woman a mother? Then are all who give birth mothers?

The urge to find answers to these questions flew like a flag in **her** heart.

Just then my mother slowly came and stood beside me. Looking at my face with affection overflowing, she asked anxiously, “What is it, son? You look different somehow.”

“Nothing, Amma. Sit down!” I said. She seated me and sat down too.

“Amma! I have a doubt—you must explain it!” I said.

“What is it, kanna? You read without rest—you speak to the whole town—and you come to me with a doubt?” Mother teased affectionately.

“Even one who is forever reading—an incomparable genius—the world-famous Newton—may at times fail to understand the worth of a mother. My doubt arose on that basis.”

“Hmm! Go on,” Mother said.

“What is motherhood? Who is a mother? Is it the woman who gives birth? Or the woman who cares for and raises the child? I want an explanation!”

A new light appeared in Mother’s eyes. She answered calmly:

“I’ll tell you something that actually happened. Listen! When you have heard the story, your doubt will clear and you will have your explanation.” With that, she began her story. This was the story Mother told:

“Anna! I don’t want to marry now. Please don’t trouble me. Leave me alone.”

“Thambi! Is marriage a pit of fire? Why do you tremble like this? Look at me and your anni—aren’t we living happily?”

“I’m not saying there should be no marriage, Anna! I’m saying I don’t need it now. Whatever property we may have, unless I have the respectability of earning a hundred rupees a month, who will willingly give me a daughter?”

“Then don’t ask for a rich girl—a highly educated girl! Why not marry Maragatham, Tailor Ekambaram’s daughter, as your anni says?”

“What a joke! The whole town will say Chandran’s younger brother Sekar could not get a bride from a big family because rich households rejected a groom who had no job. No, Anna! Instead of living merely on the property Father left us, I too will earn at least a few coins a month, as you do. Then I will get a bride from a big family!”

“Maragatham is a girl of gold. Your anni is desperate that she should be the one you marry.

“She believes you will not act in a way that wounds her feelings. After that, it is your choice!”

“Anni! Are you angry with me? Don’t you understand what I’m saying, Anni?”

“Soundari! You speak to Sekar yourself! Don’t take my life with this. You two decide something. I’m leaving.”

“Sekar! Did you see your anna go away hurt? If you marry and cool our hearts, will the whole household drown, thambi?”

“Anni! Of all people, Tailor Ekambaram’s daughter? He is dirt poor! That Hyder-era sewing machine is his only property!”

“But—his sewing is very beautiful!”

“You are the only one who could praise that sewing. Give him a shirt to stitch and he’ll put the pocket on the back and the sleeve at the neck!”

“I’m not talking about *his* sewing, thambi! I mean what he has produced—Maragatham. Do you know how beautifully she stitches choli blouses and everything else? Now she stitches whatever I need. Thambi! Maragatham is a very good girl. She has a fierce desire to marry you. The moment I mention you, she blushes, bends shyly and smiles. Ah! Even you are blushing now! All right...all right...so you too have the desire. You’ve been refusing all these days only because you were embarrassed to tell your anna. Thambi! Sekar! Tell me—shall I tell your brother to take care of all the wedding arrangements?”

“All right, Anni; as you wish.”

Relatives talked among themselves that luck had struck Tailor Ekambaram’s daughter. Those who had wondered, with mouths hanging open, who would ever marry this beauty from a poor household now shut their mouths.

Sekar and Maragatham began rolling the cart of life together. Maragatham’s affection and good nature, and the way she conducted herself with an understanding of family responsibility, continually poured happiness into Sekar’s heart. Only after Maragatham came into the house did he begin to understand that until then something large had been missing from that home and family. He had assumed that his brother and sister-in-law were living in perfect contentment. Only after Maragatham arrived did he learn the grammar of how family life ought to be. He could see the great difference between his own life and his brother’s. Because of it, he felt deep sympathy for his brother.

Chandran would return home tired after finishing his work and sit near the dining table. Soundari would say, “I’m terribly tired! Maragatham! Put some food out and go. He’ll serve himself.”

Chandran’s face did not change when he heard it. Maragatham would not go too close to Chandran. She would carry the food to the table, leave it there, and return. Chandran would serve himself and satisfy his hunger.

After Maragatham came, the maid and the cook had their wages settled and were sent away. Every task fell upon Maragatham’s head. Soundari’s purpose became completely clear to her. Maragatham realized that the whole reason Soundari had stood on her head to arrange her marriage to Sekar was that the house would thereby obtain, without wages, both a good maid and a cook. She did not reveal that anguish to her husband. Afraid that disorder might arise in the family because of her, she suppressed her anger and kept her head bowed. Soundari, meanwhile, behaved in that house like

<!-- anthology scan: 244; printed page: 235 -->

a maharani, making a great show of herself. Seeing Maragatham’s suffering, Chandran one day opened his heart to Soundari:

“Whatever else she may be, isn’t she also a daughter-in-law of this house? Should we make her suffer like this?” he asked Soundari softly, with restrained feeling.

“I tried to get your brother married to a poor girl precisely so that she would do the work for me. Otherwise, with the body I have, if I had to work in this house I would simply die early!” Soundari said, shedding tears.

“Don’t cry! A pregnant woman should not shed tears like this! Maragatham is pregnant too. I only said it because she is doing more than she can bear, working without rest and suffering.”

“Oh, poor thing! Such concern for your younger brother’s wife! Couldn’t you show even half that concern for me?”

With that she lengthened her face, got up, went away and lay down on the bed.

Then Chandran began trying to pacify her; first he fell into the chapter of surrender.

Within a week of each other, Maragatham and Soundari became mothers to beautiful baby girls. Lying beside the child she had borne, Soundari’s eyes became fixed on the wedding photograph hanging on the opposite wall. She took pride in her own beauty there, standing beside Chandran like a fruit-laden tree, her figure still unspoiled. Her appearance like a golden statue—her striking build—her face spilling beauty—suddenly produced some kind of fear in her; it was visible in the distortion that flashed across her face. A storm had begun inside her.

Soundari had become a mother. Even while the child had been inside her womb, the pain it gave her had

<!-- anthology scan: 245; printed page: 236 -->

already made her body thin. Now, in the name of milk, this child would suck away all her blood as well and ruin the shape of her body.

Such a frightening cry rose from one corner of her heart, outdoing the throat of a wolf; it echoed through every cell of her blood and made her tremble.

She stood up and looked at herself in the full-length mirror. She stared without taking her eyes away at the half-clad pictures of Hollywood actresses hanging in the room. She gazed patiently at pictures of the gopis standing in knee-deep water and angrily asking Muralidharan to return the clothes they had lost. Again she looked in the mirror. “If I begin feeding the child, the shape of my beauty will collapse—my youth will be destroyed!” her heart lamented again and sobbed.

Her eyes turned elsewhere. Her gaze passed through the gaps in the window bars and fell upon a calf drinking milk from its mother cow in the distant cowshed. Her eyes also saw the mother cow licking the calf’s back with its tongue. From near the garden wall came a “keech” sound. She looked; a monkey carrying its baby leapt from the top of the wall to a tree. From a corner of the enclosure came an “uch, uch!” sound. She looked; their house dog had surrendered its milk-sac to its five puppies and lay exhausted in sleep. Above, a “meow, meow” split her ears. She looked; surrounded by kittens, the house cat had made its bed on the loft and lay entranced by the music of its children. She also knew that this cat had now come to that house for the third time, produced half a dozen kittens, and was attending to the work of feeding them.

Amid the shouting of a battlefield, as though somewhere the string of a veena had trembled lightly, a soft voice sounded amid the demonic shouting in her heart.

“Dogs, cats, cows and monkeys all feed their young. Do you not possess even the feeling of motherhood that they have?”

That soft note was immediately suppressed, and a crazed voice thundered loudly enough to deafen the ear.

“Dogs, cats, cows and monkeys feed their young. But they have no worry about their beauty being ruined—or their youth declining! Are they going to stroll on the beach with four people? Must they go to the cinema every day? Do they have the burden of going on picnics with friends? Among women of high society who come to parties and entertainments, letting their clothes fall loose and displaying all the splendour of youth like Ajanta paintings and Mamallapuram sculptures, it is the honour of a woman of this age to appear without losing even the little measure of youth she has! Should you abandon that merely to protect a meaningless word called ‘motherhood’ and sit with your head bowed among four people? Your beauty, woman with mango-fruit skin! Do not bury the fullness of your youth in the dust!”

In the end, the demonic voice that argued the case for youth won. The mother’s voice became like a lute with a broken string.

The result was that Maragatham took responsibility for feeding not only her own daughter but Soundari’s child too. She became mother to both children.

“See how good it was that we made the poor girl Maragatham Sekar’s wife!” Soundari said in delight and embraced Chandran.

In the pleasure he found in that embrace, Chandran babbled, “You’re a clever one, my darling!”

The mother’s milk meant for the infant to drink was swallowed by medicine. Soundari, whose youthful glow remained undiminished, looked herself up and down proudly in the mirror and moved among people. She went to the beach with her husband.

Sekar was no longer prepared to endure Maragatham’s suffering. It was true that at first he had not wanted to marry her. She was not unattractive. She was not unfit for family life. But she was poor, and because of that he had hesitated. He was not a man who held the principle that one should never marry a poor woman. His reason had been that, in the environment of his house, only a girl from a big family could live as an equal to the others; if she were poor, she would inevitably become a servant. After Soundari told him that Maragatham desired him, an affection for her had arisen without his realizing it. It ended in marriage, and now the two had become parents to a child.

Just as he had feared, Maragatham was not treated as an ordinary equal member of that house. For fear of damaging the love and respect he held for his elder brother, he had been gritting his teeth and practising patience. One day that patience burst its dam and erupted.

The previous night Chandran’s child had had a slight fever; Sekar’s child had a stomach illness.

The two cried by turns through the night, causing trouble without end. Maragatham never closed her eyes. Rocking both cradles and singing “Aaraaro”

and giving medicine for their illnesses and carrying them about were her work throughout the night.

Without taking the children’s cries into their ears, Chandran and Soundari stayed in their bedroom for a long time, laughing and talking.

Sekar could not bear the cruelty. Should he cry over his wife’s suffering? Or should he cry over those hard-hearted people who, unconcerned about the child they had borne, were enjoying life?

Dawn came. After struggling all night with the children, Maragatham finally dozed off for a little while after daybreak. Because of that, Soundari did not get her ‘bed coffee’. Enraged, Soundari roasted Maragatham in scorching words.

Maragatham said nothing and went about her work. Her only concern was that no quarrel in that family should arise because of her. Unable to stand against Soundari’s abuse, Maragatham stared about in fear like a deer struggling unseen by the fire. Seeing her, Sekar’s heart shook, and he hurled words at his sister-in-law like tongues of flame.

“Maragatham is my wife. She is not your servant. She is not the milk-woman who exists to raise your child!” he growled.

“All right, stop it! As though you went and married some great princess!” Soundari mocked. Chandran came there and applauded her sarcasm. Was he not the permanent slave of the youth Soundari guarded so carefully from harm? Naturally, he stood on her side and spoke of justice.

Words between the brothers grew into a quarrel that reached the point of blows. Both lifted guns and began walking toward the face of death.

A terrifying situation had arisen: whose bullet would fire first, and into whose chest would it enter?

Maragatham threw herself between them and tried to make peace, but failed. She ran to Soundari, who was lying peacefully in the room, and cried, “Come! Somehow let us reconcile them both!” Soundari seized Maragatham by the hair, pulled her, struck her on the cheek, and began screaming: “You small-minded dog! Not even spit is left for us! It is my fault for bathing a dog and placing it in the middle of the house.”

While Soundari was shouting in this sudden burst of fury—words rattling out one after another—she abruptly began to cough. The cough would not stop. When it finally did, she became faint. Soundari fell to the floor.

Terrified, Maragatham screamed, “Ayyo! Come quickly! Ayyo! Come quickly!” The elder and younger brothers, who had been standing with their guns aimed, rushed in. Forgetting the fight, Sekar flew to the doctor’s house.

After examining her for a long time, the doctor said, “This is T.B.—the bone-wasting disease! Look after her carefully!” He wrote out medicine and left!!

Soundari lay awake on the bed, turning from side to side!
