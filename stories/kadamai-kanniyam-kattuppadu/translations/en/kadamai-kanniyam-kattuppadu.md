The section of the prison at Palayamkottai where I was confined was called the ‘quarantine.’ It stood outside the school for young offenders. There were forty rooms there. One of them was allotted to me. The other thirty-nine were filled to overflowing—with solitude and emptiness, not people!

When I woke in the morning, what met my eyes was a tamarind tree—a leafless tamarind tree stripped of its greenery!

At once, that tamarind tree at Kayathar where Kattabomman was hanged would also appear before my eyes; not to my outer eye—to my inner eye!

<!-- source scan 26; printed page 24 -->

The golden rays—the hands—of the red sun would embrace and lift the child Earth, and the hue of that light would slowly spread. Even over the desolate area where I was.

Only afterward would they open the door so that I could come outside. Not the prison gate—the door of the room!

The feeling that prevailed was that I had come from a tiny little cage into a larger cage around it.

By day, the sun scorched; when time refused to pass quickly, the body sweltered from it, and the mind sweltered too.

At nine at night they would lock the room door again—with me inside it, of course!

In the tender-leaf days of youth, delighting in all kinds of amusing riddles was part of my life.

“Sokkan is playing ball in the open wilderness” was one of them. One day that ‘Sokkan’ came to play ball on my side too! Yes; a squirrel arrived at the prison enclosure where I was! Tormented in mind from seeing no human face, the moment I saw the squirrel’s face my heart cooled, my face blossomed, and I found great comfort. I gently picked it up in my hand and laid it down in the cupboard in the room.

But, poor thing, it too was a patient like me! Because a crow or a sparrow had pecked it,

there was a wound on its body; not in its heart, as with me! The reason was that it knew nothing of politics!

I put medicine on its pain. I also gave it fruit to eat. Even so, nothing pleased it more than ‘freedom.’ In that matter, I too did not wish to act like Mr. Bhaktavatsalam. After all, I have a heart! So I released the squirrel and sent it toward the garden.

The squirrel left me and went away. I parted from it. It will not remember me. Neither the medicine I gave for its illness nor the fruit I gave its mouth will come back to its memory. But the three stripes on the back of the squirrel that parted from me alone will keep resounding in my heart as the three words,

Duty

Dignity

Discipline

Wherever I may be, I will keep hearing that resounding call.
