# Iragasiyam!

He was a poet. He delighted in adorning Mother Tamil with gold and gems and glittering diamonds! On a bed of gold, in a cradle of sandal, he would fondle and praise the Tamil child. “My eye! My pearl! My Tamil melody!”—with such words he would caress his beloved of poetry. His fame became a beacon upon a hill.

They called him the king of writing.

The people sang of him as a treasure of thought.

Ripened experience. Rich, weighty words. With these he stood upright in the Tamil world.

**Periodicals...** without his garland of writing, they would not taste sweet!

**Drama...** without his song sounding, it would not shine!

**The world of cinema...** without his songs resounding, it would have no flavour! Thus he grew.

He would encourage the poor! He would turn cowards into heroes!

“Can the thorn of the screw-pine and the tender shoot of the plantain join together?” he would ask, and explain that “Tamil culture and Aryan art will not adhere; they will tear apart.” Many wrote praising him.

“O poet! Lord of art! Treasure of thought! I have seen your poem. You composed a song filled with anguish about the sorrow of widows; on seeing it I sobbed and shed floods of tears. I long to work beside one of such greatness. Will my desire be fulfilled? Send me a reply. Thus, Villavan.”

**A letter like this!**

“I saw your song in the journal, master of writing! One who has captured my heart! I am poor. Will you do me a favour? This humble one has talent in writing poetry. If you will undertake the task of publishing my poetry collections as a book, my wish will be fulfilled. Send a reply at once! I shall wait. Yours, Inmozhiyaan.”

**A mudangal like this would arrive!**

“O lion among poets, who pours a rain of thought as though a sea-sluice had opened! Wipe the tears of this lowly Kannappan! For many days I have had a great longing to write poetry! Fulfil that thought for me!”

**A letter like this!**

“Your skill with words in drama deserves praise. The hero and heroine gained greater esteem from your song than from the roles themselves. Might I receive the great opportunity to sing your song? Might your dramatic stage come within my reach? Even if I merely hold the chamaram in the mother’s scene, that is enough; if I gain the fame that this humble one stood wearing stage make-up in the theatre where your song resounds, that is enough. O virtuous one! O honourable one! Tiger! Lion! Holy one! Grant me a new task with splendour! Greetings, Vanangamudi.”

**A sheet like this!**

“O light of the cinema world! Origin! Justice! This useless fellow, whose fate is to lie in the street, dreams of setting foot in the world of art. They say one needs a catamaran to go to sea. Not from greed for money; to serve in the world of art—

<!-- source scan 110; printed page 101 -->

—and that too to serve by singing your poems—friend, I who suffer hold limitless longing; help me! Thus, Thaaymani.”

**A postal letter like this!**

“My lord who writes about the poor! My story is filled with sorrow. Show me a way!”

“A role in a play”

“A scene in the cinema world”

“An opportunity in the journal you write”

“Money is not what I need! I will serve the field of art”

“My mouth must sing in your song”

“Say yes. Otherwise accept my death”

“I wrote many mudangal—there is no reply; why?”

“‘There is no work,’ you wrote; I cannot bear the pain”

“The dramatic stage!”

“The cinema world!!”

“The newspaper office!!!”

Letters praising him piled up by the thousands. Every letter that sang his fame stood seeking his help. There was no limit to his growth. He grew; he grew; he went on growing!

One day, suddenly, the sound of lamentation rose from his house! Hearing the cries, everyone ran there. The whole town gathered and rushed in. He had died. That poet who had won undying fame had died. And he had died by suicide. That lord who had sung to make cowards into heroes had died. He had killed himself—the man who delighted in fastening anklets on Mother Tamil and decking her in beautiful ornaments.

<!-- source scan 111; printed page 102 -->

The body of that noble man hung from the roof-beam!

The town asked, “By what force did he give up his life?”

The whole world wondered, unable to understand why a man lacking nothing had taken his own life. He himself hung there peacefully. The police came. They examined the body. In the poet’s shirt pocket they found a scrap of paper. On it he had written—

**“The reason for my death is the box lying at my feet”**—thus!

The police opened the box. The public too watched eagerly. The box was opened. Inside were thousands of **recommendation letters**: “Give me a job”—“I can sing. I want a chance in cinema”—and so on!

**Now everyone understood  
why he had shown them his death!**
