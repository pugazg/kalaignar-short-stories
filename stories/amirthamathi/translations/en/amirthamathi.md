# Amirthamathi

“My heart has become a volcano, my friend; I cannot bear this pain! They say a mother has no child—let them say whatever they like and go. But do they at least raise that child properly? Not even that; they torture it and ruin it. How can I bear this burden of sorrow? Tell me, Sundar—you tell me! Day after day I have spoken only pleasant news with you. Do not be angry with me because today I speak of something that will make even you weep, brother! I cannot bear this cruelty! I know that only one kind word from you can lighten the burden in my heart. But in this matter even you will be defeated. I have been hurt that deeply.”

“My dear fellow! Speak without crying—first tell me what happened! What unheard-of thing has come upon you?”

“Nothing unheard-of has come—it is the usual work of deceivers! But this time it has happened in a place where it had never happened before!”

“At your in-laws’ house?”

“I am not talking about a family matter, Sundar! This is a dreadful event that has made me boil and erupt!”

<!-- source scan 232; printed page 223 -->

“Then among some friends?”

“Friends? Brother! You and I reached a sound conclusion about friends many days ago! Disaster has happened—it has happened where it never should have!”

“Say it so I can understand—don’t confuse me too!”

“They have stolen!”

“What? Money?”

“What if money goes? When have you ever seen me worry like this about money?”

“Then what—the child’s pearl necklace?”

“Apparently that thief had no need of it.”

“Then what did he steal? Who is that thief? Tell me!”

“If I tell you the thief’s name, you will not believe me—I did not believe it at first either! Only now is my heart boiling. Even the womb of a mother who has lost her son would not burn like this!”

“All right—you have even begun shedding tears of blood!”

“That is my story, my friend! My life itself has become a pool of tears!”

“Are you going to tell me now or not? I ask you for the last time—tell me the details!”

“They have stolen my writing!”

“What?”

“You know that I drew an imaginative picture based on the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*, don’t you?”

“Yes, you told me! I never got around to reading it—you had even titled that imaginative work ‘Where Is Happiness?’, hadn’t you?”

“That is the one, brother! It has been stolen by kuthars!”

<!-- source scan 233; printed page 224 -->

“Only the title?”

“No! No! I am not talking about the title! The ideas and the dialogues have been stolen by those sinners!”

“You are trembling!”

“Have you ever seen me tremble like this? Even when I saw the corpse of my beloved sister, did I not stand like a tree—did I cry? Did I tremble? Sundar! You know it yourself. Even when the queen of my heart, the lamp of my household, closed her eyelids without opening them and departed for the world of death, I did not wail aloud. I stood in silence—I became like a dead tree, but I did not tremble, brother! Even when my dear child, who used to leap toward me crying ‘Appa! Appa!’, died, I did not pour out tears. I was shaken—but I did not wail. You know that too! Dear brother, now I tremble—I writhe! I do not wish to hide it. Had a sickle fallen upon my neck, the head would have gone one way and the trunk another! But this sickle has hacked into my heart, Sundar! I scream—I cry out so that it may fall upon the ears of this world—I shout! Will you say, ‘No! One must not cry like a coward’? I do not cry because I think my single man’s tears will melt and civilise the stone hearts of those sinners. Nor am I like a child who thinks crying will bring fruit, or a pretender who thinks crying will bring profit. When the skull is split, blood pours out, does it not? Tears pour out in the same way. Whether my tears have power or not—I have no need to research it. I am crying—no—I have been driven into a state in which I must cry!”

“You are badly wounded. Be quiet for a while and regain some peace.”

“I cannot, Sundar! The burden on my heart must lessen. I will keep saying something or other—you will call me mad. Call me that; I do not care!”

“I will join you and cry too—but at least explain the matter so I can understand it!”

“That is what I told you. I fashioned an imaginative kaviyam called ‘Where Is Happiness?’ based on the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*. Wicked men have struck and carried away the prose styles in it to appropriate them!”

“I am asking who those wicked men are—tell me that first!”

“Those thieves are more wicked than the queen called Amirthamathi in the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*!”

“Amirthamathi! *Yasodhara Kaviyam*! I have read none of it, and you come to me with an example!”

“You have not read the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*? Is it not among the five minor epics? It is a Jain work that tells the history of the king of Avanti. Sundar! There is not the slightest difference in character between the Amirthamathi who appears in it and the rogues who stole my writing. In short, there is a great deal of connection between this incident and the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*!”

“All right! At least tell me that in a way I can understand.”

“Yasodharan was the king of Avanti. His crowned queen was Amirthamathi. Yasodharan was a king who understood the queen’s heart and gave her happiness. A handsome man. A gentle man. Lord of the country! The charming Amirthamathi had the fortune of adorning the pleasure pavilion of such a just ruler. Is it an ordinary thing to be the peahen of a king’s bed? There were maids; there were many servants standing ready to beg her favour! Eating food mixed with gold—her body filled with abundance—‘Love overflows, companion—go call the guardian!’ The instant she called, they would run, dancing and singing—

—and bring the king. An ambari on a male elephant? A ride in an ornate chariot? Sandals made of poon-romam? Tiger’s milk? A horse’s horn? Whatever she asked, they would provide—there were countless attendants for that very purpose. Amid all this influence and luxury walked that young beauty Amirthamathi!”

“Ah, what a magnificent description! Tell me! Tell me more!”

(Sundar realised that he had forgotten the sorrowful news his friend had come to tell and had become absorbed in the flavour of the epic. So he urged him to continue.)

“The beautiful Amirthamathi and King Yasodharan one day indulged in pleasures in the inner palace.”

“Oho! It seems the graceful part is only now beginning—tell me, brother, tell me!”

“They finished composing the poem of the bed—and their eyes grew tired.”

“All right, you finished it quickly! Then they woke in the morning—that is all, isn’t it?”

“No, no. While the king slept, the queen opened her eyes. Yes! The flower awoke; the bee was asleep. As she lay awake that night, a sweet song reached her ears.”

“All right.”

“She turned her mind toward the direction from which the song came. She longed to clasp the man who had raised that song!”

“Ayyo—then what?”

“The next day she sent her companion Gunavathi to find out who had sung at midnight. The companion went and found him. The singer was an elephant keeper. Do you know what sort of beauty he possessed? Listen to how the poet describes him through the companion:—

<!-- source scan 236; printed page 227 -->

‘A body criss-crossed with veins; a donkey-like gait; warped fingers and hands; a small face; irascible; like a raging monkey; hunchbacked; with deeply sunken, ruined eyes!’

“Describing his deformities in many more ways, the companion tells the queen that it is improper to desire him. But Amirthamathi flies into insistence: ‘I do not care what he looks like. I must take delight in him.’ Gunavathi tries to stop her: ‘When ripe fruit is available, should one pluck an unripe fruit, Amma? You are the wife of a great king endowed with every beauty—should you wish to fondle and sport with a mere gutter-man who looks like a monkey, Mother?’ But the queen says, ‘What do I care how he looks? He is a fine musician—I feel that happiness lies in his love.’ She forcibly drew that song-learned elephant keeper to herself and delighted in his embrace. Day by day that dog-like love began to grow. One day the king himself stood at a distance and saw their love-play. Do you know what happened then? Do you know what sight the king saw? Amirthamathi, who usually went to meet the elephant keeper, arrived a little late that day. For that, the elephant keeper—that misshapen man named Attabangan—seized Amirthamathi’s beautiful black hair and flung her down, gripped her hands with both of his, and demanded like fire, ‘Why did you come late?’

“She was the mistress of servants who would run a *kadam* or a *yojanai* at a command to carry out her wishes—the wife of the ruler of the realm—yet she found pleasure in being scolded and beaten by an elephant keeper with a decaying body.

“A thousand people waited to worship her flower-like feet—yet she pressed that keeper’s feet to her eyes and begged forgiveness for arriving late. The king who saw this grew furious. The sinner who found happiness in that keeper’s song cunningly mixed poison for the king who had become an obstacle and did away with him—

<!-- source scan 237; printed page 228 -->

—that sinner’s name was Amirthamathi! That, Sundar, is the summary of the *Yasodhara Kaviyam*.”

“Very good—the story of a queen who rejected sandalwood and found happiness in a sewer is very good! The title ‘Where Is Happiness?’ that you gave this kaviyam is most fitting.”

“Do you understand now that the person who stole my writing and that Amirthamathi are one in character? Listen, Sundar! Do not turn elsewhere and change the subject. The Amirthamathi of the kaviyam leaves the king and seeks the elephant keeper; she even commits adultery with that grotesque man. Goodness tastes bitter to her, destruction tastes sweet! It is the same with the person who stole my writing—honey tastes bitter to him! Yes, brother! Apparently theft tastes sweet.”

“Who is it? Tell me!”

“If I tell you, you will not believe me! He was such a good man—someone has turned him into poison. Ayyo, they will not live long! The rogues who swallowed, without labour, the imaginings I made by melting my brain will not live long! Sweat dripping, pain pouring through my ribs, working without closing my eyes, I created that adapted literature—and those who took it without labour and put it into their mouths will not escape a fitting judgment!”

“Shh! Be quiet! Do not agitate yourself.”

“You are not in my position—is that why you say so?”

“Are you and I not one—are we different?”

“Then what is the way out of this? At least tell me that! Is there no destruction for this injustice?”

“Why not? The story you yourself told contains the ending! Amirthamathi went seeking her ‘Where Is Happiness?’ She killed her husband too. But that petty-minded woman became, in the kaviyam, a stain-filled symbol of shame—could that clever woman change it? In the same way, those who stole your writing will become erukku flowers blooming in a garden of disgrace. Do not fear!”

“Sundar, you alone are my consolation!”

“Do not worry! Who are those literary thieves? Tell me!”

“I will tell you later—I cannot speak now.”
