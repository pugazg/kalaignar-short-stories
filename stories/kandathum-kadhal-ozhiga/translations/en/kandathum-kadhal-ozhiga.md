On the opening night of the play “Thirelapathi Vasthirabharanam” at the Devi Gana Sabha, the performance was late. As the time dragged on, the great noises rising from all four sides struck one another and scattered, making even that thatched theatre shed tremble.

The drama proprietor believed there was no need for briskness in rehearsing stage business. He conducted scene rehearsals only while the actors were putting on their costumes. He believed that very carelessness displayed his professional genius.

At the dangerous point when the audience could no longer be managed, the drama bell rang. The first scene was the council hall of the five Pandavas. In their hurry, Arjuna came carrying the mace, while Bhimasena came carrying the bow.

When Dharmar called, “Bhimasena,” and the man holding the bow bowed saying, “Anna,” laughter thundered as though the shed were shaking in an earthquake. The curtain dropped with a snap. After they corrected even their weapons, the curtain rolled up again. Thirelapathi entered with a swaying gait—the shed fell silent.

“She’s...really beautiful!”

“Yes! A face cut perfectly suited to the role.”

“Look, that careless smile just flows!”

“Flows and flows!”

“What, are you mocking?”

“No mockery...Kumar! Even over a stage actress, this much pennural....?”

“Is pennural all a stage actress has? Grace, refinement, emotional expression—everything is her slave. If life is not to lose its flavour, one must receive the love of queens of art like her.”

“Then say it—the love has begun.”

“Yes! When one sees a maiden and the heart is stolen at once—isn’t that love?”

“What, are you raining poetry?”

“It’s a rain of love, deaf fellow!”

“All right, madness...it has become complete.”

“Who is mad? Fool! This is love at first sight!”

“Kumar!”

“Nataraj! Don’t disturb me. I have no time to listen to you. She is about to open her mouth.”

“These writers are all like this,” Nataraj muttered and turned back to the play.

Kumar became both storm and boat.

“This fool Dharman is a wooden Maharaja! A golden idol comes and stands before him, and instead of embracing it he is holding court!” his mind mocked.

“If only I were Dharman—why Dharman, even if I were acting Sahadevan...by now, this lovely peacock!.....” Kumar swallowed the saliva gathering in his mouth.

“Right, tomorrow itself I must join this sabha as an actor!” His head rose as though he had discovered a sublime resolution.

After finishing his lines to Bhimasena, Dharmar turned toward Thirelapathi. Thirelapathi stood bowing. Looking at her with a blossoming face, Dharmar began, **“Beloved! Sita! Come near!”** and—

—did not finish; the shed laughed, “Che! Go inside!”

No one was prepared to forgive the actor playing Dharmar on the ground that he had accidentally repeated a line from the previous day’s **“Ramayanam”** play. Once the shed imposed a restraining order on Dharmar, he began a vow of silence. Thirelapathi stood blinking in alarm. Thinking he might speak Dharmar’s line himself, the actor playing Arjuna began, “Beloved Thirelapathi, come near! My younger brother Arjuna is apparently going on digvijayam....”

“Hey! You go inside too!” the shed ordered angrily.

The drama proprietor Pushparagam Pillai ran onto the stage and began, “Brothers—”

“Five brothers are standing there without moving...go talk to them!” shouted the shed.

All five ran inside. “Hey, the five Pandavas are running! Thirelapathi is standing alone. Catch her! Catch her!” The shed roared with laughter.

Thirelapathi too ran inside. The curtain fell. No one in the shed remained seated. A stone struck the face of Kali Devi painted on the front curtain. Crying “Kuyyo! Muraiyo!” the women ran in all directions.

Stones tore through the thatch and flew inside. The drama proprietor shouted from within over the loudspeaker; it was useless. “Are you all human beings?” the loudspeaker demanded. There was no reply to that either. Only the loudspeaker itself was smashed and fell. The guards and the public came to blows.

“Hey, Bharata war!” cried one mocking thug. By then someone had put a matchstick to work. The play “Lanka Dahanam” began. The fire—

—grew and leapt. The proprietor was nowhere to be seen. The Kali Devi curtain caught fire. The shed collapsed, burning as it fell. The actors scattered toward the fields nearby. Kumar shouted, “Nataraj! Nataraj!” He was tormented by the thought that the cuckoo enshrined in his heart might be burnt and charred. “Where is my Komalavalli?” he cried and ran. Just then Thirelapathi flew out of the dressing room. Kumar followed...fire in the shed! Fire all through Kumar’s heart!—and desire drove him on, imagining that fire would turn into a sweet fruit. Running on, Thirelapathi stumbled on a field ridge and fell. He too fell. His queen of the heart neither spoke nor breathed. Kumar laid her across his lap and stroked her cheeks. He took his towel and fanned her face. He rubbed her chest. A few minutes of blissful silence.

“Darling!”

The word that burst beyond restraint mingled with a deep breath. He gently shook the head of his queen of art. Thirelapathi regained consciousness and opened her eyes. She sat up. In the red glow of the theatre shed burning far away, Kumar remained motionless, admiring the golden body of his beloved gleaming brighter.

Thirelapathi had now regained full steadiness. Kumar’s delight became a flood of the Kollidam. Crying, “My jewel!” he embraced Thirelapathi...

Thirelapathi laughed, put a hand to the head, and took off the ‘dopa’ wig!
