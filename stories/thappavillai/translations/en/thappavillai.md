Sharp spear-like points of broken glass had been embedded facing upward along the brow of that tall wall! Beside them ran wires charged with electricity. Sitting near the covered well, without even wiping the tears glinting in his eyes, the prisoner stared at the majesty of the massive wall, so high that merely looking at it was enough to make one reel. Just as the depth of the well, covered by wooden planks, could not be seen from outside, perhaps he wished that the emotion-laden thoughts seeping through the depths of his heart too would remain unseen.

The fresh brightness on the face of that young man—with his sturdy body, attractive face and prominent moustache—had not been wholly dimmed by prison life. He kept moving his lips as though cursing the heavy wall that stood arrogantly between him and the world.

Whether or not he had read the histories of sky-high forts and ramparts raised to protect the capitals of great kingdoms and mighty emperors being shattered to dust, he did not fail to build a castle in his mind around the thought: why should this fortress too not be brought crashing down—

<!-- source scan 93; printed page 84 -->

He may not even have known the historical fact that in France the Bastille prison was broken open and revolutionary leaders freed in a single day. But he wondered: would no one pierce this wall and rescue him too?

There could certainly be a thirst for freedom in such an expectation, but there could hardly be any real hope in it. For he was no political prisoner! He was not one of the front-line fighters of a freedom movement arrested under man-snatching laws, preventive-detention laws and other repressive laws—laws let loose while the police themselves stirred up disorder merely to advertise their own kithaappu and then claimed they were imprisoning people in advance to prevent it. World history is not without pages recording great revolutions that rose declaring, ‘Whoever thrusts leaders worth their weight in gold into prison and lets darkness graze over the land—let us count them as nothing and reduce the prison fortress to dust!’

His position could not be compared with any of these, for he had been accused of a crime and sentenced. That was why each surge of anguish would rise and then subside like a sorrowful background refrain. Whenever that fire died down, a long sigh came out as though scorching the tip of his nose.

He lay asleep on the front veranda of his house, on a bamboo-strip cot woven with palmyra fibre. When awake, the thoughts that leapt within him took shape as scenes, and in the world of dreams he danced and sang in delight...

His poor parents, resolving to arrange the marriage of their only son, searched for a bride in many places and at last selected a graceful young woman as the bride—

<!-- source scan 94; printed page 85 -->

They chose her. He looked again and again at her fair complexion, her sel-kendai eyes and her lips the colour of a cockerel’s comb, and was transported with delight. The marriage took place.

Arrangements were made for the wedding night. In one corner of the thatched house a palmyra-leaf screen was set up, and that itself became the bridal chamber. Guavas on one plate; hill bananas on another; buffalo milk in a small pot; new korai mats; leather pillows stuffed with coconut fibre.

She came. She stood shrinking with shyness. For a while he simply devoured her—with his eyes! “Thangam,” he said; his tongue faltered. His lips dried. With trembling hands he held hers. She moved. He rose. He embraced her shoulders. They sat down together. She tried to slip from his grasp; he would not let go. In that sweet tussle the earthen lamp tipped over. The room went dark...

He opened his eyes. Everything he had seen was a dream. Policemen stood before him. He rubbed his eyes thoroughly and looked. Yes—the police! He tried to cry, ‘Ayyo!’ His throat closed up.

“Hey! We are arresting you in connection with the murder of the pannaiyar,” the sub-inspector shouted menacingly. There was no answer from him. Handcuffs were put on his wrists. He was dragged away. He had not even a moment to pine over the marriage dream or take pleasure in remembering it. They locked him in the lock-up. A man who had been enjoying the priceless breeze blowing across the open world was made a slave and thrown into a filthy room like a civet-cat cage. The next day came the word ‘remand’, and an order that he must remain in the sub-jail for fifteen days!

Had he really killed the pannaiyar? Night—day he kept crying, “No; no.” His aged parents wept again and again at the cruelty of having their one and only son shut in a cage and separated from them.

“Ayyo! Our boy was pure gold—diamond! A king who never went looking for village quarrels! So many people in the village had reason to be furious with the pannaiyar! The chandalan pannaiyar unjustly seized all of Uththandi Konar’s field bunds—couldn’t Uththandi have become angry? That shameless great man harassed Sivaraman’s younger sister when she went to the stream—doesn’t Sivaraman know how to lift a sickle? The whole village says he joined an opium business and cheated this Durai Pandi—couldn’t Durai Pandi have chopped off that traitor’s head? Leaving all of them aside, must it have been our son, driven from his land, who took revenge? Ayyo, God! Are the poor and ruined the only easy fools in this world? This year we had been longing somehow to tie the marriage-block to his leg in the month of Thai; and now his fate has ended like this! What are we to do?”

Mother and father spent every hour weeping and wasting away. Muthu, held on remand in the sub-jail, had only one hope: that the innocent would not be punished.

During those fifteen days of remand, something happened that was like a drop of honey falling into his grief-filled heart. In the sub-jail enclosure, close to the cage in which he was confined, four or five women were held.

One of them looked almost exactly like the young woman he had seen in his dream. On asking around, he learned that though her name was not Thangam—

<!-- source scan 96; printed page 87 -->

—it was the closely related name ‘Ponnu’, and he was greatly astonished. There was not even the shadow of a stain on the young woman’s face. She might have been twenty or twenty-two. The pride of youth swelled and overflowed through every part of her. ‘Who is she? Why is she wasting away in prison? Could she too be accused of murder?’ Questions bored into him. Quietly he asked the others. The first answer he received gave him enormous satisfaction: she was unmarried! He delighted himself by imagining that right there in the sub-jail, through the prison officer, he might finish the marriage talks and become engaged to her.

She had been implicated in an illicit-liquor case. That was the second piece of information he learned about her. He could not believe it. Even if she had really been arrested in a liquor case, he did not consider it disgraceful. Nor did he think badly of several poor mothers who, like her, were counting prison bars with small children beside them. Instead he pitied them.

There is no way to make a living in the country. Because there is no way, the stomach burns with hunger. Wife and children starve. The head of the family cannot endure that cruelty! Important men obtain ‘permits’ and drink, and society even grants that a special respectability. Poor people who drink illicit liquor to ease their worries, and the utterly poor traders who distil illicit liquor depending on those poor customers, spread everywhere. To household heads it appears an easy means of livelihood—and the only one available. Until the police catch them, they engage in it as a cottage trade. What can those born as their wives, children and daughters do? They too are forced to take part in the trade. The woman and children in an oil merchant’s house know how to work the oil press.

<!-- source scan 97; printed page 88 -->

Likewise, every member of the family of someone who runs a cigar company learns to roll cigars. In the same way, the illicit-liquor trade too is becoming a cottage industry in many regions. Disputes over partnership money and business profit-and-loss accounts may land the chief operators in cages, but they give no one reason to boast that justice, fairness and law are being enforced.

It was in such circumstances that Ponnu became a caged bird. She had refused to yield to the desire of the short man with whom she conducted the cottage-trade partnership. As a result, one night she was handed over to the police along with a pot of liquor.

For Muthu, remanded on the accusation that he had murdered the pannaiyar, looking at her brought some consolation. Within a day or two he understood that she too felt the same. It was true that, locked in separate cages inside the jail and watched by warders, they could not meet, talk and converse freely. But who was there to draw a curtain over their eyes—or build a dam against the smiles shed by their white teeth?

Locked into his cage in the evening, Muthu would sit near the bars in the morning and watch the sky turn pale. Then Ponnu, under the special privilege given to the women, would be released first to go toward the well and would eagerly look at the cage in which Muthu was held. He too would look. The eyes of the two would speak and reach all sorts of decisions.

This could not continue for all fifteen days. The reason was that the case against Ponnu was dismissed and she was suddenly released. On the day of her release she gathered courage and took leave of Muthu, saying, “I’ll go and come back.”

Once she left, he again fell into the suffering of prison life. Until then he had prayed to be freed somehow in order to escape the accusation against him and cool the hearts of his mother and father. Now a new reason for freedom had appeared. It was she! Muthu throbbed with the desire to meet her again somehow.

The remand ended. The trial continued. The case went to the Sessions Court. Judgment was delivered: the court decided that he had murdered the pannaiyar. Muthu escaped the death sentence; he was sentenced to life imprisonment! He was sent to the central prison to undergo rigorous imprisonment. Fourteen years in prison! Yet depending on his conduct and remissions, he might see the outside world in roughly eight years. Ayyo—what would become of his parents by then? Would that beauty Ponnu, whom he had come to love, wait until then? Muthu sat staring with burning eyes at the high wall.

“What is it, Muthu?” his prison friends asked, patting his back as they gathered around him.

“What kind of fool are you, Muthu! Look here...I got a death sentence in Sessions Court...I appealed in the High Court; it was reduced to life imprisonment. In the same way...if you appeal too...your sentence may be reduced—or you might even be released!”

One prisoner began preaching. Not only he; all the prisoners urged Muthu to file an appeal. Muthu thought. Should a part of his youth rot away inside prison? Should his father and mother weep outside? Should he waste away without seeing his beloved? He asked himself.

<!-- source scan 99; printed page 90 -->

But did he not need the means to file an appeal? No one came forward with an answer to Muthu’s question.

One day word came that someone had arrived to see Muthu. He went to the ‘interview’ room. What a startling sight! Ponnu stood there with an old man. The old man spoke. Ponnu stood with tears in her eyes.

“Thambi! I know everything. My daughter Ponnu told me everything! My daughter is ready even to give her life for you. Everyone says that if you appeal you will certainly get released. Once you come out, your marriage will be the very next week!”

“Appeal or whatever else! Doesn’t all that need money?” Muthu lamented.

“Don’t worry about the money, thambi! Somehow in this world I’ve done a little ‘agadu-bagadu’ and earned a few coins. Let money earned by a bad road be used on a good road like this, thambi!”

“If I don’t get released on appeal the way you expect, will Ponnu marry me even after ten years? Is she ready for that?”

Ponnu herself answered the question.

“Tell him I am ready, appa!”

Tears gathered in Muthu’s eyes—but they were tears of joy!

Muthu told the news to his prisoner friends. They too congratulated him that good times had begun. Among them an astrologer-prisoner explained, according to shastra, that the seven-and-a-half-year Saturn that had held Muthu had departed that very day.

**Days passed...**

The appeal was being heard in the High Court. Muthu’s dreams filled him with fresh excitement. One day, sleeping on a platform in the prison, he again saw the happy dream he had seen when the police first arrested him. The scene was the wedding night with the beautiful Ponnu. They spoke happily about how they had met in the sub-jail—how they had spoken with their eyes—how each had made the marriage decision within their own hearts.

“I kept stroking the prison bars thinking they were your fingers,” Muthu said.

“Are my fingers really that rough?” Ponnu asked with a laugh.

Defeated by her teasing, Muthu covered her lips with his own.

It was a dream, after all! He woke. He realized that he was still in prison. He wondered what had happened to the appeal. The prison officer approached; warders came with him.

“Come, Muthu!” the officer called. Muthu went with them. He could read nothing from the officer’s facial expression. Some officials’ faces look like glass without silvering. That was the kind of face the officer had.

The longing to know whether he had won the appeal tossed him about. The officer entered the enclosure where prisoners for the gallows were shut in. A cage was opened. Muthu was pushed inside. The cage was locked. Muthu understood nothing. He stared.

“Ayya! Ayya! What is this?” he cried.

“You filed an appeal, didn’t you? This is the result!”

<!-- source scan 101; printed page 92 -->

“Ayyo, am I not being released?” he asked.

“You are being released! This is the release you got on appeal! Release from the world! The Sessions Court sentence was not enough, so this is the sentence the High Court has given you!”

Having said this, the prison officer shut and locked the door and went away.

The saying, “The innocent will not be punished!”—spoken by some preacher—seemed to take the form of letters before his eyes and mock him.
