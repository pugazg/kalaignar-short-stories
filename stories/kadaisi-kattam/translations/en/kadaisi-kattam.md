# Kadaisi Kattam

“Honourable Judge! The charge against me, standing here in the dock, is that I am a murderer! And I am going to admit it. I stand here ready to accept the punishment for it too. What if I refuse to accept it—where are you going to let me escape? I am not unaware that a hanging rope will be ready to tighten around my neck, and beside the prison a small pit will be ready to cover my body once its breath has stopped. Murderer—and not merely that, a man who murdered his mistress—a man who turned into a corpse a beloved woman who deceived her own husband and gave a place in her heart to a secret lover—the town will curse me in all these ways; kith and kin will revile me. Even if my body, covered by earth, is no longer before their eyes, I know they will spit on the name **Doctor Babu**. I wish to explain to you the reasons for the murder so that at least this petty history of mine may serve as a lesson to others.

“I have no hope that by giving reasons I can win release! For none of the reasons I am about to give has the power to declare me innocent—

<!-- source scan 206; printed page 197 -->

—No! I myself killed my illicit lover **Manjula**.

“I did not kill her in a frenzy. I killed her in a state in which I did not know myself. I am a doctor. I lived in Gopalapuram and practised medicine there. The house I lived in was a large two-storeyed house. My clinic and my family were upstairs. In the house below, Manjula lived with her father. My wife Kokila and Manjula were very close friends! Within a few days of our moving into that house, the two had become inseparable companions. My wife Kokila came from a lineage of devoted wives. When she rose from bed in the morning, she would never leave the room without touching my feet and bowing. Day or night, whatever the hour, she ate only after I had eaten. Because my name was Babu, she would not utter any word beginning with the first syllable ‘baa’. Her love, respect and devotion toward me were beyond measure. Though she was somewhat old-fashioned, the thought that she was a wife who regarded her husband as a god had created immeasurable affection for her in me.

“Manjula, who lived downstairs, was married. Her husband was in Delhi. Some small quarrel had arisen between husband and wife, grown serious, and in anger Manjula had returned to her father’s house. Manjula was very beautiful, a fashionable woman. Her small blue eyes had the power to fascinate anyone. Whenever I returned home after going out, I had to pass the lower house to reach the upper floor. Such occasions caused Manjula and me to meet often. Must one ask a woman, ‘Do you love me?’ in order to know? Can it not be known merely by looking? That movement of Manjula’s eyes shook my heart from its roots. One night I returned home at nine. Manjula was sitting at the entrance of the lower house.

“Kokila and Manjula would usually sit chatting together. That day Kokila was nowhere to be seen. I asked Manjula, ‘Is your father not here?’ ‘No, he has gone out of town,’ she said. ‘Didn’t Kokila come down to sleep either?’ I asked, lingering there. ‘She was talking with me until just now—then said she had a headache and went to sleep,’ Manjula replied shyly. I went upstairs and looked—Kokila was sleeping soundly. I walked quietly back to the veranda of the lower house. Manjula had bolted the street door and, turning around, was startled to see me. She stood with her head lowered. I went near her. She did not move. I touched her. ‘Has Kokila gone to sleep?’ she asked. That question was the sign of ‘consent’! After that, whenever we found an opportunity, Manjula and I began singing our song of love. My wife Kokila came to know of it. She pleaded with me and begged me many times. But in my intoxication with Manjula I paid no attention to Kokila’s lament. Kokila tried to stop me with many words of advice.

“Hostility grew between Kokila and Manjula. The relationship between Manjula and me grew too. I hardly spoke properly to Kokila. At all times there were thoughts of Manjula—dreams of her! Her father stood as an obstacle to our meetings. Overcoming that obstacle gave us a new kind of pleasure. One day Kokila came to me and threatened, ‘I am going to commit suicide.’ I said, ‘Go ahead and do it; I have no objection whatsoever.’ It became Kokila’s work to go to the room of the household deity, complain and cry. She fell at its feet and wept that things might happen as the deity willed.

“One day Manjula’s husband Gopal suddenly arrived from Delhi. Gopal’s visit to Chennai was made in order to pacify Manjula—

<!-- source scan 208; printed page 199 -->

—and take her back. Since Manjula’s anger did not subside, on his father-in-law’s advice Gopal decided to stay there for a month. Manjula and I now met only with great difficulty. I begged her not to go to Delhi. She too had made the same decision as I had. Kokila had believed that if Manjula went to Delhi peace would return to her own life; the news that Manjula was not going fell on her like thunder. Gopal, meanwhile, stayed on in the confidence that he could somehow reconcile with his wife.

“Gopal and I became friends. He did not know that I and his wife were ‘friends’ too! One month—two months—three months—Gopal remained in Chennai. At last I heard that Gopal and Manjula were going to Delhi. I asked Manjula. She nodded ‘yes’ and ran away. I did not know that she had said it merely to tease me. A single fury rose in me! Was Manjula to go and live in Delhi while I remained in Chennai longing for her? I groaned.

“I burned to kill her so that she would be of use to no one. That day I had been outside; I returned home with a ‘revolver’, having decided to murder Manjula and prevent her journey to Delhi. A servant girl stood in the lower house. I asked her, ‘Where is Gopal?’ ‘They say he has gone back to his town,’ she replied and hurried away. I searched the lower house for Manjula. She was not there. Thinking in disappointment that she too had gone, I went upstairs. My wife Kokila was not there either. A letter lay on the table. I opened it and read.

“Dear Babu! I am freeing myself from you. I ask you to forgive me. I wondered whether I too should do wrong because of the wrongs you committed. The world will say this is not proper; wise people will say so too. But let the decision of this one woman become a lesson so that people like you may reform and live rightly. I leave with you the woman who captured your heart. From now on my life will be with Gopal in Delhi. Namaskaram.”

“Who—Kokila? My wife Kokila? Had she gone away with Gopal?” I screamed. I forgot myself. I stood reeling. At that moment Manjula had come before me. I could not see clearly. Manjula looked like Kokila to me. In the blink of an eye I raised the revolver. I fired. Fired—and kept firing! The frenzy subsided. Only then did I realize that I had killed Manjula. I was arrested. Now I stand here in this dock.”

With that, the accused finished his speech. The judge asked, “You admit that you are the killer, do you not?”

“Yes; I am a murderer, I am a murderer!” Doctor Babu cried, striking his face several times. At that moment his moustache fell off! Doctor Babu picked up the moustache and hurriedly pasted it back on. The people watching the court scene burst out laughing. Even the lawyers struggled to suppress their laughter. The judge came down angrily and shouted with authority at the accused: “I sentence you right now. With such a careless nature, unable even to paste your moustache on properly, you have come here to act before thousands of people—

—you are not fit for this drama company at all! I dismiss you from this company at once. Wipe off the ‘make-up’ and get out!” The curtain fell. However much the actor playing Doctor Babu pleaded, he received no pardon from the company proprietor who was playing the judge.
