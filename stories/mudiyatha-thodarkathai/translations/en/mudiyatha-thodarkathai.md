# The Never-Ending Story

1

Prison—night

[Sangu and Sandhanam, two prisoners, are talking]

Sangu: What is it, brother Sandhanam! You ought to be happy—you’re going to be released tomorrow! Your stamped-out rice, ounce of curry, neem-tree breeze and peanut ball—today is the last of all that, isn’t it?

Sandhanam: Yes, Sangu. I feel very sad that I have to part from all of you. For nearly eight years we have lived as friends inside this jail. Sangu! Don’t forget all our friendship, my boy!

Sangu: How could I forget, brother! You tell those stories day after day—could I forget them? You sing old songs and explain their meaning—could I forget that? Whatever food you get, you give me half—could I forget that? Getting the friendship of educated people isn’t even much of a

great thing, brother; getting the friendship of educated, intelligent people is a great fortune. In that way, brother, I am fortunate indeed.

Sandhanam: You have been a good companion. Now, in this wicked world, whom am I going to befriend?

Sangu: You don’t need any man’s friendship, brother! You and your family are enough. Go and live your life happily!

Sandhanam: Family! Who do I have with whom to make a family...?

Sangu: Why are your eyes filling, brother? Whenever family comes up, you always become like this! For all these eight years I’ve been asking what the matter is. Won’t you tell me your story?

No! No!! Don’t cry over this, brother! There’s only today left. Be happy today and go out tomorrow!

Sandhanam: Sangu! Today I am going to tell my story. I am going to unload the burden from my heart. Who do I have as good a friend as you? If I don’t tell my sorrowful story to you, to whom will I tell it?

Sangu: Brother—if it is going to hurt you too much, don’t tell it.

Sandhanam: What pain, my boy! How many times have we seen prisoners being dragged, writhing, to the gallows inside this very prison? My story is only a little more painful than that—that is all. I’ll tell it; listen. Do you know why my face is so hideous? Can you understand why the flesh hangs torn like this? Do you think I was born this way? My mother used to describe me as having a mango-like complexion. All the relatives, they say, would praise me: “He looks like an Aaduthan Raja—a male lion!” Why, even she fell in love with me because she desired my beauty.

Sangu: Oh! So there was a lover too?

Sandhanam: There was once. Not now. Never again.

Sangu: Did she die, brother?

Sandhanam: As far as I am concerned, yes.

Sangu: Why?

Sandhanam: She loved me for my beauty. When I became disfigured, she hated me...

Sangu: What?

Sandhanam: Don’t be surprised, Sangu... women are like that....

Sangu: Not all women are like that, brother!

Sandhanam: Your arithmetic is wrong—and the reason is that you are not married, Sangu!

Sangu: Haven’t I told you that I am married?

Sandhanam: Then no one has poured anything on your face. That is why you know nothing about women.

Sangu: Ayyo—did they pour acid on your face?

Sandhanam: Yes, Sangu, yes! Not because they disliked my being handsome. She had become bewitched by my beauty, they said; to stop that, they tortured me.

Sangu: Tell me your story in a little more detail, brother!...

Sandhanam: I will. Just don’t interrupt for a while! Kumudha studied with me. A maiden from a Kubera-like rich household. I was a farmer’s son. Determined somehow to fulfil my dead father’s wish, my mother educated me. Kumudha and I studied together... All right, why stretch the story? We became lovers. Apart from the fact that a poor man loved a powerful man’s daughter, no one could find any other fault in our love. As usual, our marriage plan was broken apart. My aged

mother ended her life. One night Kumudha ran to my hut and said, “I have become your bonded slave. Come, let us make a family.” I warned her, “The opposition will rise like a mountain, Kumudha!” She said, “Our love is the little chisel that will split the mountain.” How could I refuse? That very night we left. In another town our family began. It was happy—filled with happiness. Why should I now weep remembering that life! A Selvam was born to us too.

Sangu: You have a child, brother?

Sandhanam: I do. Listen to the rest!... With the child we walked joyfully along the road of life. Kumudha’s elder brother had gone to Malaya to earn money. He returned. A rough brute—it was not easy to settle matters with him by fighting. Someone had told him that it was my beauty that had enslaved Kumudha. My Kumudha herself used to say that often. Her brother set out with burning acid, saying he would destroy that very beauty! I was asleep at home. He came. I woke. He flung the acid onto my handsome face. Ayyo—the burning! Even now I am afraid when I remember it.

It burned worse than the stomach-fire of the jealous! They say it is somewhat better not to touch the place where acid has been poured. But in an emergency do such thoughts come to mind? I wiped my face with my hand. That was when all this flesh tore away. Kumudha’s brother ran. I tried to chase him but could not. I fell in the street. Kumudha and the child were not at home then. She had gone to leave the boy at school. Some people from the street immediately took me to the hospital. Kumudha and little Selvam ran there crying. Kumudha screamed when she saw the horror of my face. At first I thought she was screaming at the height of sorrow. Only later did I guess that she was screaming because the handsome face she needed was gone. The next day, while still in the hospital, I heard that Kumudha had gone somewhere. Only little Selvam was cared for by people of the street. A case was brought against Kumudha’s brother. I too received treatment and came out with this disfigured face. The case dragged on for nearly five years, and in the end Kumudha’s brother was acquitted. The judgment said he was not guilty. I could not bear it. I thrashed in intolerable rage. But I thought that if I acted rashly, little Selvam would be left without anyone—

and after thinking it over, I used some people’s recommendation to place Selvam in a school charity boarding hostel in another town. Only then did I breathe a great sigh.

After that sigh came another breath—the breath of revenge against Kumudha’s brother. Kumudha had gone somewhere because she could not bear my disfigured form. The burning acid her brother threw made me realise the great truth that Kumudha had not loved my heart—she had loved only my beauty. Even so, if a thorn pricks us as a warning that the road ahead is bad, can we pull out that thorn and kiss it? Don’t we break it, snap it and fling it far away? In the same way I had no mind to spare the brute who had ruined my face and thereby taught me that the path of love was dangerous. I poured advice into my dear Selvam’s ears: “Commit any mistake you like! But never love a woman!” I told him that a thousand times, took a knife in my hand and set out.... Kumudha’s brother, the Burmese woman and their little girl were sitting near the courtyard. I entered without asking anyone. Apart from them, no one else was in the house. Kumudha’s father had died two years before. I had heard that the Burmese woman had tuberculosis and was suffering badly. Though all these sorrowful events had struck Kumudha’s brother and caused him pain, I wanted to settle the score with him directly—

I was burning to do it. I rushed toward them. The Burmese woman was coughing badly. I drove the knife in my hand into Kumudha’s brother’s chest—yes, straight toward the heart of the sinner who had disfigured me. I stabbed him again and again. He died there. That is the result, Sangu: all these years of imprisonment! He killed me without killing me and walked free. I killed him in one stroke and receive punishment.

Sangu: I can’t bear your story, brother! Let it go. Tomorrow, when you get outside, at least take good care of your son!

Sandhanam: It is for my Selvam that I am still living. But one thing—I will never allow my son to love any woman.

Sangu: Don’t say things like that in anger, brother!

Sandhanam: I am saying it calmly. The boy came to see me even last month, didn’t he? Even then I sent him away only after saying, “Don’t love anyone!” Love, before the demon that desires beauty? No, my boy, no!

Sangu: All right, brother, come. There, the food call has come! Let us eat. Where are the two of us going to eat together tomorrow—you outside, I inside...! Come, brother, come!

[The two leave to eat.]

Night. The moon is pouring down a rain of light. Released from prison, Sandhanam walks along, swelling with pride at the thought of his offspring, happiness rising in him because he is going to see his son. He walks toward the hostel where the boy lives. Seeing the world after so many years brings wave after wave of new thought. His son must have studied well and grown capable. He would have obeyed the lesson his father gave him: “Love is a deadly poison.” What greater fortune is there than a son who never disobeys his father? As Sandhanam walks thinking all this, the sound of a song reaches his ear. He too had sung songs many times in moonlight with Kumudha. A similar song—now a song that stirs his anger—reaches him. It is cooling music; the words are familiar to him. But why does that song now sound like thunder? It is the very song Kumudha used to sing! It is the song his lover had sung with her face resting on Sandhanam’s shoulder!

“Love is a continuing tale! We shall accept even death—
and attain companionship as well—
love is a continuing tale!”

Those graceful, stirring lines touched and shook Sandhanam’s heart. “Yes, only Kumudha could sing like that—no one else could sing—

that,” he said to himself. Fire sprang from his eyes. Uttering “Kumudha” in a terrifying voice, he rushed toward the direction from which the song came. As he expected, there were only two people singing.

Sandhanam strode rapidly toward them. The two were speaking while embracing each other—a young man and a young woman.

Young man: My dear—what a joyous breeze this song brings! Even if I hear it a thousand times I never tire of it. Shall I tell you why? Or is it the sweetness of this beauty’s voice?

Young woman: Neither.... Even meaningless lyrics and a joyless tune become honey in the imagination when I lie in the lap of this beloved!

Young man: Chitra...you who stole my mind... won’t every day heap up joy like this day?

Young woman: Don’t say “like this day.” The next day will say this day has been defeated; the day after will defeat that next day; another new day will kill even that day—what do you say, Selvam, am I right?

Yes—it is Sandhanam’s son Selvam who lies there delighting in words of love with Chitra. Sandhanam has seen it with his own eyes. The son of the man who preached “love is a deadly poison” is lying on a flower-bed stealing the heart of a new young woman. Unable to bear his rage, Sandhanam sprang forward shouting, “Hey—

Selvam!” The young parrot Chitra was terrified. She could not look at that ghastly man’s face; she screamed. Sandhanam caught the girl as she ran and struck her hard. Taking the blow, she fled. “Father! Father!!” Selvam cried. “Come here—I’ll take your life!” Sandhanam growled, seized Selvam’s hand and dragged him away in a frenzy.

Caught in Sandhanam’s grip, Selvam was shaken to the core. He had expected to receive his father’s loving embrace; instead he struggled inside a crushing iron grip. His eyes became pools of water. Crying “Father! Father!!” he was subjected to Sandhanam’s brutal assault. They reached a dark pavilion surrounded by trees. Beside the tall trees whose foliage would not even let moonlight enter stood a low broken wall. Sandhanam flung Selvam on top of that wall. Women of a household carry a kuthuvilakku with love and devotion overflowing. But when they carry a lit lamp to set it down in the hall and their hand slips, spilling oil and burning wick onto them, they suddenly throw it away without caring that it is a fine lamp. Just so, because of the wrong his dear Selvam had committed, Sandhanam forgot all the love he had for him and hurled his son onto the low wall—like a woman of the household throwing away the sacred brass

lamp! Selvam, who had fallen face down at the foot of the wall, rose with a groaning heart. For a moment he too forgot that the man before him was his father. Sparks of anger burst from his eyes. Through gaps among branches, leaves and foliage, the moonlight peeping in with a silver smile showed Selvam’s face to Sandhanam. Sandhanam, his whole body trembling and terrible contortions rising across his face, growled at his son.

“Fine education this is! Did you come here to stage a love drama? You came to gain the fragrance of knowledge, yet yours is a midnight course. You came to seek Viveka Chintamani, yet you embrace Virakthaba Chintamani. If I send you to an educational institution to gain knowledge, you run until your legs ache to Kama’s dance hall! After so much trouble, did you come here to study an epic of love, fool?”

Though he had stood silent while his father spoke, Selvam felt that he had to say something in reply. He also thought he should not. In spite of himself, a few words jumped out. And then? If a small breach appears in a dam, does not a great flood follow?

“Love and study have nothing to do with one another.”

“That is exactly why I ask: why did you behave like an idiot? What do you know about love?”

“I know! Love is the enchanting portrait of a youthful heart. Withered old hearts do not know its greatness!”

An angry laugh came to Sandhanam. When a son tells his own father the truth about love, would the father not laugh? And in that situation, what wonder was there in a laugh mixed with irritation? Sandhanam stood still for a moment. In that half-instant a great struggle must have taken place in his heart. Otherwise there would have been no reason for him to come down a little and speak more gently. What could that struggle have been?

“Poor boy! What does this young man know about love? Youth is the age that will even pick up a snake and play with it. There are stories of great men—men of stature—being destroyed by that intoxication. What has he seen of the treachery called love?” Sandhanam must have spoken so with his own heart. When that inner conversation ended, he began speaking to his son.

“Selvam! I know the trade too, boy—listen. An educational institution is an ocean. You must plunge into it and bring up pearls; you must not go around gathering the seashells of love. “It was in history that I read of the love between Shah Jahan and Mumtaz! It was in English literature that I read that Juliet and Romeo conducted a radiant love!”

“You read that—but perhaps you never read how Caesar, enslaved by Cleopatra’s parrot-like nose, and Antony were ruined! Love, love! A savage fantasy clawed out by a few writer-vultures driven mad. Selvam could not catch Sandhanam’s furious run. He understood only one thing clearly: his father was going somewhere with some catastrophic decision. He was desperate somehow to obtain his father’s forgiveness. If a man had a small extra finger sprouting beside his five fingers, how angry would he become if someone called him, “Hey, six-fingered one”? Even if the caller praised him, “O six-fingered one, beloved

<!-- anthology scan: 76; printed page: 74 -->

man!” he would still be angry. “O five-handed one! O single-tusked one! O rider of the mouse!” When devotees teasingly address Vinayaka so, perhaps the god can sit like stone without worrying about his bodily defects and unusual form. It is very hard for living human beings to bear such mockery. And if a son himself mocks his father’s ugliness, could Sandhanam bear it? Had he not brooded and brooded over the disfigured form that had come upon him until his mind festered? Therefore he could not forgive his cherished son. Not even the thought of turning back to look at the son running after him for forgiveness arose. At last Selvam decided he would never see his father again. Exhausted from running, he stood gasping, his upper and lower breaths competing, unable even to tell which way he had run.

Two great waterfalls poured from his eyes. His mouth muttered, choking and stumbling, his tongue tangling: “Father! Father!” Then Selvam burst into a howl. No one knew how long he cried like that. Afterwards he turned back the way he had come. He felt he had to see Chitra. Where could he search for her in that darkness? Only if he found her could he reduce the weight on his heart a little. Selvam, who had turned back after failing to catch Sandhanam running through the night, had discovered from the first page of the diary that Chitra, who had been his lover, was his own sister. At once it was as though fire had been poured into both their hearts.

Elsewhere, Sandhanam, whose heart had been shattered by Selvam’s words, was rapidly climbing a mountain rock. He climbed with the decision that from the top of that sloping rock he could smash his disfigured body to pieces. That rock rose from the earth as though stretching its peak to touch the edge of the sky. Sandhanam ran upward along it with a despairing kind of joy, thinking of the road to death. A high rock—and beneath it a terrifying chasm. Into the savage embrace of that

Suicide is an unworthy act, the deed of cowards, and yet dying also requires an extraordinary courage. It was with that courage that Sandhanam climbed upward on the dangerous rock standing toward the sky—in order to fall downward and die. He reached the summit. There he saw a figure already standing before him. His speed slowed. The figure stood at the edge of the rock, looking down and sobbing. At last a word burst out loudly: “Joyful death! Here—accept this poor Kumudha!” With those words the figure prepared to leap. Before she could, Sandhanam sprang there. The man who had come to kill himself had been placed in a situation where he must save another life.

Kumudha! Kumudha! Yes—that was the name that had demolished the fortress of his heart! The cyclone that had entered the flower garden of his heart! “Me? I too came here to kill myself, just like you. First tell me your story. Then I will tell you mine. After that the two of us will begin our journey to the world of death!” Sandhanam said. Though he altered his voice as he spoke, he was still standing in such a fire of anger that a question arose within him: should he strangle her throat?

“The introduction itself is a lie. Isn’t your name Kumudham?” Sandhanam asked.

“How do you know my name?”

Kumudham’s whole body trembled as she said it. Wanting to lay down the weight on her heart, wanting the flood from her eyes to dissolve at least some of the great stones of sorrow, she continued.
